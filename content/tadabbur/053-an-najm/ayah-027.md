# The Naming: When Desire Redraws the Map of the Unseen

## Introduction

Let me give you the ayah first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch the meaning.

> إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱلْءَاخِرَةِ لَيُسَمُّونَ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةَ تَسْمِيَةَ ٱلْأُنثَىٰ

*Inna alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bil-ākhirati la-yusammūna al-malāʾikata tasmiyata al-unthā.*

*"Indeed, those who do not believe in the Hereafter name the angels with the naming of the female."*

At first hearing, this sounds like a verse about a strange ancient argument. A correction of one specific pagan belief — that the angels were female, that they were "the daughters of God." You might think: *that's not my problem. I don't believe the angels have a gender. I don't worship al-Lat or al-Uzza. What is here for me?*

Stay with me. Because Allah does not open this verse by saying *"those who claim the angels are female."* He opens it somewhere else entirely. He reaches back, behind the false claim, and puts His finger on the **root** of it. And the root He names is not ignorance. It is not stubbornness. It is this: **لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱلْءَاخِرَةِ** — *they do not believe in the Hereafter.*

### Rebuilding the world

To feel the weight of this, you have to stand in Makkah, sometime in the early years of the revelation.

The Quraysh have a sophisticated theology. They are not crude atheists. Ask any of them, *"Who created the heavens and the earth?"* and the Quran tells us they will answer without hesitation: *"Allah."* (You will find this exact admission in Surah Luqman and Surah az-Zukhruf.) They believe in a Creator. They believe in a supreme God.

But around that supreme God they have built a vast architecture of intermediaries. And among the most cherished of these are three names — **al-Lat, al-Uzza, and Manat** — the goddesses. You heard them named just a few verses before this one, in 53:19-20. And the Quraysh held a particular belief about these and about the angels: that they were **female**, and more than that, that they were **the daughters of Allah**.

Now here is what is so striking, and what the classical commentators — al-Jalalayn, al-Muyassar, al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi — all converge on with one voice: the people making this claim about the angels are described first and foremost as *the ones who do not believe in the Hereafter.* As al-Muyassar puts it, *"they do not affirm the life to come… and they have no sound knowledge that confirms what they said — they follow nothing but conjecture."*

And this is the whole architecture of Surah An-Najm. Look at how the surah began. It began with the Prophet ﷺ, and the declaration: **وَمَا يَنطِقُ عَنِ ٱلْهَوَىٰ** — *"He does not speak from desire. It is nothing but revelation revealed."* (53:3-4). Truth coming **down** from above. A man receiving, faithfully, what is given.

And now, by verse 27, we are shown the exact mirror image. People speaking **from desire**. Truth not received but **manufactured**. Names not given but **invented**.

### The hook

So here is the question I want you to carry into this reflection — and I promise you it is not an ancient question, it is the most modern question there is:

*Why does Allah connect the act of naming the unseen incorrectly to the loss of belief in the Hereafter?*

What does **forgetting the final accounting** have to do with **feeling free to redraw the map of reality**?

Because once you see the thread between those two things, you will never read this verse as someone else's problem again. [PAUSE]

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Cognate Accusative — *They name with a naming*

Let me teach you a feature of Arabic first, in plain terms, before we ever look at the verse. Because once you have it, the verse will open by itself.

In ordinary speech, in any language, we strengthen a verb by adding an adverb. *"He ran."* Weak. *"He ran fast."* Stronger. We borrow a second word to push the first.

Arabic has a more elegant, more forceful tool. Instead of borrowing a foreign adverb, the verb **doubles itself.** It produces a noun from its own body — its own verbal noun, its *masdar* — and places it right after itself as an object. So instead of *"he ran fast,"* Arabic can say, literally, *"he ran a running."* In English that sounds clumsy. In Arabic it is the height of emphasis. The grammarians call it the **مفعول مطلق** — the *maf'ūl muṭlaq*, the absolute or cognate object. It is the language gripping its own verb and squeezing it for certainty. It says: *this happened, truly, fully, without any doubt — I confirm it with its own essence.*

Now look at the verse.

> لَيُسَمُّونَ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةَ **تَسْمِيَةَ** ٱلْأُنثَىٰ

*la-yusammūna* — "they surely name." And then, right behind it, born from the very same root (س-م-و): **تَسْمِيَةَ** — *tasmiyah* — "a naming."

*They name the angels — with the naming of the female.*

The verb has doubled itself. **They name with a naming.** [PAUSE]

Let's sit with what this does. Allah could simply have said, *"they call the angels female."* A flat report of an error. But that is not what He says. He uses the construction that means: *they do this deliberately, fully, with conviction.* This is not a slip of the tongue. This is not a careless figure of speech they stumbled into. The cognate accusative tells you they have **planted** this name. They have committed to it. They have built it into their worldview and they defend it.

And there is something even sharper here. Notice that the whole verse is *already* wrapped in emphasis before we even reach the doubling. It opens with **إِنَّ** — *inna*, "indeed," the particle of certainty. And then on the verb itself sits the emphatic **لَ** — the *lām* of emphasis: *la-yusammūn*, "they *surely* name." So count the layers. *Inna.* The emphatic *lām.* And then the cognate accusative. **Three separate emphasis structures stacked on one short sentence.**

Why would Allah pour this much emphasis onto a *false* claim? You would expect heavy emphasis on a truth He wants to drive home. Why hammer down on their lie?

Here is what this invites us to consider. The emphasis is not endorsing the content — it is **exposing the posture.** Allah is photographing the *confidence* with which they speak about something they cannot possibly know. He is freezing the frame on a person who has never seen an angel, who admits he cannot see the unseen, and who nonetheless pronounces upon it — *not tentatively, not as a guess,* but with the full, tripled, hammered-down certainty of someone reporting the obvious.

That is the indictment. Not merely *"you are wrong,"* but *"look how sure you are about a thing you have no access to."* [PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Root س-م-و — Naming, and the Sky

Now I want to take you into the root itself, because it hides something almost unbearable in its irony.

The verb here — *yusammūn*, "they name" — and its verbal noun *tasmiyah*, "a naming," both come from the root **س-م-و (s-m-w).** This is a Form II verb: *sammā.* In Arabic, Form II — the doubling of the middle letter — characteristically intensifies an action or makes it deliberate and causative; here, *sammā* is the active, intentional assigning of a name to something. Naming as a *deed.*

But hold the root in your hand for a moment. **س-م-و.** This is the same root from which Arabic builds the word **سَمَاء** — *samāʾ*, the **sky, the heaven.** And the word **اسْم** — *ism*, a **name** — which the lexicographers connect to the idea of *elevation*, of *that which is raised up and made known,* the marker by which a thing rises into recognition.

Do you feel it? At the very heart of the word "to name" is the idea of **height.** Of rising. Of the heavens.

A name, in the deepest sense of this root, is meant to be a thing *lifted up* — an act of pointing toward elevation, toward the sky, toward what is above.

And what do these people do with this root of elevation? They take the **angels** — the inhabitants of that very *samāʾ*, the most exalted of created beings, beings of pure light and obedience — and with the language of *raising-up* they **drag them down.** They press a fabricated identity onto them, pulled not from the heavens but from their own household preferences. They use the vocabulary of the sky to insult the dwellers of the sky.

The root that means *to elevate* is here bent into an act of *degradation.* [PAUSE]

This is what I mean when I say the linguistic structure is not decoration. The choice of this root — the *only* root in Arabic that fuses naming and the heavens into one set of letters — turns the verse into a quiet, devastating commentary on what they are doing. They are reaching up to name the sky-dwellers, and what comes out of their mouths is the shape of their own desire.

### Element Three: The Absence — *the female*, singular, and the unspoken knowledge

Now read absence as carefully as presence.

Look at the final word: **ٱلْأُنثَىٰ** — *al-unthā* — "the female." Definite. **Singular.**

Arabic could easily have said *al-ināth*, "the females," plural — they were, after all, assigning femaleness to *many* angels. But the verse says *tasmiyata **al-unthā*** — "the naming of **the female**," in the singular, as a category, as a type. It is as though Allah is holding up the *abstraction* of it: not "they called these particular angels female," but "they applied to the angels *the designation: female* — the whole idea of it, stamped onto beings who are utterly beyond such a category."

And here is the asymmetric absence — the thing the verse *withholds.* The verse tells us, with three layers of emphasis, *that* they named. It does **not** give them a single shred of *grounds* for naming. There is no "because they saw," no "because it was revealed," no "because they knew." That space — the space where evidence should sit — is left completely **empty.**

And Allah does not leave you to wonder why it is empty. The very next ayah, 53:28, fills the silence: **وَمَا لَهُم بِهِۦ مِنْ عِلْمٍ ۖ إِن يَتَّبِعُونَ إِلَّا ٱلظَّنَّ** — *"They have no knowledge of it at all. They follow nothing but conjecture."* Al-Muyassar draws this out explicitly: they have *no sound knowledge that confirms what they said; they follow only ẓann — conjecture — which avails nothing against the truth.*

So the structure across these two verses is a courtroom. Verse 27: *here is the confident claim, hammered three times over.* Verse 28: *here is the evidence — and there is none.* The fullness of their certainty is set directly against the emptiness of their knowledge. **The louder the claim, the more total the silence beneath it.** [PAUSE]

### Bridge

So what has the language shown us?

A people taking the root of *elevation* and using it to *degrade.* Naming the unseen with the full, tripled force of certainty — and standing on absolutely nothing. A confident map of a territory they have never visited.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, not just about seventh-century Makkah — but about reality itself, and about the human heart in every century, including ours.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: We Build Our Cosmos in the Image of Our Desire

Let me start where you actually live.

You have a belief about something you cannot fully see — about how your colleague *really* feels about you, about whether that decision was *truly* fair, about what a person *meant* by what they said. And here is what the research on human cognition has shown again and again, under the name *motivated reasoning*: we do not first gather the evidence and then arrive at the conclusion. Very often we **arrive at the conclusion we want, and then go looking for the evidence.** The desire comes first. The "knowledge" is recruited afterward, to defend it.

You have felt this in yourself. The argument you found *suddenly* convincing — was it convincing because it was strong, or because it gave you what you wanted? The flaw you *somehow* couldn't see in your own plan, but spotted instantly in someone else's? This is not a defect of the unintelligent. It is the default setting of the human mind. The intellect, more often than we admit, does not *lead* the heart. It is the heart's **lawyer.** It builds the case for whatever the heart has already decided it wants to be true.

Now bring that home to the verse. [PAUSE]

The Quraysh did not look at the angels — they could not — and *deduce* femaleness. They had a desire. In their culture, daughters were associated with intimacy, with closeness, with cherished belonging — and also, painfully, with a status they themselves looked down upon (the same surah, a few verses on, will catch them in exactly this contradiction: assigning daughters to Allah while burying their own). They wanted a cosmos populated by familiar, intimate, lesser intermediaries — beings they could relate to, beings who would *intercede* for them, beings shaped like their own social world. And so they reached up into the heavens and **named the angels into the shape of that desire.**

This is the deepest meaning of *tasmiyah* here. To name is to **define.** To define is to *master.* When you put a name on something, you are claiming the authority to say what it *is.* And what these people were doing — what *we* do — is claiming the authority to define **ultimate reality** itself: to say what God is, what the unseen is, what comes after death, *according to what we wish were the case.*

We do this now in subtler dialects. *"I can't believe a loving God would..."* — and we finish the sentence with our own preference, and we have just named the unseen with the naming of our desire. *"My idea of spirituality is..."* — and we describe a reality with no demands in it, no reckoning, conveniently shaped to leave us exactly as we are. We are not, most of us, calling the angels female. But we are doing the deeper thing the verse exposes: **manufacturing a picture of the unseen that serves us, and then mistaking our preference for knowledge.** [PAUSE]

And here is the practical edge. Tomorrow, when you find yourself *certain* about something you cannot actually see — someone's motives, the meaning of an event, the nature of God Himself — stop and run the verse's test on yourself. Ask the question of 53:28: *"What is my عِلْم — my actual knowledge — here? Or am I following ظَنّ — conjecture dressed up as certainty because it gives me what I want?"* That single question, asked honestly, is a quiet act of *tawhid.* It is the refusal to play god by naming the unseen.

### Theme Two: When the Reckoning Goes, the Tongue Goes Free

Now to the thread we left hanging at the very start. *Why* does Allah root all of this in **لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱلْءَاخِرَةِ** — *they do not believe in the Hereafter?*

Let me give you a human picture first.

Imagine two people writing a report. The first knows that next week an auditor will check every figure against the records — line by line, with consequences. The second has been told, quietly, that no one will *ever* check this report. That it will be filed and forgotten. That there is no auditor, no review, no accountability of any kind.

Now — which of the two will be more careful with the truth? You already know. It is not that the second person is evil. It is that **accountability is the gravity that keeps speech honest.** Remove the gravity, and words begin to float free of reality. They become tools of convenience. The second writer is not lying, exactly — he is just no longer *anchored.* Why labor over what's *true* when nothing rides on it? Why not write what is *pleasant,* what is *easy,* what *flatters?*

This is the thread. [PAUSE]

Belief in the Hereafter is the belief that **there is a final audit of reality.** That every word will be weighed. That what *is* will one day be laid bare, and what we *said* will be measured against it. **بَلِ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنُ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِۦ بَصِيرَةٌ** — the human being will be a witness against himself. The Hereafter is the great anchor that ties the human tongue to the truth, because it guarantees that the truth *matters,* that it will *return,* that there is a reckoning in which the gap between *what I said* and *what was real* will finally be closed.

So watch what happens when that belief dissolves. When a person stops believing in the final audit, *reality stops pushing back.* If there is no day on which the truth of things will be established, then what is the cost of naming the angels female? What is the cost of naming God in the image of my desire? **Nothing.** There is no auditor. The territory will never be checked against the map. And so the tongue floats free, and the imagination redecorates the heavens to taste.

This is why Allah does not begin the verse with the *error.* He begins with the **engine** of the error. The false naming is the smoke; the denial of the Hereafter is the fire. *Because* they do not believe their words will ever be weighed, they feel free to weigh nothing — to speak of the most exalted realities with the most careless certainty. The corruption of *belief about the end* produces the corruption of *speech about everything.* [PAUSE]

And now turn it around onto yourself, because this is where it stops being history.

How carefully do *you* speak about the unseen? About Allah, about the next life, about what He loves and hates — when you are tired, when you are in an argument, when a particular answer would win you the point? The degree of *care* in your speech about sacred things is a live readout of how present the Hereafter actually is in your heart. Not in your creed on paper — in your heart, right now. When the reckoning is vivid to you, you become *precise.* You say *"and Allah knows best."* You refuse to claim what you do not know. You feel the weight of every name you place on the unseen. And when the Hereafter grows dim, you will notice your own speech grow *loose* — confident where it has no right to be, casual with the sacred, quick to make God in the image of the moment's need.

So the practical application is almost frighteningly simple. **Guard your tongue about the unseen as a way of strengthening your heart about the Hereafter.** Train yourself to say *"I don't know"* about what you don't know. Let *"Allahu aʿlam"* be a reflex, not a formality. Every time you *refuse* to name the unseen beyond your knowledge, you are quietly testifying that there is an Auditor, that the truth matters, that the Day is real. The discipline of honest speech and the reality of the Hereafter hold each other up. [PAUSE — major pause]

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture revealed

Now step back and see the whole shape of it, because the surah built this with intention.

An-Najm opened with a man and a vision. **وَمَا يَنطِقُ عَنِ ٱلْهَوَىٰ** — *"He does not speak from desire."* The Prophet ﷺ, receiving truth that descends from above, adding nothing of himself, naming nothing of his own. Pure reception. The tongue perfectly anchored to reality because the reality was *given* to it.

And here, at verse 27, stands the exact inverse. People speaking *entirely* from desire. Truth not received but invented. The angels named not by what they are but by what the namers wish. The tongue cut loose from reality because the reality that would anchor it — the Hereafter, the final audit — has been denied.

Two ways of speaking. Two relationships to the unseen. One receives and is silent before what it is given; the other fabricates and is loud about what it cannot know. The surah is named **An-Najm** — *the Star* — and it opens swearing by the star as it *descends.* Truth comes **down.** Everything in this surah turns on the difference between what *descends* to us as revelation and what we *manufacture* and try to send up. [PAUSE]

### Bringing it together

See now that the linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight, approached from two directions.

The **cognate accusative** — *they name with a naming* — showed us certainty without grounds. The theme of **motivated reasoning** named the engine of that certainty: desire wearing the mask of knowledge.

The **root س-م-و** — naming fused with the heavens — showed us people using the vocabulary of elevation to drag down the dwellers of the sky. The theme of **the lost Hereafter** explained *why* they dared: when there is no final reckoning, nothing pushes back against the desire, and the heavens become clay in the hands of the imagination.

And the **absence** — the empty space where evidence should have been, filled by the next verse with *"they have no knowledge, only conjecture"* — that absence *is* the whole teaching. The distance between the loudness of their claim and the emptiness beneath it is the exact measure of what happens to a soul that no longer believes its words will be weighed.

### Questions to carry

1. Where in my life am I most *certain* about something I cannot actually see — and is that certainty built on knowledge, or on what I *want* to be true?
2. When I speak about Allah, about the unseen, about the next life — is my speech *careful,* or has it grown loose and casual?
3. If I am honest: have I ever quietly redesigned my picture of God to leave me more comfortable, more unchallenged, exactly as I am?
4. How vivid is the Hereafter to me *right now* — not in what I believe on paper, but in the precision and humility of how I actually speak?
5. What would change about my tongue this week if I truly lived as though every word were being recorded for a final audit?

### One-sentence distillation

When the heart stops believing in a final reckoning, the tongue feels free to redraw the map of the unseen according to its own desire — naming reality not by what it *is*, but by what we wish it to be.

### The closing invitation

You came into this verse thinking it was about an ancient argument over the gender of angels. I hope you leave it knowing it was about *you* — about the quiet, constant human temptation to play god by naming the unseen, and about the single belief that holds that temptation in check: the certainty that one Day, every word returns.

The invitation is not guilt. It is *precision.* It is the dignity of a tongue that knows its limits — that says *"I know"* only of what it knows, and *"Allah knows best"* of everything else. That is not weakness. In a world of people loudly naming what they cannot see, it is the rarest strength there is.

### Brief duʿā

*O Allah, You who taught Ādam the names of all things, keep my tongue honest before the unseen. Do not let my desires name what only Your knowledge can name. Make the Day of Reckoning so vivid in my heart that every word I speak is anchored to the truth — and let me meet You having said of You only what is worthy of You. Allahu aʿlam, and to You is the return.*
