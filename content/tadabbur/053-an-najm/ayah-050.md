# The First of a Series: Why Allah Calls 'Aad "al-Ūlā"

## Introduction

وَأَنَّهُۥٓ أَهْلَكَ عَادًا ٱلْأُولَىٰ

*Wa-annahu ahlaka 'Ādan il-ūlā*

**"And that it is He who destroyed the first 'Aad."**

Four words in Arabic. One of the shortest ayahs in this surah. And inside it, an entire philosophy of history.

Before we can hear this ayah the way its first listeners heard it, we have to rebuild their world — because for them, the word *'Aad* was not a footnote from an ancient textbook. It was a living presence in their imagination.

Picture Makkah in the early years of revelation. The Quraysh are traders. Their caravans move north toward Syria and south toward Yemen, and along those routes, the desert is littered with ruins. Collapsed structures. Abandoned settlements half-swallowed by sand. The Arabs passed these places, camped near them, told stories about them around fires. And the oldest name in all those stories — the name that stood for vanished greatness itself — was 'Aad.

When an Arab wanted to say something was impossibly ancient, he said it was "from the time of 'Aad." When he wanted to describe overwhelming strength, 'Aad was the measuring stick. The Quran itself records their boast: **"Who is stronger than us in might?"** (41:15). Another surah describes their city as one **"the like of which was not created in the lands"** (89:8). 'Aad were, in the Arab memory, the original superpower. The civilization at the top of every genealogy of greatness.

And Surah An-Najm is early Makkan revelation — delivered into a city that is mocking, resisting, and growing dangerous. The surah has just finished one of the most breathtaking sequences in the Quran: the wa-annahu cascade. *And that it is He* who makes you laugh and makes you weep. *And that it is He* who gives death and gives life. *And that it is He* who enriches and suffices. *And that He* is the Lord of Sirius — the brilliant star that, as classical commentators note, some Arabs had taken as an object of veneration.

And then, without changing rhythm, without raising its voice, the same series continues:

*And that it is He who destroyed the first 'Aad.*

Here is the question I want you to carry into this reflection. Allah did not simply say "He destroyed 'Aad." He said He destroyed 'Aad **al-ūlā** — *the first* 'Aad.

Why "the first"?

First implies a sequence. First implies there was — or will be — a second. When you number something, you are telling your listener: *this is not an isolated event. This is the opening entry in a series.*

And if the destruction of the mightiest civilization in human memory was only *the first* of its kind…

…then who is next?

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: أَهْلَكَ — When "It Perished" Becomes "He Destroyed"

Before we open this word, let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs work — because the entire weight of this ayah hangs on a single letter.

In English, we have pairs of sentences like these: "The door opened" versus "I opened the door." "The company collapsed" versus "He collapsed the company." The first sentence in each pair describes something that *happened* — no agent in view, just an event. The second sentence names the one who *made it happen*.

English does this by rearranging the sentence. Arabic does it by reshaping the verb itself. The base pattern — what grammarians call Form I — often describes the event: something occurs, something undergoes a change. And then Arabic can stretch that same root into Form IV, typically by adding a hamza at the front, and the verb transforms: now it names the one who *brings the event about*.

Hold that, and now look at our root: **هـ-ل-ك**, *hā-lām-kāf*.

The Form I verb, *halaka*, means *to perish*. And listen to how the lexicons describe it — *halāk* is what happens to things on their own. Provisions run out. Garments wear thin. A man dies. A fire burns down to ash. *Halaka* is the verb of natural endings — the quiet entropy built into every created thing. Nobody has to do anything for *halāk* to occur. Things simply… stop.

This is, if you think about it, exactly how the modern mind talks about civilizations. Rome *fell*. The Maya *collapsed*. Empires *decline*. We narrate the death of nations the way we narrate autumn — as a season, a natural process, a curve on a graph bending downward all by itself. Historians debate the *factors* — climate, economics, overextension — but the grammar is always intransitive. Civilizations perish. No subject required.

Now look at what the Quran does.

The verb in our ayah is not *halaka*. It is **أَهْلَكَ** — *ahlaka*. Form IV. The hamza has been added, and with it, the entire sentence acquires a subject. 'Aad did not merely perish. *He* — Allah — *caused them to perish*. Brought them to their end. The fall of 'Aad was not weather. It was not entropy. It was not the impersonal grinding of historical forces.

It was a verdict, carried out.

**Stay with me here**, because the placement makes this even sharper. This ayah belongs to a chain — *wa-annahu… wa-annahu…* — "and that it is He… and that it is He…" — running through the closing movement of the surah. Every link in that chain does the same work: it takes something the listener assumed was simply *part of life* and reattaches it to its true Agent. You thought laughter just happens? *He* makes you laugh. You thought tears just come? *He* makes you weep. You thought wealth accumulates through your cleverness? *He* enriches. And you thought empires just rise and fall, the way tides do?

*He destroyed the first 'Aad.*

[PAUSE]

Now notice something — an absence. Read this ayah again and ask: *where is the wind?*

Elsewhere, the Quran tells us exactly how 'Aad ended: **a furious, screaming wind that Allah imposed upon them for seven nights and eight days** (69:6-7), a wind that tore them up *"as if they were trunks of hollowed palm trees"* (69:7). Al-Baghawi, commenting on our very ayah, identifies them as the people of Hud, destroyed by the *rīḥ ṣarṣar* — the bitterly cold, shrieking wind. The instrument exists. The Quran knows it intimately. Other surahs linger on it for whole passages.

Here: nothing. No wind. No seven nights. No hollowed palm trunks. Just the bare verb and its object. *He destroyed 'Aad.*

Why?

Because in this surah, the instrument is not the point. **The Agent is the point.** When you describe a storm in detail, the listener's imagination fills with the storm — the sound of it, the violence of it — and the storm starts to feel like the cause. An-Najm strips all of that away. It will not let you hide the Agent behind His instrument. There is no weather event standing between 'Aad and their Lord in this sentence. There is only *He* and *them* and a single completed verb.

And it *is* completed — *ahlaka* is in the perfect tense. Done. Sealed. Filed. The mightiest civilization the Arabs could imagine, the people who asked "who is stronger than us in might?" — their entire story, from the height of their power to their erasure from the earth, fits inside one past-tense verb.

Three words, and a superpower is history.

That is what this single word reveals: **the ending of nations is not a process; it is a sentence — in both senses of the word.**

### Element Two: ٱلْأُولَىٰ — The Word That Turns an Event Into a Pattern

Let's open the word the whole ayah turns on.

**ٱلْأُولَىٰ** — *al-ūlā*. From the root *ʾ-w-l*, the root of firstness, of precedence, of what comes before. It is the feminine form of *awwal*, "first." Allah did not destroy "'Aad." He destroyed **'Aad the First**.

The early reciters held onto this word so tightly that they would not even let it stand fully apart from the name it modifies. Alongside the plain reading *'Ādan il-ūlā*, al-Jalalayn preserves a second canonical recitation in which the *tanwīn* of *'Ādan* is swallowed into the *lām* and the hamza drops away entirely — *'Ādal-lūlā*, the two words run together into one unbroken breath. As if the destroyed people and the number stamped upon them could not be pronounced apart.

Here is a general truth about language: you only number things that come in a series. No one says "my first wife" unless there was a second. No historian wrote about "World War One" until there was a World War Two — before 1939, it was simply "the Great War." The moment a number attaches to an event, the event stops being singular. It becomes an *instance*. An entry in a sequence. And the listener's mind immediately does what minds do with sequences: it asks what comes next.

So when Allah calls 'Aad *al-ūlā*, the question is unavoidable. The first — relative to what?

The classical commentators preserved several answers, and I want to build them for you the way one builds a composite sketch — not as competing options where one must be right, but as camera angles on the same reality, each adding a layer.

**Al-Qurtubi** records the first angle: they are called "the first" because they came *before Thamud* — chronological precedence among the destroyed nations of Arabia. Simple, grounded, and it fits the very next ayah, where Thamud follows: *"and Thamud, and He spared none"* (53:51). 'Aad first, Thamud after. A sequence of destructions, in order.

He then reports a second angle from **Ibn Zayd**: 'Aad were called "the first" because they were *the first nation destroyed after the people of Nuh*. Feel how this deepens the word. Now *al-ūlā* is not just "earlier than Thamud" — it is "the opening case." After the Flood, humanity began again. And 'Aad were the first of that new humanity to grow mighty, grow arrogant, and be erased. They are the *precedent*. The first ruling in the case law of nations.

Then al-Qurtubi reports a third angle, from **Ibn Isḥāq**: there were *two 'Aads*. The first 'Aad was destroyed by the screaming wind — and there was a later 'Aad, destroyed in its turn. **Al-Baghawi** fills in this picture: the first 'Aad, the people of Hud, were destroyed by the *ṣarṣar* wind, but they left descendants — and those descendants became *'Aad al-ukhrā*, the later 'Aad.

And here the classical scholars themselves do not all settle the picture the same way — an *ikhtilaf* worth knowing. Reading the very same contrast, **al-Jalalayn** identifies "the later" not as a second 'Aad at all, but as Thamud, the people of Ṣāliḥ — so that *al-ūlā* simply marks 'Aad as the *earlier* of the two great Arabian nations Allah erased. The disagreement is real. But notice that it leaves the load-bearing point completely untouched. Whether "the second" is a later 'Aad or Thamud, the word *al-ūlā* does the identical work either way: it fixes 'Aad as the *first* of a sequence that did not stop with them.

Sit with that third layer for a moment.

[PAUSE]

If this reading is in view, then *al-ūlā* is not only pointing backward in time. It is pointing at a sequel. There was a *second* 'Aad — a people who carried the name, who descended from the survivors, who *knew exactly what had happened to their ancestors* — and who repeated the story anyway. They had the ruins. They had the family memory. The first 'Aad was their own grandfather's grave.

And it didn't save them.

Now layer the angles on top of each other and look at the composite. Earlier than Thamud. The first case after the Flood. The original of which there was a copy. Every angle is saying the same thing from a different side: **'Aad the First is the prototype.** The pattern-setter. The opening entry in a ledger that kept — and keeps — filling.

And here is what this means for the listener. The word *al-ūlā* quietly transforms the entire ayah from history into prophecy. "He destroyed 'Aad" is information about the past. "He destroyed 'Aad *the First*" is a statement about a *pattern* — and patterns, by definition, are not finished. A first implies a series. A series implies the question every Makkan listener — and every listener since — must ask in the privacy of their own chest:

*Which number am I?*

### Element Three: وَأَنَّهُۥٓ — The Oldest Warning in the Oldest Books

One more feature — shorter, but it reframes everything.

Our ayah begins with **وَأَنَّهُۥٓ** — *wa-annahu*, "and that it is He." That little word *anna* is a connector. It does not stand alone; it hangs from something. So we have to ask: grammatically, what is this whole cascade of declarations hanging *from*?

Trace the chain backward through the surah and you arrive at 53:36-37:

**"Or has he not been informed of what is in the scrolls of Musa — and of Ibrahim, who fulfilled?"**

Everything that follows — *that no bearer of burdens bears the burden of another… that man shall have only what he strives for… that it is He who makes laugh and makes weep… that it is He who destroyed the first 'Aad* — all of it pours out of those scrolls. This is the Quran quoting, in substance, the oldest scriptures: the ṣuḥuf of Ibrahim and Musa.

**Here's what's remarkable.** That means the destruction of 'Aad was already being recited as a warning *in the scrolls of Ibrahim* — generations upon generations before Quraysh, before Makkah's towers of pride were even built. The warning about the first destroyed nation is itself among the *first* warnings. The oldest scripture carried the oldest case study. And the surah will seal this with an echo you can actually hear: its closing declaration, just six ayahs later, is —

**"This is a warner from the first warners"** — *nadhīrun min an-nudhur **il-ūlā*** (53:56).

The same word. *Al-ūlā.* The first 'Aad in ayah 50; the first warnings in ayah 56. The surah binds them with one shared word: the *first* destruction and the *first* warning belong to the same ancient pattern — and both have arrived, together, in the streets of Makkah.

And notice, finally, the company this ayah keeps inside the *wa-annahu* chain. The very same grammatical series that says *He destroyed 'Aad* also says *He makes you laugh* (53:43). Your laughter tonight at dinner and the annihilation of the world's proudest empire sit in the same list, governed by the same *annahu*, flowing from the same hand. And the chain reaches just as forcefully in the other direction: a few links earlier it declares *that it is He who gives life* (53:44) — and al-Tabari, opening that verb, explains it as the breathing of the spirit into the lifeless drop, *the dead* nutfah *made living by His placing the spirit within it.* So the one Hand that files away a superpower inside a single perfect-tense verb is the same Hand that coaxes a living human out of a dead drop of fluid. The Agent who ends the largest thing originates the smallest. The intimate and the cataclysmic — one Agent, one series, no seams.

**The bridge:** so the language has shown us three things. A verb that refuses to let history be impersonal. A single adjective that turns one destruction into an open series. And a frame that makes this the oldest warning in the oldest books. Now let's ask what all this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about us.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Ruins Don't Write Memoirs

During the Second World War, the American military studied bombers returning from missions over Europe. They mapped every bullet hole on the returning planes and prepared to add armor exactly where the holes clustered. A statistician named Abraham Wald stopped them. You're studying the wrong planes, he said. These are the ones that *made it back*. The bullet holes you're looking at are the survivable hits. The fatal hits are on the planes that never returned — and those planes aren't here to show you anything.

That insight has a name now: **survivorship bias**. We draw our lessons from what survived, because what survived is all we can see. The failed startups don't give TED talks. The collapsed civilizations don't publish histories. The drowned don't describe the storm. Our entire picture of "how the world works" is assembled almost exclusively from the testimony of survivors — which means it is systematically, structurally blind to the most important data of all: *what destroys*.

Every culture builds its self-understanding this way. We study Rome's rise, Athens's golden age, the empires that left libraries. The nations that were erased completely — erased so thoroughly that, as the next ayah says of Thamud, *He spared none* (53:51) — they leave no professors, no archives, no voice. History is written by the survivors not only because they're biased, but because **the destroyed are silent by definition.**

Now bring this ayah into that silence.

*Wa-annahu ahlaka 'Ādan il-ūlā.*

Who is speaking here? Not a survivor of 'Aad — there were none to tell it. Not an archaeologist reconstructing from shards. The One who did it. **This ayah is the testimony of the only Witness who was present at every collapse in human history — including all the ones no human record remembers.** The Quran repeatedly steps into exactly this gap: *"and how many a generation We destroyed before them — do you perceive of them anyone, or hear from them a whisper?"* (19:98). Allah preserves the memory of the destroyed *precisely because the destroyed cannot speak for themselves* — and because the living desperately need the data the silence is hiding.

Think about what this means practically. Quraysh looked around Makkah and saw nothing but confirmation. The trade was flowing. The idols were crowded with pilgrims. The system *worked* — look how long it had worked! That is survivorship reasoning in its purest form: *we are still here, therefore what we are doing is sound.* The bullet holes they could see were all survivable.

And revelation arrives and says: you are reading the returning planes. Let Me tell you about the ones that never came back. Let Me tell you about 'Aad — *the first one* — the strongest of all, the one whose holes were fatal.

[PAUSE]

There is a personal register to this too, quieter and closer to home. Each of us runs our private life on survivorship logic. *I've cut this corner before and nothing happened. I've delayed tawbah before and the door stayed open. I've neglected the prayer through whole seasons and my world didn't collapse.* Our evidence that we are fine is always drawn from the times we survived — never from the data of those who didn't, because we never hear from them. The friend whose heart finally hardened past feeling does not send a report. The man whose last delay turned out to be his last day files no testimony.

The ayah's mercy is this: it speaks *for* the silent. It hands you the missing half of the dataset before you become part of it.

**The practical application:** the next time your heart offers you the argument *"nothing has happened so far"* — about a sin, a delay, a hardness you've been tolerating — recognize the argument for what it is. It is the logic of the planes that made it back. Then deliberately seek out the other testimony: read the Quran's accounts of the nations, visit a graveyard, sit with the endings. Build the missing data back into your picture of reality before reality builds it in for you.

### Theme Two: "This Time Is Different" — The Lie That Numbers Refute

There is a saying among financial historians, sharpened over centuries of watching bubbles inflate and burst: the four most expensive words in any language are **"this time is different."**

Every speculative mania in recorded history has been attended by experts explaining why the old rules no longer apply. New technology. New economy. New era. The pattern that destroyed everyone before us has been — we are assured — repealed in our case. And every time, the pattern, unimpressed, proceeds. The phrase has become a dark joke among economists precisely because its track record is perfect: whenever a civilization says it, it is wrong.

But here is the thing — the lie is not really an economic lie. It is a spiritual one, and it is far older than markets. It is the default setting of every powerful people at the height of their power. 'Aad said it in their own words: **"Who is stronger than us in might?"** (41:15). Translation: *the pattern applies to weaker peoples; we are the exception.* Quraysh said it in their own way: we are the people of the Ḥaram, the neighbors of the House; what happened to others cannot happen here. And every soul says it in the smallest, most intimate way: *I know how that story ends for other people. My story is different.*

Now feel what the single word *al-ūlā* does to this lie.

By calling 'Aad "the First," Allah does not argue with the exceptionalists. He does something more devastating: **He numbers them.** You think you are unprecedented? Here is precedent. You think you are an exception? Here is the *rule*, with its first instance named and dated in the oldest scrolls on earth. The word "first" is the quietest possible way of saying: *there is a list, the list is open, and your name is not protected from it.*

And remember what al-Baghawi and Ibn Isḥāq preserved for us — the layer that should stop us cold. There was a *second* 'Aad. The descendants of the survivors. A people who did not merely know the story of the first 'Aad the way Quraysh knew it, as ancient lore — they *were* its sequel, carrying its very name. If any nation in history had the standing to say "we've learned the lesson," it was them. The ruins were their inheritance. The warning was their family history.

And they became *'Aad al-ukhrā* — the later 'Aad — and joined the list anyway.

And there is a further turn the commentators surface right here, one that closes off the *other* exit from the lie. We instinctively assume the destroyed nations must have been lesser peoples — cruder, more savage, more primitive than ourselves. The surah says the reverse in its very next breaths: of the people of Nuh it declares they *"were even more unjust and more rebellious"* (53:52), and al-Muyassar reads the whole roll-call the same way — these earliest nations, Nuh's people and 'Aad and Thamud, were *more* defiant and *more* entrenched in their disbelief than the generations that came after them. Allah did not open the ledger with marginal cases. He opened it with the strongest civilization the Arabs could name *and* among the most hardened in rejection. So the exceptionalist is cornered from both sides: you cannot say *we are too advanced for that fate*, and you cannot say *we are not as wicked as they were.* The first names on the list were mightier than you and more rebellious than you — and they are still only the *first* names.

And the surah does not merely assert that the list is open — it lets you watch it unspool. Read on past this ayah and the entries keep arriving: Thamud, of whom *He spared none* (53:51); the people of Nuh before them (53:52); and then the overturned cities, which al-Muyassar describes in unsparing detail — Allah turned them upside down, made their highest their lowest, and clothed them in stone falling from the sky like rain (53:53-54). The word *al-ūlā* opens a ledger, and the very next verses fill four more lines of it before the page is even done. The "first" was never going to be the last; the surah proves it in front of you.

[PAUSE]

This is the most sobering truth in the ayah, so let's sit with it rather than rushing past: **knowledge of the pattern does not, by itself, exempt you from the pattern.** The second 'Aad knew. Quraysh knew the stories of 'Aad and Thamud better than we do — they walked the routes, they recited the poetry. We know more than all of them; we have the entire Quran, sixty-some references to the destroyed nations, archaeology, history. And the nafs performs the same maneuver in every era, with perfect confidence: *yes, the pattern is real — and it does not apply to me.*

What, then, actually breaks the pattern? The Quran's answer is consistent everywhere the nations are mentioned: not strength, not knowledge of history, not lineage from survivors — but *response*. Returning. The peoples who turned were spared; the people of Yunus stand in the Quran as the proof that the series can be exited (10:98). The list of the destroyed has a first entry. It does not have to have your entry.

And here the paradox of the ayah resolves into mercy — because ask yourself: *why is this verse here at all?* Why would Allah inform Quraysh, mid-cascade, that He destroyed the first 'Aad? If the destruction of Makkah were simply decreed, no warning would be sent; you do not send notices to the already-condemned. **A warning is only sent where escape is still possible.** Every threat in the Quran is, structurally, an invitation. The ayah names the first of the series precisely so that the listener can refuse to become the next of it.

**The practical application:** find your own "this time is different." Everyone has one — the area of life where you privately believe the known consequences won't reach you. The health habit, the unrepented sin, the relationship you're neglecting on the assumption it will always be there, the tawbah scheduled for a vaguer, older version of yourself. Name it tonight — actually name it, in words. Then say to yourself what this ayah says to history: *it was not different for the first, and they were stronger than me.* And take one concrete step — tonight, not at the vague later — out of the series.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architectural Reveal

Now step back, because the surah has been building something around this ayah that you can only see at the end.

Surah An-Najm opens with **the star when it falls** — *wan-najmi idhā hawā* (53:1). It closes its great cascade with the *Lord of Sirius* (53:49) — the most brilliant star in the sky, which some Arabs had venerated — followed immediately by the fall of 'Aad, the most brilliant civilization in their memory. The surah's geometry is consistent from its first breath: **everything that rises and shines — stars, empires, peoples — is governed, and everything governed can be brought down.** The star falls in ayah 1; the superpower falls in ayah 50. Only the Lord of the star and the superpower remains.

And then the echo we uncovered: *'Ādan il-ūlā* in ayah 50 — *an-nudhur il-ūlā*, "the first warnings," in ayah 56. The same word, *al-ūlā*, twice in the surah's final page. The first destruction and the first warning, bound together — because they have always traveled together, from the scrolls of Ibrahim and Musa down to this Messenger ﷺ standing in Makkah, *"a warner from the first warners."* The series of destructions has a first entry; the series of warners answering it has run unbroken just as long. Two chains, side by side through all of history. The only question any generation answers is which chain it joins.

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight from two directions. The Form IV verb *ahlaka* told us that nations do not simply perish — they are *brought* to their end by an Agent; and the theme of survivorship bias told us why we cannot see this on our own: the destroyed are silent, so the living mistake their survival for safety. The word *al-ūlā* told us that 'Aad's destruction was the first of a series; and the theme of "this time is different" told us why the series keeps growing: every entry on the list believed itself to be an exception, including the second 'Aad, who carried the first one's name. The grammar and the human condition are describing the same reality. The ayah's language *is* its argument.

### Questions to Carry

1. In which area of my life am I reasoning like a returning plane — taking "nothing has happened so far" as evidence that nothing will?
2. The second 'Aad inherited the ruins of the first and repeated their story. What warning have I *inherited* — from family, from history, from my own past — that I am currently treating as someone else's lesson?
3. When I narrate my own setbacks and the rises and falls around me, do I narrate them as weather — or can I hear the *ahlaka*, the Agent, behind the events?
4. If a "first" implies a series that is still open — what would it take, concretely, for my name to be in the other series: those who heard the warner and turned?
5. The same *wa-annahu* that destroyed 'Aad also makes me laugh and weep. Do I actually live as if my smallest moods and history's largest movements are in the same Hand?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**It takes Allah three words to file away the mightiest civilization humanity ever produced — and He calls them "the First" so that every listener, in every age, will feel the open question of who comes next, while the door to leave the series still stands open.**

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, recite this one short ayah — *wa-annahu ahlaka 'Ādan il-ūlā* — and let yourself feel its size: four words holding an entire fallen world. Then name, honestly, your one "this time is different." Not to despair over it — the warning was sent because escape is possible — but to bring it out of the silence where survivorship logic protects it, and place it in front of the One who makes laugh and makes weep, who gives death and gives life, and who has been sending warners since the first warnings. That is all tadabbur asks of this ayah: not that you fear history, but that you stop assuming you are exempt from it — and step, freely, into the other chain.

### Du'a

O Allah, Lord of Sirius and Lord of 'Aad, You who hold our laughter and our weeping in the same hand that holds the rise and fall of nations — do not let us be deceived by our own survival, and do not let us hear the oldest warning in Your books as someone else's story. Write us not among the series of the heedless who said "this time is different," but among those who heard a warner from the first warners — and turned. Āmīn.
