# The Well-Digger Who Hit Rock

### Surah An-Najm, Ayahs 33–34

> **أَفَرَأَيْتَ الَّذِي تَوَلَّىٰ ۝ وَأَعْطَىٰ قَلِيلًا وَأَكْدَىٰ**

> *Afa-ra'ayta alladhī tawallā · wa aʿṭā qalīlan wa akdā*

> **"Have you, then, seen the one who turned away — and gave a little, and then withheld?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Behind These Words

Before we open these two short lines, I want you to picture something that has nothing to do with religion at all. I want you to picture a man digging a well.

This is the old world. There is no machine. There is a man, a spade, and the hard ground of Arabia. He needs water — for himself, for his animals, for his family. So he begins to dig. The first few feet come easily; the topsoil is soft, it lifts away. He is making progress. He can feel it. And then, somewhere down there in the dark of the hole he is making, the spade comes down and instead of the soft *thud* of earth, there is a sharp *crack* of metal on stone.

He has hit bedrock.

The Arabs had a precise word for this. The hard layer of rock that stops the digger is *kudya*. And the verb for what the digger does when he strikes it — when he gives up, lays down the spade, climbs out of the hole with no water — that verb is **akdā**.

Hold that picture. A man who started digging toward water, who made a little progress, and then hit something hard and stopped. Walked away with a dry well.

[PAUSE]

Now let me place you in the world of this surah. Surah An-Najm has been moving like a storm. It opened by swearing **by the star when it falls** — *wan-najmi idhā hawā* — and it has been climbing through the most extraordinary territory in the whole Qur'an: the night the Prophet ﷺ was taken up, the sight of the greatest signs of his Lord, the truth that **he does not speak from his own desire**. Then the surah turns and, one by one, knocks the idols off their pedestals — al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manāt — exposing them as **nothing but names** the people inherited from their fathers. And then it turns its attention to the people themselves: the ones who **follow nothing but conjecture** about the world to come, who **want nothing but the life of this world**.

And right there, in the middle of this great unmasking, Allah does something startling. He stops talking about idols and crowds and *humanity in general* — and He points His finger at **one man**. One specific case. He says to His Prophet: *afa-ra'ayta* — **"Have you, then, seen** *that one*? The one who turned away?"

The crowd dissolves. The lights come down. A single figure is standing in the spotlight now, and we are being asked to look at him.

Here is what is stunning, and what we are going to spend our time uncovering. This man is not described as an open enemy. He is not described as someone who never believed. He is described as someone who **started**. Who **gave**. Who was *digging the well*. And then — one word, the last word of the second ayah — he hit something, and he stopped.

Who is this man? What did he hit? And why does Allah preserve his portrait forever in His speech — not as a villain with a sword, but as a digger who walked away from his own well?

Let's go and see.

---

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have three small things to open. Each one is a door. Behind the first is the question Allah asks *you*. Behind the second is the tragedy of *what this man could have been*. And behind the third — the last word — is the entire emotional and spiritual weight of these two ayahs, compressed into a single image of a man and a rock.

---

### Element One: *Afa-ra'ayta* — "Have you, then, seen?"

Let me first teach you the shape of this word before we feel its force.

In everyday Arabic, if someone wants you to truly *look* at something — not glance, but **consider**, **take in**, **let it register** — they don't just say "look." They say *a-ra'ayta?* — "Have you seen?" It is the question you ask when you are holding something up for someone and you need them to *really* take it in. It's what a friend says when they grab your arm and turn you toward something: *"Do you see this?"*

The word here is **أَفَرَأَيْتَ** — *afa-ra'ayta*. The root is *r-'-y* (ر-أ-ي), the root of seeing. But notice it is not standing alone. It carries a little hook at the front: the *a-* of a question, and the *fa-* that means **"then,"** "and so," "as a result." This *fa* is a connective. It ties what is about to be shown to **everything that just came before it** in the surah.

So this is not a fresh question dropped from nowhere. It is Allah saying: *Given everything I have just shown you* — the falling star, the truth of the revelation, the emptiness of the idols, the people who chase only this world — *given all of that... have you then seen this one?*

Now, here is what I want you to sit with.

The grammatical addressee is the Prophet ﷺ. "Have **you** seen." Singular. It is spoken to him. But you and I both know that when the Qur'an asks "have you seen," it is not really requesting information. The Prophet ﷺ is not being asked to go gather data. **The question is a hand on the back of your neck, turning your head.** It is a device that reaches *past* its grammatical target and lands on whoever is listening — which means it lands on you, right now.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah does not say "*there is* a man who turned away." He does not give us a report. He makes us *witnesses*. He puts us in the position of *seeing* this man with our own eyes. Why?

Because a report you can file away. A *sight* you cannot un-see. When you have watched something happen, it becomes part of you. Allah is not informing us about a category of person. **He is making us watch one specific human being make a specific choice** — so that we cannot later pretend we did not understand the choice, because we will be tempted to make it ourselves.

Here's what's remarkable: the question has no spoken answer. He says "have you seen the one who...?" and then He simply *describes* him and lets the description hang there. No verdict is stated in these two ayahs. He shows you the man and trusts the showing to do the work. **The silence after the description is where your own heart is supposed to speak.**

So before we have even met the man, the structure has already done something to us. We are not jurors who will be told the verdict. We are eyewitnesses who will have to reach it ourselves.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Tawallā* — "He turned away" (and the root that means the opposite)

Now we meet him. *Alladhī* — **"the one who"** — *tawallā*.

**تَوَلَّىٰ.** "He turned away."

I need to teach you something about this word, because on the surface it just means "turned away," and if we leave it there we will miss the entire tragedy buried inside it.

The root is *w-l-y* (و-ل-ي). And here is the thing you must know about this root: **it is the root of nearness.** It is one of the most beautiful roots in the entire Arabic language. From *w-l-y* comes *walī* — a close friend, a protector, an ally, one who is *near* to you. From *w-l-y* comes *mawlā* — a master and helper bound to you in loyalty. When Allah says **He is the *Walī* of those who believe** (2:257), He is using this root: *Allah is the one who is utterly near to them, who stands beside them, who protects them.* The whole root, at its core, means **closeness — to draw near, to be allied, to stand beside.**

Now feel the form. This verb is in what the grammarians call **Form V** — *tawallā*. Form V in Arabic very often carries a **reflexive** force: the action turns back on the doer himself. *Taʿallama* — he *taught himself*, he learned. *Takabbara* — he made *himself* great, he became arrogant. The doer does something *to his own self*.

So watch what happens. Take a root whose heart is **nearness** — and put it in a form that means **doing something to yourself** — and you get *tawallā*: **he turned his own self away.** He took the very faculty that was *made for drawing near* and he aimed it in reverse.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the ache in that?

This is not the word for an enemy who was always far. This is the word for someone who had the *capacity for nearness* — every human being does — and who **used that capacity to walk in the opposite direction.** The same root that gives us *walī*, the beloved friend of Allah, here gives us the man who turned away. The distance between *being Allah's walī* and *being the one who tawallā* is not a distance of vocabulary. **It is one movement of the heart, in two possible directions.**

The word itself is whispering: *you were built to come close. Look what he did with that.*

And notice — this is not a man who fell down. The word is not "he stumbled" or "he failed." *Tawallā* is **deliberate**. You turn away on purpose. You have to *decide* to present your back to something. Al-Jalalayn is stark in its gloss: it uses the word *irtadda* — he *apostatized* — turning away not from an abstract principle but from the faith he had already begun to inhabit. The tafsir tradition fills this in: this is one who **turned away from obedience**, who **turned his back on faith after facing it**. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both preserve the well-known account that this was about a man — many of the early commentators name al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra — who had **begun to follow the Prophet ﷺ**, had faced toward the light, and then was taunted by his people: *"You've abandoned the religion of your forefathers? You've called them misguided, claimed they're in the Fire?"* And he was afraid. He said, *"I feared the punishment of Allah."*

[PAUSE]

Sit with those words for a moment. *"I feared the punishment of Allah."* Al-Jalalayn preserves this statement verbatim in its gloss — *qāla innī khashītu ʿiqāba-Llāh* — as if ensuring the record stays honest about what this man actually said. He said the *right* thing. He felt the *right* feeling. His fear of divine punishment was not performance — it was real. That fear is exactly what had drawn him toward the truth in the first place. It is what *should* have kept him there.

But here is what a half-committed heart can do with a real feeling: it can treat it as a **credential** rather than a **call.** The man's fear of Allah's punishment was genuine — and it became the very thing he placed on the table when he went looking for a way out. He feared Allah. And that fear made him *negotiable.*

And someone made him an offer.

We'll come to the offer. But hold this: **he had already turned toward the truth.** *Tawallā* is the word for a man turning his back on something he was *already facing*. You cannot turn away from what you never faced.

Among the narrations the commentators preserve, this specific occasion is the most widely reported — though al-Muyassar reads the ayah more broadly, as applying to **anyone** who turns from obedience to Allah and then chokes off his giving. Both readings live in the same verse: a particular man, and a permanent human type. The Qur'an keeps the door open so that *you* might recognize yourself in the doorway.

So we have our man. He had the capacity for nearness. He faced the truth. And then he turned his own self around. **One clear movement.** Remember the digger — he, too, started toward the water.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Akdā* — "and then he hit rock"

Now we arrive at the word this entire reflection has been walking toward. The last word of the thirty-fourth ayah. The word that, once you understand it, makes the whole portrait snap into terrible focus.

But we have to approach it the way the ayah does — through what comes just before it. Listen to the full second line:

**وَأَعْطَىٰ قَلِيلًا وَأَكْدَىٰ** — *wa aʿṭā qalīlan wa akdā* — **"and he gave a little, and then he withheld."**

Three movements: *gave — a little — and then this last word.*

First, *aʿṭā* — **"he gave."** The standard, generous Arabic verb for giving, handing over, bestowing. The word itself is open-handed. So even here, even in the portrait of a man being indicted, there is an act of giving. He was not purely a withholder. **He gave.** The hand opened.

Then — *qalīlan* — **"a little."** And notice what Allah does *not* tell us. He does not tell us *what* the man gave, or *to whom*. The object is left open. The tradition supplies the story — a sum of money promised to the man who offered to **carry his sin for him** — and we'll honor that. But the Qur'an leaves the *what* unspecified, and that unspecified gap lets the verse breathe far beyond one transaction. What it pins down is not the *what* but the *measure*: **a little.** The hand opened — but only a crack. A token. A down-payment.

Al-Tabari is precise about the recipient: *aʿṭā ṣāḥibahu qalīlan min mālihi* — **he gave his companion a little of his wealth.** Not anonymous charity. Not a general act of generosity. A *ṣāḥib* — a specific companion, a specific associate — received a specific, measured amount. There was a deal between two people, and what changed hands was the down-payment on that deal.

And then. The last word. **وَأَكْدَىٰ.**

[PAUSE]

I told you this word at the very beginning, before you knew you would need it. *Akdā.* The root is *k-d-y* (ك-د-ي), and it comes from the world of the **well-digger.** *Kudya* is the **hard layer of rock** that a digger strikes when he is tunneling down toward water. And *akdā* — this very Form — is the verb for **what happens when the digger hits that rock and can go no further.** He *akdā*: he struck bedrock, his digging died, the well stopped, no water came.

This is not a metaphor I am importing from outside. This is the **literal, root meaning of the word Allah chose** to end this man's portrait. The lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, al-Rāghib's Mufradāt — preserve it: *akdā* is the digger who hits stone and **stops, exhausted, dry-handed.** Al-Jalalayn confirms this with forensic precision in its gloss: *akdā*, it says, is taken from *al-kadiyya*, which is "solid ground like rock" (*arḍ ṣulba ka-l-ṣakhra*) — "that stops the one digging the well when he reaches it from going any further." Every classical commentator who opens this word reaches the same physical image. The digger. The hard layer. The silence of the spade meeting something it cannot move. What is remarkable is that these scholars are not reaching for the image for beauty. **They are describing what the word actually carries in its bones.**

[PAUSE]

Now feel what Allah has done. He could have said "he gave a little and *then he stopped giving*." Plain. Clear. Instead He reached for the word that carries an entire **image** — a man with a spade, deep in a hole he himself was digging, who made a little progress and then *struck rock and quit.*

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* — Allah pointing at an unseen reality through a thing your senses already know. The physical image is the digger and the rock. **But the digger is not the subject.** The subject is *the human heart.*

Because what is the rock? What is the *kudya* this man hit?

He started toward something — toward faith, toward nearness, toward water. He gave a little. And then he reached the layer where it would have **cost him.** Where the giving would have to go from a token to a *commitment.* Where staying with the truth meant losing the approval of his people, losing his wealth, losing his comfort. That was his bedrock. And there — at the layer where the cost finally became real — he put down the spade.

[PAUSE]

Notice what the text does *not* say after *akdā*. There is no announcement of punishment. No sentence handed down. No declaration of what became of him. The ayah simply ends — the digger in the hole, the spade against the rock, the water still unreached. The portrait is held open. Open enough for your reflection to enter it. Open enough to make you ask: *have I been here? Have I reached a layer that felt like rock — and quietly climbed back out?*

## PART TWO: THE OFFER

Now let us open what the reflection has been holding in reserve: the deal itself.

Al-Jalalayn's account does not stop at the taunting. Here is what the classical sources tell us happened next. When this man said *"I fear the punishment of Allah"* — he was not making a private confession. He said it to the very person who was pressing him to return to the religion of his forefathers. And that person heard it. And understood immediately what it meant: **this man has a price. His price is the removal of his fear.**

So the offer was made. Al-Jalalayn preserves its terms: *"Return to shirk, and I will guarantee to carry Allah's punishment on your behalf. Whatever you fear — I will bear it for you."* Give up the direction you were heading. Go back to what you were. And in exchange, the thing that drove you toward the truth in the first place — your fear of divine punishment — will be *lifted off you and placed on my account.*

[PAUSE]

Let that transaction sit in the open air for a moment.

Someone offered to absorb divine punishment as a service. And the man across from him — the man who had been drawing near, who had said with his own mouth that he feared Allah's punishment — heard this offer. And he did not refuse it.

Instead, al-Jalalayn tells us, he paid: *wa aʿṭāhu min mālihi kadhā fa-rajaʿa* — "he gave him a certain amount from his wealth, and he returned" — returned to shirk. The money changed hands. The deal was partially enacted. And *akdā* — he withheld the rest of what he owed.

The classical commentators note a subtle difference in what *akdā* refers to at this point. Some read it as him stopping the financial payment before the full amount was delivered — a transaction left incomplete even at its material level. Others, following al-Muyassar's broader reading, take it as him ceasing his generous giving altogether: the deal was the occasion that disclosed a truth already present in his heart, that his hand had always had a stopping point. Both readings converge on the same portrait. He gave a little. And then he hit rock. And stopped.

[PAUSE]

Now watch where the surah goes the very next moment. The ayah that immediately follows — 53:35 — does not wait:

**أَعِنْدَهُ عِلْمُ الْغَيْبِ فَهُوَ يَرَى**

*"Is with him knowledge of the unseen, so that he sees?"*

This is divine rhetoric at its most pointed, and it arrives like a cold wind. The man made a transaction about the Hereafter — about punishment, about what awaits beyond the veil of this world. He accepted a promise regarding something that exists entirely **behind the unseen.** And Allah's immediate question is: *does this man possess knowledge of the unseen? Can he actually see what he is trading in?*

No one can carry another's punishment. No one can peer into the next world and guarantee safe passage. No one holds *ʿilm al-ghayb* — knowledge of the unseen — except Allah. And yet this man exchanged something real — his direction, his beginning, his nearness — for a promise about something no human being has the authority to promise.

A few ayahs later, the surah states its great foundational principle: **أَلَّا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌ وِزْرَ أُخْرَى** — "that no bearer of burdens shall bear the burden of another" (53:38). The man's deal — the entire premise of it, the offer and the acceptance and the down-payment — is dismantled not eventually, not at judgment, but *immediately*, in the same breath. Allah does not wait until the Hereafter to expose the structural impossibility of what was sold to this man. **He exposes it here, in the same surah, in the lines that follow directly.**

What the man purchased was a promise that, according to the word of Allah, no one in the universe has the power to keep.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most devastating critiques in the entire Qur'an, and it is delivered without raised voice or elaborate condemnation. Just: *afa-ra'ayta* — have you, then, seen this man? And then a portrait. And then a question about who actually holds knowledge of the unseen. And then the principle. The structure itself is the verdict. Allah does not have to *pronounce* the man wrong. He simply places the man's deal next to the reality of the unseen, and the deal collapses under the weight of its own premise.

## PART THREE: WHAT ROCK DO YOU HIT?

We have been circling a man from the distant past — a man we know only through what classical scholars preserved, a man who stood in the light and then calculated that the price of staying was too high.

But the Qur'an did not preserve his portrait for *his* sake. It preserved it for ours.

[PAUSE]

So here is the question I want to leave with you. Not a rhetorical question. A real one.

What is your *kudya*?

Every person who has taken faith seriously has, at some point, reached a layer of ground that felt different from the topsoil above it. The first few feet of any spiritual effort come relatively easily. The prayer that feels powerful in the first week. The Qur'an that opens up in the first days of Ramadan. The commitment made after a talk that moved you. The feeling, at the beginning, that *this is real and I am going to keep going.*

And then — sometimes suddenly, sometimes gradually — the spade hits something. And it is not soft.

It might be the cost to your relationships. It might be the loss of a certain kind of standing among people whose approval you need. It might be the financial implication of doing the right thing in a situation where the wrong thing would have been profitable. It might be the internal cost — the moment when faith asks you to *actually change*, not just feel differently, and you realize that changing is harder and more expensive than you thought.

**That layer is your kudya.** And the question the ayah quietly asks — with the *afa-ra'ayta*, the hand on the back of your neck turning your head — is: *when you hit it, what did you do?*

[PAUSE]

The man in this ayah did something very human, and very tragic. He did not leave faith in rage. He did not become an enemy who picked up a sword. He *negotiated.* He found a way to externalize his fear — to hand it off to someone who could absorb it for a fee — and to keep some of the surface appearances while quietly not going any further down. He gave a little. He paid a little toward the arrangement. And then *akdā.* He stopped. And climbed back out of the hole.

There is a version of this most of us have lived in some small way. The prayer kept for a month and then let slip when life got harder. The commitment held to truth until it cost something real, and then held more loosely. The giving begun and then — for reasons that seemed reasonable in the moment — not continued. We gave a little. And then the rock. And then, often, we found our own way to tell ourselves the stopping was wise.

[PAUSE]

But here is the final thing I want you to take from these two ayahs. It is not a warning. It is something stranger and more merciful than a warning.

The well-digger who hit rock and stopped — his well is **still there.** The hole is still in the ground. He did not fill it in. He did not collapse it. He walked away. And a dry well is not a sealed well.

The man these ayahs describe is not described as one who could *never* return to the spade. He is described in a moment — a specific, named moment of stopping. The Qur'an does not say he stopped forever. It describes the act of *akdā*, and then it falls silent. And in that silence — the same silence that hangs after *afa-ra'ayta*, after the question that received no spoken answer — there is space for the reader to find themselves in the portrait. And to decide what the person they are looking at will do next.

**You are not condemned for hitting rock.** Rock is real. The cost of going deeper is real. The layer that stops you is genuinely hard. What the ayah is asking is not "why did you feel the resistance?" It is asking — quietly, with the *fa* connecting back to everything the surah has already shown — *what did you do when you felt it?*

[PAUSE]

---

Surah An-Najm opened with a star falling — *wan-najmi idhā hawā*. There is something in that image: the star that descends, that comes down from the height it had occupied. And the surah has been moving ever since toward a question about why some of us, in the presence of something true and illuminating, descend from the height we had briefly reached. Return to the ground we thought we had left.

*Afa-ra'ayta alladhī tawallā.* Have you, then, seen the one who turned away?

The question is still open. The portrait is still lit. And we are still standing in front of it, being asked whether we recognize the man — whether we see the spade lying on the ground — whether we feel the rock beneath our own feet.

*Wa aʿṭā qalīlan.* He gave a little. He reached toward it. The hand opened.

*Wa akdā.* And then he stopped.

May Allah make us among those who, when we hit the rock, ask Him for what the well-digger never asked for: not a promise from another human being to carry what only Allah governs — but the strength to keep the spade moving through the layer that seems impassable. Because the classical sources tell us something the man in these ayahs never waited to discover: on the other side of *kudya*, if you go through it, is where the water is.
