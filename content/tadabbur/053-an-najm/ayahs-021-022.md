# An-Najm 53:21-22 — The Crooked Division

> [ayah:53:21] أَلَكُمُ ٱلذَّكَرُ وَلَهُ ٱلْأُنثَىٰ
> *A-lakumu adh-dhakaru wa lahu al-unthā*
> **"Is the male for you, and for Him the female?"**
>
> [ayah:53:22] تِلْكَ إِذًا قِسْمَةٌ ضِيزَىٰٓ
> *Tilka idhan qismatun ḍīzā*
> **"That, then, would be a lopsided division."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: A People at the Edge of a Pit

Before we open these words, I want you to stand somewhere uncomfortable.

You are in Arabia, fourteen centuries ago. A man is waiting for news of his wife's labor. Word comes: it is a girl. And the Qur'an itself preserves what happens to his face in that instant — **ظَلَّ وَجْهُهُ مُسْوَدًّا وَهُوَ كَظِيمٌ** — "his face darkens, and he is choked with suppressed rage" (16:58). He withdraws from his people, ashamed. He turns a question over and over in the dark: *do I keep her in humiliation, or bury her in the dust?* (16:59).

This was not a rare horror. The practice had a name — *waʾd*, the burying of infant daughters alive — and the Qur'an names the day it will be answered for: *and when the buried-alive girl is asked, for what sin she was killed* (81:8-9).

Now hold that man's darkened face in your mind. And then look up — because the very same culture that recoiled at a daughter on earth looked up at the heavens and said something astonishing about the sky.

Just two verses before ours, Allah has named their gods: **أَفَرَأَيْتُمُ ٱللَّـٰتَ وَٱلْعُزَّىٰ ۝ وَمَنَوٰةَ ٱلثَّالِثَةَ ٱلْأُخْرَىٰ** — "Have you considered al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt, the third, the other?" (53:19-20). These three great goddesses of the Arabs — and the angels alongside them — they called **banāt Allāh**, *the daughters of God*.

[PAUSE]

Sit with the contradiction. A daughter in your own home is a catastrophe you would bury in the sand. A daughter assigned to the Lord of the worlds is your theology, your pride, the names you carve over your sanctuaries.

That is the world standing behind these two short verses. And into that world, Allah does not send down a lecture. He sends down a **question** — and then a single, strange, crooked-sounding word as a verdict.

Here is what we are chasing today: how does Allah dismantle an entire system of belief not by introducing new information, but by holding up a mirror — by simply forcing a people to look at what they had already decided, and feel its shape in their own mouths?

Stay with me. The word at the end of verse 22 is going to *sound* like the thing it describes. But we have to earn it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Question That Isn't Asking — The Hamza of Rebuke

Let's begin with the very first sound: **أَ** — *a-*. One letter. The interrogative hamza, the *hamzat al-istifhām*, the little prefix that turns a statement into a question: *a-lakum* — "is it for you?"

But before we touch the verse, let me teach you something about questions, because not all questions are looking for answers.

Think of how you actually use questions in your own life. A parent finds a mess and says, *"Is this how I raised you?"* They are not gathering information. They know exactly how they raised you. The question is not a request for data — it is a mirror turned toward the listener. Or a friend who has been wronged: *"After everything I did for you?"* No answer is wanted. The question *is* the rebuke.

Arabic grammarians named this precisely. When the interrogative form is used not to seek knowledge but to confront, they called it **istifhām li-t-tawbīkh** — the questioning of reproach — or **at-taqrīʿ**, the questioning of censure.

Now hear what al-Qurtubī says about our verse — directly, with no hedging:

> "He said it by way of *taqrīʿ* and *tawbīkh* — reproach and censure — *'is the male for you and for Him the female?'* — as a refutation of their statement that the angels are the daughters of God, and the idols are the daughters of God."

[PAUSE]

So notice what Allah does *not* do. He does not say: *"You are wrong. The angels are not daughters. God has no children."* That would be a statement — and a statement can be argued with, deflected, debated.

Instead He hands them a question. And a question of this kind does something a statement cannot: **it makes the listener do the work.** The mind, hearing *"Is the male for you, and for Him the female?"* is forced to internally answer. And the moment they answer honestly — *yes, that is in fact exactly what we claim* — the absurdity detonates inside their own heads. They are not told they are wrong. They are made to *hear themselves*.

This is the genius of the rebuking question. It does not push the truth onto you from the outside. It opens a door and lets you walk through it, only to find yourself standing in the very position you were defending — now, suddenly, unbearable to occupy.

One letter. **أَ**. And the entire architecture of self-exposure is already in motion.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Order of Things — *lakum* Before *lahu*

Now look at how the question is built, word for word:

**أَلَكُمُ ٱلذَّكَرُ** — *a-lakumu adh-dhakaru* — "Is for-you the male?"
**وَلَهُ ٱلْأُنثَىٰ** — *wa lahu al-unthā* — "and for-Him the female?"

I want to teach you one feature of Arabic word order before we go deeper, because everything turns on it.

In Arabic, the normal, expected order of a sentence about possession or assignment would lead with the thing, then say who it belongs to. But here, in both halves, the preposition comes *first*: **lakum** (for-you) before **adh-dhakar** (the male); **lahu** (for-Him) before **al-unthā** (the female).

Grammarians call this fronting — **taqdīm**. And the rule is simple and reliable: *when a word is moved to the front of its expected position, that fronting carries emphasis.* It is the difference, in English, between "I gave the book to you" and "**To you** I gave the book." The fronted element is the one under the spotlight.

So what is under the spotlight here? Not the male. Not the female. The **recipients**. *For you. For Him.* The whole verse is lit up around the question of **who gets what.**

This is a verse about *allocation*. About a division of property. And the structure forces our eyes onto the two parties receiving the shares.

Now — notice something. Notice the *order of the two halves themselves.*

The male comes first, and the male is assigned **to you**. The female comes second, and the female is assigned **to Him**.

Why does that order matter? Listen to al-Muyassar unpack exactly what is happening underneath:

> "Do you assign to yourselves the male — *the one you are pleased with* (alladhī tarḍawnahu) — and assign to Allah, by your false claim, the female — *the one you are not pleased with for yourselves* (allatī lā tarḍawnahā li-anfusikum)?"

[PAUSE]

There it is. The order of the verse is not neutral. It *enacts the priority of the people who made the division.* They reached into the world, took what pleased them — sons — and kept it for themselves, *first*. And then, what was left over, what they could not bear, what darkened their own faces — that they handed upward, to God.

**They served themselves first and gave God the remainder.**

Read the absence here, because this is where it lives. Allah does not say *"you have insulted Me."* He does not even, in this verse, defend His own transcendence. The insult is left completely unstated — and that silence is louder than any protest. The structure simply lays the division on the table, in the order it was actually made, and lets us watch a people take the best for themselves and offer their Creator their own discards.

The fronting puts the recipients in the light. The order shows us the sequence of greed. And not a single accusing adjective has been spoken yet.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: ḍīzā — When the Word Is Shaped Like the Wrong It Names

Now we arrive at verse 22, and at a word so unusual that you will not find it anywhere else in the entire Qur'an.

**تِلْكَ إِذًا قِسْمَةٌ ضِيزَىٰ**
*Tilka idhan qismatun ḍīzā.*

Let me build it piece by piece, because the construction is a trap snapping shut.

First: **تِلْكَ** — *tilka* — "that." A demonstrative, pointing back at the division they just made. *That thing you just did.*

Then the hinge — and do not miss this hinge: **إِذًا** — *idhan*. It means "in that case," "then," "if so." It is a word of *consequence*. It says: *granting what you've claimed — accepting your own premise for the sake of argument — here is what necessarily follows.*

This is the move philosophers call *reductio ad absurdum*: I will not argue with your premise. I will simply **accept it** and walk you to where it inevitably leads — and let you see the wreckage at the end of your own road. *Idhan* is the word that does this. *Fine. Have it your way. Then —*

Then comes the noun: **قِسْمَةٌ** — *qismah* — "a division, an apportionment, a splitting of shares." This is the language of inheritance, of dividing an estate, of fairness in distribution. A *qisma* is precisely the kind of thing that is supposed to be *even*. It carries within it the expectation of balance.

And then — the verdict word. **ضِيزَىٰ** — *ḍīzā*.

[PAUSE]

Let me be honest with you about this word, because honesty is part of reverence. *Ḍīzā* is not in the word-list I can verify root-by-root from the corpus before me. But its meaning is firmly attested across the classical lexicons. In *Lisān al-ʿArab*, under the root *ḍ-y-z* (and a related form *ḍ-w-z*), the verb *ḍāza* means *to wrong someone, to deprive them of their due, to deviate from equity*. *Ḍāzahu ḥaqqahu* — "he cheated him of his right." So *ḍīzā* means: **a division that is unjust, lopsided, deficient, deviating from what is fair.** A split that doesn't split evenly. A share-out that cheats.

Al-Muyassar reaches for an even sharper word in its gloss: *jāʾirah* — tyrannical, oppressive. The *jāʾir* is not merely the one who takes more than his fair portion; he is the one who commits *ẓulm*, active injustice against another party. This distinction matters. A *qisma jāʾirah* is not simply a scale that reads uneven — it is a scale that *wrongs* the one assigned the lesser portion, a division in which someone is actively cheated of what is their due. Applied here, through the hypothetical lens of *idhan*, the indictment becomes total: were the Quraysh's claim true, the Lord of all creation would have been dealt the lesser portion by those He created. The word *ḍīzā* does not just describe a tilted scale. It describes a scale held down by a thumb.

Now put the whole thing back together and feel it land: *"That, then, would be a — lopsided — division."*

But here is what I need you to hear, with your ears and not just your mind. **Say the word.** *Ḍīzā.*

The heavy, emphatic **ḍ** — a letter that sits low and dark in the mouth. The long stretched **ī**. And then the falling, sliding ending — the *alif maqṣūra* that tips the word downhill, *ḍī-zaaa*, trailing off-balance. The word does not sit level. It *tilts*. It ends on a slope.

And it has been placed right beside **qismatun** — *a division* — a word that should be even, balanced, fair. *Qismatun ḍīzā.* The fair-thing, named by the crooked-sounding word.

[PAUSE]

This is the principle the Arabic-speakers among the first listeners would have *felt* before they analyzed it: **the sound is shaped like the meaning.** A crooked division is named by a word that is itself phonetically crooked — heavy, stretched, falling off to one side. You do not need the dictionary to know something is wrong with it. Your tongue tells you before your intellect does.

So watch the whole sentence operate as one closing jaw:

*Tilka* — that thing.
*Idhan* — granting your own logic.
*Qismatun* — is a division —
*Ḍīzā* — that limps.

He took their premise. He did not refute it. He simply followed it to its end and named the result with a word that *sounds like the wrong it describes.* The verdict is delivered, and the verdict has a crooked spine.

### The Bridge

Step back and see what these three pieces have done together.

The **rebuking question** made them answer for themselves. The **fronted order** exposed the sequence of their self-service — best for me, remainder for God. And the **crooked verdict word** named the result in a sound that tilts.

No new information was added. Not one fact they didn't already hold was introduced. Allah took what was already in their hands and turned it over so they could see its underside.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about a tribe's idols fourteen centuries ago, but about reality itself. About what *we* do, every day, in the privacy of our own hearts.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The God of Our Leftovers

Let me start somewhere completely modern, and completely yours.

Think about how a day actually distributes itself. You wake. The best of your mind — sharp, fresh, undefended — goes immediately to the phone, the inbox, the commute, the deadline. You spend the gold of your attention on the world. And then, at the very end — when your eyes are heavy and your focus is spent and your body is already half-asleep — you remember. *I haven't prayed ʿishāʾ.* And you stand up, and you give to the Lord of the worlds the last, exhausted, leftover three minutes of a self that has already been spent everywhere else.

[PAUSE]

I am not saying this to shame you. I am saying it because the Quraysh's error and ours are *the same error*, wearing different clothes.

They reached into existence and kept the best for themselves — the sons they were pleased with — and handed upward the share they didn't want. We reach into our day and keep the best for ourselves — the alert hours, the prime energy, the undivided attention — and hand upward the share we have left over.

The structure of verse 21 showed us the *order of greed*: the male, **first**, for them. Look at the order of your own day. What gets served first? And what is God given — the first-fruits, or the remainder?

This is the deeper architecture of the error — and it runs in every direction, including inward. The Quraysh did not think of themselves as greedy. They thought of themselves as practical. *Of course* you take what you value; *of course* God — vast, eternal, needing nothing — gets what you cannot use. The reasoning has its own internal logic. What it lacks is any consciousness of the transaction itself.

This is what the *rebuking question* does. It forces the transaction into the open. *Is the male for you, and for Him the female?* You cannot answer that question without suddenly *seeing the order*. And the moment you see the order, you feel its weight. Not because you are told it is wrong — but because, hearing it asked back to you, you know.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Names We Give Our Gods Tell Us Who We Are

There is something else buried in this passage that the classical commentators preserve, and it is more unsettling than the surface argument.

Al-Tabari, in his commentary on these verses, observes that the names of these goddesses were not invented from nothing. They were *morphological feminizations of Allah's own names*. Al-Lāt was derived from *Allāh* — the feminine *tāʾ* appended to feminize it, just as the masculine name *ʿAmr* becomes the feminine *ʿAmra*, or *ʿAbbās* becomes *ʿAbbāsa*. Al-ʿUzzā was derived from *al-ʿAzīz* — the Mighty One. The Quraysh did not simply invent goddesses and claim them as God's daughters. They took the real divine names, ran them through a grammatical filter of feminization, and handed the result back as the objects of their devotion.

[PAUSE]

Sit with the full depth of this. The act of distortion was not only theological — it was morphological. They heard *Allāh* and produced *al-Lāt*. They heard *al-ʿAzīz* and produced *al-ʿUzzā*. The goddesses were grammatically derived from the God they were displacing, then installed in their sanctuaries as intercessors. And what the Quraysh wanted from these intercessors was exactly the role they reserved for women in their own social world: not primary actors, but go-betweens, the ones who smooth relations between the powerful. They built their theology in the image of their sociology, and they borrowed the raw material for the building from God's own names.

This is how idolatry tends to work — it rarely constructs from nothing. It takes what is real, inflects it slightly, domesticates it, fits it to the shape of what the people already want, and then worships the result.

[PAUSE]

Ibn Kathir connects the thread directly to what comes immediately after our two verses: *"They are but names which you have named — you and your fathers — for which Allah has sent down no authority. They follow but a guess and that which they themselves desire"* (53:23). The key phrase is *that which they themselves desire* — the pull of the self toward what it already wants to be true. The goddesses were not discovered. They were *wanted* into being.

Al-Muyassar frames what underlies the entire position with a single, precise phrase: they follow *hawā al-nafs al-munḥarifa ʿan al-fiṭra al-salīma* — the desire of a self deviated from the sound natural disposition. The *fiṭra* — the deep original nature woven into the human being at creation — would not, on its own, arrive at this division. To hand God the lesser share while keeping the better for yourself requires a prior departure from the inward sense of equity that the soul is born carrying. The crooked division begins inside before it is ever carved into stone over a sanctuary.

[PAUSE]

And here is where the verse catches us too — because the same process runs in miniature within every self. We do not always remake God at the level of formal theology. Sometimes we remake Him at the level of expectation. We want Him to confirm our judgments, to prefer what we prefer, to be on our side in the specific arguments we are already winning. And so we find real Quranic verses and read them at the angle that gives us back our own reflection — we derive a convenient theology from the real one, the way the Quraysh derived *al-Lāt* from *Allāh*.

The *istifhām al-tawbīkh* breaks exactly this. A rebuking question cannot be read at an angle. It faces you directly.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Three: What God Does Not Say

There is something in the architecture of verse 22 that should make us pause before *ḍīzā* lands.

God is being attributed — by the people He created — the worse share of a division He did not make. He is being assigned the leftovers of a people's own preferences, named in language derived from His own names but feminized and diminished.

And what He sends down in response is: a question. And a crooked-sounding word. Not a defense. Not an assertion of transcendence. Not a list of divine attributes to counterpose against their claim. Simply: *Is the male for you, and for Him the female? That, then, would be —* ḍīzā.

[PAUSE]

This is itself a form of majesty that the Qur'an deploys with great precision. The one who needs to defend himself is someone who can be diminished. The one who responds to an insult with enumerated counterarguments is someone who fears the insult might land. But a question — addressed not to God's dignity but to the listeners' *own internal logic* — requires no defense of the One it concerns. It only requires the listeners to think clearly.

The transcendence of God is not argued here. It is *assumed so completely* that the verse does not need to assert it. It only needs to show us what we look like without it. The question exposes the listeners to themselves, and *ḍīzā* names the result in a sound they feel before they parse.

[PAUSE]

This is a principle worth carrying beyond these verses. When you encounter a moment of complaint — *why is my share smaller, why did this happen to me, why doesn't God's arrangement make sense from where I stand* — this passage offers not an answer but a posture. Tilt the *qisma* and look at it from the side. Does it stand level? Does the word for what you are claiming *sound* like justice — or does it trail off-balance, heavy at the start, sliding at the end, unable to hold itself upright?

## CLOSING: THE WORD THAT SOUNDS LIKE THE THING

Let me leave you with the sound.

*Tilka idhan qismatun ḍīzā.*

If you have been with me this whole journey — through the rebuking question, through the fronted order, through the philosophy of *idhan*, through the architecture of what we assign and what we keep — then you can hear what the first listeners heard.

The word *ḍīzā* does not announce itself as broken. It does not label itself as unjust. It simply *is*. Heavy at the start, stretched in the middle, trailing off uneven at the end — a word that cannot hold itself level, sitting next to *qismah*, a word that is supposed to mean balance.

That is how truth often arrives in the Qur'an. Not as an argument you have to accept. As a *feeling in the mouth* that you cannot unknow once you have felt it.

A people looked at their daughters and felt the weight of shame. They looked up at God and offered what they could not bear to hold. They took His own names, added a letter, feminized the grammar, and called the result divine. And a word was sent down that was shaped exactly like what they had done — crooked, leaning, unable to stand upright — and that word was placed next to the one that means *fairness*, and left there.

*Ḍīzā.*

You already know what that sounds like. You already know what that feels like. The question — that one letter, that **أَ**, the *hamzat al-istifhām* that asks without asking — is still open.

What are you handing upward?

And what are you keeping for yourself?
