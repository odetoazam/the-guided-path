Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You

This module is about gaps. Some of what people are most certain about in this story is not in the Quran, and some of what is in the Quran was left unfinished on purpose. Both facts teach something.

What is not there

There is no serpent in this story. The Quran does have snakes, and Mūsā's staff becomes one more than once, but no snake appears anywhere in the garden in any of the seven tellings. The tempter in the Quran is a voice, not an animal.

The tree is never identified. It is "the tree," every time, with no species and no fruit. Commentators have offered guesses for centuries, and the Quran has declined to settle it in all that time, which is itself the answer about how much it matters.

Ādam's wife is never named, not once in the Quran. The name people use for her comes from outside it.

There is no physical description of Iblīs at all. No form, no appearance, nothing. For a being this present in the book, the Quran gives you nothing to picture.

Put those together and a pattern shows. Every one of the missing details is the kind that would turn this into a story about those characters, back then. Take them out and what is left is a mechanism with no period costume, which is exactly what can still be running now. The silences are what keep it about you.

Told seven times, and never the same twice

The refusal is narrated in seven chapters, and each telling keeps the refusal and varies what surrounds it. One gives you God's diagnostic question. One gives you the four directions he says he will come from. One gives you a detail that appears nowhere else, and the rest of this module is about that one.

Each retelling is aimed at a different question, which is why the scene comes back so often. Two of the eleven verses stand outside the scene. One of those is a single line in a description of the Fire, naming his hosts. The other is the verse we end this module on.

The disagreement that has never been settled

First, though, one of the nine. In Sūrat al-Kahf the scene is told again, and it carries a clause that appears nowhere else in the Quran:

وَإِذْ قُلْنَا لِلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةِ ٱسْجُدُوا۟ لِـَٔادَمَ فَسَجَدُوٓا۟ إِلَّآ إِبْلِيسَ كَانَ مِنَ ٱلْجِنِّ فَفَسَقَ عَنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّهِۦٓ ۗ أَفَتَتَّخِذُونَهُۥ وَذُرِّيَّتَهُۥٓ أَوْلِيَآءَ مِن دُونِى وَهُمْ لَكُمْ عَدُوٌّۢ ۚ بِئْسَ لِلظَّـٰلِمِينَ بَدَلًا

"And when We said to the angels: Prostrate to Ādam — so they prostrated, except Iblīs. He was of the jinn and departed from the command of his Lord. Then will you take him and his descendants as allies other than Me, while they are enemies to you? Wretched it is for the wrongdoers as an exchange."

Al-Kahf (18:50)

He was of the jinn. The command was given to the angels, so was he one of them or not?

This is a real disagreement among the earliest scholars. It is recorded rather than hidden, and it has not been resolved. Al-Ṭabarī opens his treatment of the clause with the words the people of interpretation differed, lays out the positions with their chains, and does not rule between them. Here is what he preserves.

First, the position that he was an angel. Reports go back to Ibn ʿAbbās that Iblīs was among the noblest of the angels, a keeper over the Gardens, with authority over the lowest heaven. On that reading "of the jinn" describes his post, the way you would call a man Makkī or Madanī for where he belongs. Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib is reported as saying he was chief of the angels of the lowest heaven. Reports from Qatāda have it that "the jinn" was the name of a tribe among the angels, and that Iblīs was of it.

This side has an argument and not only an opinion. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported putting it in one line: had he not been of the angels, he would not have been commanded to prostrate. The command in the verse is addressed to angels. He was held responsible for disobeying it. So he was inside the group addressed.

Second, the position that he was of the jinn proper. Ibn Kathīr takes the verse at its plain sense and argues from what he was made of. His original nature betrayed him, because he was created from fire while the angels were created from light. He cites the report in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim from ʿĀʾisha, that the Messenger ﷺ said the angels were created from light, and the Quran itself says elsewhere that the jinn were created from fire. His own stated reason in Ṣād is You created me from fire. On this reading the exception in the verse is exactly what it looks like. He stood among them without being one of them.

Al-Ḥasan is reported to have put it in a phrase, that God returned him to his lineage. At the moment of the test, what he actually was came out.

We are going to leave this open, because it was never settled between them. Individual scholars ruled, the tradition as a whole did not, and inventing a consensus nobody reached would be dishonest. Both readings are held by named people with stated reasons, and you have now heard both.

What is worth noticing is that the answer does not change anything the course has taught. Whatever he was made of, he was addressed, he refused, and his reason was the same. The disagreement is about his species, and the verse is about his response. Those are separable, and the Quran spends its words on the second.

The verse that says he never actually knew

Now the second of the two verses outside the scene.

وَلَقَدْ صَدَّقَ عَلَيْهِمْ إِبْلِيسُ ظَنَّهُۥ فَٱتَّبَعُوهُ إِلَّا فَرِيقًا مِّنَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

"And Iblīs had already confirmed through them his assumption, so they followed him, except for a group of believers."

Sabaʾ (34:20)

The word is ẓann, which means an assumption, a supposition, a guess. Al-Ṭabarī states the implication plainly in his own words: God's enemy said that as a guess on his part that he would do it, not as knowledge, and then it came out true.

That reverses something most people assume.

When he stood there and swore he would take them all, he did not know he could. He had no data, and he had never done it before. What he had was a theory about human beings, that we would prefer the immediate to the true and that most of us would not be grateful. He ran the theory, and it turned out he was right.

So the confidence in his declaration was never certainty. It was a bet. The second half of the verse is what stings: so they followed him. The bet did not pay off because he was powerful. It paid off because people confirmed his guess for him, one at a time, for the rest of history.

He never had knowledge of you. He had a theory about you, and each time you follow it you supply the evidence.

See also what the verse does not say. It does not say everyone. Except for a group of believers sits in the same sentence as the success. That is the third time in this course that a claim of total victory has arrived with its own limit attached.

(A small note for the curious. The early reciters read the verb here in two ways, one with a doubled letter and one without. That gives either "he made his assumption come true about them" or "his assumption about them proved true." Al-Ṭabarī judges both to be well-known readings, close in meaning. The point survives either way.)

What the gaps were protecting

Add up this module. No serpent, no tree species, no name, no face. A species question left open by the scholars who knew most. And a declaration of war that turns out, on inspection, to have been a guess.

Every gap is in the same place. The Quran has withheld the details that would let you file this as ancient history about an impressive being, and it has kept the details that describe a process you are inside of.

One module left, and it is the one where all of this turns and faces you.
