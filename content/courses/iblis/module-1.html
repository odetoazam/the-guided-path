Module 1 — "I Am Better Than Him"

Most of the wrong things people do, they do without a reason they could say out loud. This wrong came with a reason. Iblīs said it in one sentence, to God, and the Quran records that sentence word for word in two different places.

The sentence that occurs exactly twice

In Sūrat al-Aʿrāf, God's question and Iblīs's answer sit in the same verse:

قَالَ مَا مَنَعَكَ أَلَّا تَسْجُدَ إِذْ أَمَرْتُكَ ۖ قَالَ أَنَا۠ خَيْرٌ مِّنْهُ خَلَقْتَنِى مِن نَّارٍ وَخَلَقْتَهُۥ مِن طِينٍ

"He said: What prevented you from prostrating when I commanded you? He said: I am better than him. You created me from fire and created him from clay."

Al-Aʿrāf (7:12)

Many chapters later, in Sūrat Ṣād, the question is put differently, but the same words come back as the answer:

قَالَ أَنَا۠ خَيْرٌ مِّنْهُ ۖ خَلَقْتَنِى مِن نَّارٍ وَخَلَقْتَهُۥ مِن طِينٍ

"He said: I am better than him. You created me from fire and created him from clay."

Ṣād (38:76)

So on two different occasions, in two chapters, he gives exactly the same reply. The phrase I am better than him appears in these two verses and nowhere else in the Quran. He only ever had one argument, and he never needed a second one or found a way to improve it.

Take the argument seriously

Courses on this subject usually call his argument stupid and move on. It is not stupid, and if you pretend it is, you miss the lesson.

Look at what he actually says. He compares fire and clay, and on the measures he picks, fire wins. Fire rises while clay stays put, fire is quick while clay is slow, and fire is impressive while clay is what you wipe off your shoes. If most people were handed those two materials and asked which ranks higher, they would answer the way he did. The argument is put together well, and it rests on something true, because God really did make them from different things.

That is the disturbing part. The first wrong that ever happened in creation was built out of a true fact and aimed against a command.

So where does the argument break? It breaks in two places, and the early commentators go after both of them.

The first break: the axes were chosen to win

Al-Saʿdī, commenting on this verse, does not treat the comparison as reasonable at all. He calls it al-qiyās al-fāsid, a corrupt analogy, and then he runs the same comparison using other measures to show how much the choice of measure was deciding the result:

The element of fire is the material of evil, corruption, loftiness, recklessness and lightness; and the element of clay is the material of steadiness, humility, and the bringing forth of every kind of tree and plant. And it overcomes fire and extinguishes it. Fire needs a material to sustain it; clay stands on its own.

al-Saʿdī, on Ṣād 38:76

Al-Saʿdī does not deny a single fact. He simply asks what happens if you rank the two materials by how steady they are, by what grows out of them, and by which one outlasts the other. On those measures the result turns right around, because clay puts fire out, and fire cannot keep burning unless something feeds it, while clay stays as it is on its own.

So his starting point was never neutral. Choosing "which one rises higher" as the test was already the arrogance, made to look like a fair measurement. Al-Saʿdī then turns his last line past Iblīs to the reader. He calls this the analogy of the master of the crowd and says its falseness is plain, and then he asks what you should expect from the analogies of that master's students, who set their own reasoning against the truth.

The second break: it answers a question nobody asked

The command was never "rank these two materials." Go back to the command we read in the last module: when I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My spirit, fall down to him in prostration. The reason for bowing was never the clay. It was the shaping and the breath, so it was about what God had done, and not about what the raw material was.

He was asked a question about obeying, and he answered a question about materials. Even a correct answer is wrong when it answers a different question from the one that was asked.

His sentence also does something quiet toward God. Read it again: You created me from fire and created him from clay. He is telling the One who chose both materials that He chose badly. The whole sentence is spoken to God, and it is a correction of Him. The refusal only looks as if it is about a human being, when really he is grading God's work and handing back the result.

God names the disease before he answers

Now go back one verse. Before Iblīs says anything, God asks him a question, and the question already contains the diagnosis:

قَالَ يَـٰٓإِبْلِيسُ مَا مَنَعَكَ أَن تَسْجُدَ لِمَا خَلَقْتُ بِيَدَىَّ ۖ أَسْتَكْبَرْتَ أَمْ كُنتَ مِنَ ٱلْعَالِينَ

"He said: O Iblīs, what prevented you from prostrating to what I created with My two hands? Were you arrogant, or were you of the exalted?"

Ṣād (38:75)

Two parts of this verse carry a great deal of weight.

The first is the phrase "what I created with My two hands." Al-Jalālayn says plainly what the phrase is for: hādhā tashrīf li-Ādam, "this is an honouring of Ādam." Everything that exists is made by God, so mentioning His two hands singles this one creation out. Al-Qurṭubī records the reading "what I created without an intermediary." Al-Ṭabarī brings a report from Ibn ʿUmar that God made four things by His hand: the Throne, the garden of ʿAdn, the Pen, and Ādam, and to everything else He said Be. On the attribute of the hands itself, al-Muyassar gives the classical position in one short clause, that it is affirmed in the way that befits Him.

So God is not asking "why did you not bow to clay?" He is asking why did you not bow to the one I honoured with My own hands? The argument about materials was answering a question God had already set aside.

The second part is that this question is not really a request for information. Al-Jalālayn names it exactly: istifhām li-l-tawbīkh, a question asked as a rebuke. God puts the wrong in front of the one who did it, in the form of a question, so that the answer has to come from the wrongdoer's own mouth.

Then look at the two choices God offers him: were you arrogant just now, or were you always one of the exalted? Al-Jalālayn and al-Qurṭubī both explain "the exalted" as meaning the arrogant. Al-Ṭabarī reads the two halves as a fork in the road, asking whether he swelled with pride at that moment or had already been carrying this pride toward his Lord before it.

Both choices lead to the same place. There is no third answer and no innocent one, because God's question has closed every way out before Iblīs even speaks. His reply then proves the diagnosis, since instead of answering either half, he repeats the very ranking that is his arrogance.

The word for the disease

The verb in God's question comes from the root k-b-r, which carries the idea of being big. In this form, istakbara, it means making yourself big and holding yourself above others. It is about putting yourself high, rather than actually being high.

Now compare that with what happened. He was not asked to do anything false, and nothing true was taken away from him. He was asked to lower himself before someone he had ranked below himself, and he had a real fact to back up his ranking.

That is why this is a trap that still works today, and not an old story about a strange being. At some point, every one of us has refused something we were supposed to do while holding a perfectly accurate reason for why we were above doing it. The accuracy of the reason is exactly what made it so convincing. The same pattern plays out in ordinary rooms, and it is only as strong as our willingness to rank ourselves above what we were told to do.

Al-Ṭabarī preserves a saying from Ibn ʿAbbās that sums up the whole thing, and we will come back to it at the end of this course:

"If a man's sin is in arrogance, do not hold out hope for him. If his sin is in disobedience, hold out hope for him. Ādam's sin was disobedience — and Iblīs's sin was arrogance."

Ibn ʿAbbās, reported by al-Ṭabarī at 18:50

Two beings did something wrong in the same garden, and there is still hope for one of them, for a reason that has nothing to do with how big the act was. The next four modules are about what that difference really is.
