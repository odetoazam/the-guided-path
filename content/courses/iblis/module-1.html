Module 1 — "I Am Better Than Him"

Most wrong things people do, they do without a reason they could say out loud. This one came with a reason. It was stated, in one sentence, to God, and the Quran records it word for word.

And it records it twice.

The sentence that occurs exactly twice

In Sūrat al-Aʿrāf, the question and the answer sit in the same verse:

قَالَ مَا مَنَعَكَ أَلَّا تَسْجُدَ إِذْ أَمَرْتُكَ ۖ قَالَ أَنَا۠ خَيْرٌ مِّنْهُ خَلَقْتَنِى مِن نَّارٍ وَخَلَقْتَهُۥ مِن طِينٍ

"He said: What prevented you from prostrating when I commanded you? He said: I am better than him. You created me from fire and created him from clay."

Al-Aʿrāf (7:12)

And in Sūrat Ṣād, chapters away, after a different question, the same words come back:

قَالَ أَنَا۠ خَيْرٌ مِّنْهُ ۖ خَلَقْتَنِى مِن نَّارٍ وَخَلَقْتَهُۥ مِن طِينٍ

"He said: I am better than him. You created me from fire and created him from clay."

Ṣād (38:76)

Two chapters, two occasions, and the reply comes back word for word — the phrase I am better than him occurs in these two verses and nowhere else in the Quran. He has one argument. He has never needed a second one, and he has never improved on it.

Take the argument seriously

Here is where courses on this subject usually go soft. They call the argument stupid and move on. It is not stupid, and pretending it is costs you the lesson.

Look at what he says. Fire and clay. Rank them the obvious way and fire wins on every axis a person would think to use. Fire rises; clay sits. Fire is quick; clay is slow. Fire consumes clay and clay cannot touch fire. If you were handed those two materials and asked which is higher, you would answer the way he answered. His premise is not a lie, and his logic runs cleanly from it.

That is the disturbing part. The first wrong thing that ever happened in creation was not a lie. It was a true fact, aimed at an instruction.

So where does it break?

What the argument leaves out

It answers a question nobody asked.

The order was not "rank these two materials." Go back to the command in the last module: when I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My spirit, fall down to him in prostration. The reason given for the prostration was never the clay. It was the shaping and the breath — that is, it was about what God had done, not what the raw material was.

He answered a materials question. He was asked an obedience question. And a right answer to the wrong question is still the wrong answer.

Notice, too, what his sentence quietly does with God. Read it again: You created me from fire and created him from clay. He is not disputing a fact — he is telling the One who chose both materials that He chose badly. The whole sentence is addressed to God, and it is a correction. He is not refusing a human being. He is grading God's work and reporting the result.

God names the disease before he answers

Go back one verse. Before Iblīs speaks at all, God has already asked a question — and the question contains the diagnosis:

قَالَ يَـٰٓإِبْلِيسُ مَا مَنَعَكَ أَن تَسْجُدَ لِمَا خَلَقْتُ بِيَدَىَّ ۖ أَسْتَكْبَرْتَ أَمْ كُنتَ مِنَ ٱلْعَالِينَ

"He said: O Iblīs, what prevented you from prostrating to what I created with My two hands? Were you arrogant, or were you of the exalted?"

Ṣād (38:75)

Two things in this verse do heavy work.

First: "what I created with My two hands." Al-Jalālayn says plainly what this phrase is doing — hādhā tashrīf li-Ādam, this is an honouring of Ādam. Everything in existence is God's making; naming the two hands singles this one out. Al-Qurṭubī records the reading "what I created without an intermediary." Al-Ṭabarī brings a report from Ibn ʿUmar that God made four things by His hand — the Throne, the garden of ʿAdn, the Pen, and Ādam — and to everything else said Be. (On the attribute itself, the classical position al-Muyassar states is that it is affirmed as God described Himself, in the manner that befits Him, without asking how.)

So God's question is not "why did you not bow to clay?" It is: why did you not bow to the thing I honoured with My own hands? The materials argument was answering a question God had already ruled out of the conversation.

Second: the question is not a question. Al-Jalālayn labels it exactly — istifhām li-l-tawbīkh, a question asked as a rebuke. God is not gathering information. He is naming the thing in front of the one who did it, in the form of a question, so that the answer has to come out of the doer's own mouth.

And look at the two options offered. Were you arrogant just now — or were you always one of the exalted? Al-Jalālayn and al-Qurṭubī both gloss "the exalted" as the arrogant ones. Al-Ṭabarī reads the two limbs as a fork: did you swell up at this moment, or were you already carrying this toward your Lord before it?

Both exits lead to the same room. There is no third answer, and no innocent one. God's question has closed the escape before Iblīs opens his mouth — and what comes out of his mouth then proves the diagnosis, because instead of answering either limb, he restates the very ranking that is the arrogance.

The word for the disease

The verb in God's question is from the root k-b-r — bigness. In this form, istakbara, it means to make yourself big, to hold yourself above. Not to be great; to place yourself great.

Hold that against what happened. He was not asked to believe something false. He was not asked to abandon anything true. He was asked to lower himself in front of something he had ranked below himself — and he had a fact to justify the ranking.

Which is why this is not an ancient story about a strange being. Every one of us has, at some point, refused a thing we were supposed to do and had a genuinely accurate reason ready for why we were above doing it. The accuracy of the reason is exactly what made it convincing. That is the mechanism, and it is running in ordinary rooms.

There is a line preserved from Ibn ʿAbbās, transmitted by al-Ṭabarī, that puts the whole thing in a sentence — and it will come back at the end of this course:

"If a man's sin is in arrogance, do not hold out hope for him. If his sin is in disobedience, hold out hope for him. Ādam's sin was disobedience — and Iblīs's sin was arrogance."

Ibn ʿAbbās, reported by al-Ṭabarī at 18:50

Two beings did a wrong thing in the same garden, days apart. One of them is still hoped for. The difference is not the size of the act. The next four modules are about what the difference actually is.

He has stated his reason. Now he asks for time — and in the next module we look at what he does with it.
