Module 3 — The Switch

Everything so far has been preparation for this module. The whole course was built around it.

So far we have an enemy whose only advantage is where he stands. He works where you are not looking, and his tools cannot touch your will. That leaves one question. What decides whether any of it works on you at all?

The Quran answers with two verses. They sit in different chapters and are not usually read together, yet each one says exactly the opposite of the other.

The verse where the machine turns on

وَمَن يَعْشُ عَن ذِكْرِ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ نُقَيِّضْ لَهُۥ شَيْطَـٰنًا فَهُوَ لَهُۥ قَرِينٌ

"And whoever is blinded from the remembrance of the Most Merciful — We appoint for him a devil, and he is to him a companion."

Az-Zukhruf (43:36)

The verb translated "is blinded" is yaʿshu. Al-Ṭabarī gives its literal meaning, which is looking without steadiness because of a weakness in the eye. So this is dimness rather than full blindness. The eyes still work, but they no longer see clearly.

The verse names what they have stopped seeing clearly. It is dhikr al-Raḥmān, the remembrance of the Most Merciful. When a person's sight grows dim toward that, a devil is assigned to him as a companion. It is assigned after the person turns away, and never before.

The verse where the machine turns off

إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱتَّقَوْا۟ إِذَا مَسَّهُمْ طَـٰٓئِفٌ مِّنَ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنِ تَذَكَّرُوا۟ فَإِذَا هُم مُّبْصِرُونَ

"Indeed, those who fear God — when an impulse touches them from Satan, they remember, and at once they are seeing."

Al-Aʿrāf (7:201)

Now put the two verses side by side.

In the first, a person's sight grows dim toward remembrance, and a companion is assigned to him. In the second, people remember, and at once they can see.

It is the same thing moving in opposite directions. When sight dims away from remembrance, the devil's work begins. When remembrance returns, sight comes back and his work is over. It is like a single switch that can only be on or off.

Notice how fast this happens. The Arabic is fa-idhā hum mubṣirūn. The little word idhā here means "and suddenly there they are," with no gap in between. The verse describes no struggle and no wrestling. They remember, and they see.

It also matters how small the attack is that remembrance clears away. The word is ṭāʾif, which al-Jalālayn explains as something that lightly visited them. It is a passing touch, not a long siege. This is the early stage before anything has grown, and that is when it costs least to turn things around.

Al-Muyassar says this leaves them seeing clearly and disobeying the devil. They have not won a hard fight. They can simply see again, and once they can see, there is nothing left to fight.

The proof is in his name

If everything he does really depends on whether you remember God, you would expect the Quran to say so directly somewhere. It does so in the very last chapter of the book, through the name it gives him.

قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ ٱلنَّاسِ مَلِكِ ٱلنَّاسِ إِلَـٰهِ ٱلنَّاسِ مِن شَرِّ ٱلْوَسْوَاسِ ٱلْخَنَّاسِ ٱلَّذِى يُوَسْوِسُ فِى صُدُورِ ٱلنَّاسِ مِنَ ٱلْجِنَّةِ وَٱلنَّاسِ

"Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of mankind, the Sovereign of mankind, the God of mankind, from the evil of the retreating whisperer — who whispers into the breasts of mankind — from among the jinn and mankind."

An-Nās (114:1–6)

His title here is al-waswās al-khannās, the whisperer who retreats.

Al-Jalālayn says he is called this because he pulls back from the heart whenever God is remembered. Al-Muyassar says he whispers when a person is heedless and hides when God is remembered.

That is a strange thing to name an enemy for. The Quran could have named him for his power, his cunning or his hatred. Instead it names him for the thing that makes him leave. So his very title tells you his weakness, and it tells you how to escape him.

The last verse widens this in a way people often miss: from among the jinn and mankind. Al-Jalālayn reads it as a clarification. The whisperer can be a jinn or a human being. A human whispers out loud the way humans do, and the words travel the same road to the same place. So part of this is simply ordinary conversation, and the same limit holds there too. The words reach you, but what you do is still your choice.

What "remembrance" actually means here

It would be easy to leave this thinking that a phrase repeated enough times works like a charm. That is not what these verses describe, and treating it that way turns a truth into a superstition.

Look at what remembrance does in 7:201. It does not push anything away. It gives a person back their sight, so they can see the situation as it really is again. Once they can see, the one who could only work in the dark has already gone. Retreating is what he does.

So remembrance here is less like a formula and more like coming back to what is true. God is real and He sees this. And the thing being made to look urgent and harmless is neither. Repeating a phrase of remembrance helps because it is a deliberate way of doing exactly that at the moment your attention has drifted.

That makes the struggle much lighter than what most people are carrying. You are not being asked to be strong enough to defeat something. You are only being asked to keep the lights on.

The oath that guarantees his defeat

There is one more thing, and it is the strangest sentence in this whole story.

Go back to the moment he is thrown out in Sūrat Ṣād. He has been expelled and he has asked for time, and God has granted it. Now he declares war, and what matters is what he swears by.

قَالَ فَبِعِزَّتِكَ لَأُغْوِيَنَّهُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ

"He said: By Your might, I will surely mislead them all."

Ṣād (38:82)

He swears "by Your might", bi-ʿizzatika.

He is declaring war on God, and to make his declaration binding he has to swear by something. The only thing he can swear by is the might of the very One he is declaring war on, because there is no rival power to call on. He knows this, and he says it out loud in the middle of rebelling.

This is the divine name al-ʿAzīz, the Almighty. Here the Quran proves it by letting the enemy himself speak. His own oath is the evidence against him.

Then in the very next breath he adds the exception himself, without anyone asking.

إِلَّا عِبَادَكَ مِنْهُمُ ٱلْمُخْلَصِينَ

"Except, among them, Your chosen servants."

Ṣād (38:83)

So he declares total war, and one verse later he himself admits where it stops. He announces that he will lead everyone astray, and in the same breath he concedes the people he cannot touch.

These two verses belong together and should never be quoted apart. If you quote 38:82 alone, you have handed him a dramatic line. If you quote both, you have what is really there. It is a boast that contains its own refutation, sworn by a might that belongs to God.

Where that leaves you

Here is the whole module put together.

Everything he does depends on your attention being somewhere else. He has no authority over you except what you give him. The Quran names him for retreating. He swore his oath by a power that is not his own, and he himself named the people he cannot touch.

The Quran's answer to an enemy you cannot see was never armour. The answer is remembering, because remembering gives you back your sight. Clear sight is the one thing his whole method cannot survive.
