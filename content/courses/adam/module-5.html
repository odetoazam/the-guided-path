Module 5 — The Words You Were Given

Every course on this site ends by turning toward you, but this one has been about you all along. Other figures in the Quran come with armies or kingdoms, while Ādam comes with a situation you already know. He was warned, he forgot, he was fooled by something that sounded like advice, and then he was left standing there after the wrong thing had already been done. So this last module has no separate step for applying the lesson. It simply lets the story finish in your own life.

What were the words?

فَتَلَقَّىٰٓ ءَادَمُ مِن رَّبِّهِۦ كَلِمَـٰتٍ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلتَّوَّابُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ

"Then Ādam received from his Lord words, and He turned to him in forgiveness. Indeed, it is He who is the Accepting of repentance, the Merciful."

Al-Baqarah (2:37)

The verse says kalimāt, words, but it does not quote them. The early scholars asked the obvious question, and their answers were recorded. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Mujāhid and al-Ḥasan said the words are the sentence in al-Aʿrāf 7:23, Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves... Mujāhid in another report, and Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb al-Quraẓī, passed on a different wording, a longer confession that ends with the very names this verse ends with. Al-Ṭabarī weighed these reports and then gave his ruling, on the grounds that the Quran itself points the way. The words Ādam received are the words the Quran shows him saying, rabbanā ẓalamnā anfusanā. To be fair, he adds that the other reports have not been proven wrong, only that no evidence supports them.

Think about what that ruling means for the two verses you have been holding onto since Module 3. On al-Ṭabarī's reading, the sentence of ownership, the one that separated Ādam from Iblīs forever, was itself the gift. Ādam did not come up with it because he was morally brilliant. It was handed to him, and his part was to receive it and say it about himself, to his Lord, while there was still time.

The reading where the words come walking

There is one more layer, preserved in the recognised ways of reciting the Quran. In the recitation of Ibn Kathīr of Makkah, one of the canonical reciters, the grammar of the verse turns around. It reads fa-talaqqā Ādama min rabbihi kalimātun, "then words from his Lord came to Ādam." Al-Baghawī records this reading plainly. Here the words are the subject of the sentence: they came to him, and they were the cause of his repentance.

Both readings have been passed down, and they agree on the meaning while showing it from two sides. In one, a man reaches out and receives the words. In the other, the rescue is already on its way to him before he has moved. Either way, the mercy comes down from God, and it comes first.

The promise on the way down

قَالَ ٱهْبِطَا مِنْهَا جَمِيعًۢا ۖ بَعْضُكُمْ لِبَعْضٍ عَدُوٌّ ۖ فَإِمَّا يَأْتِيَنَّكُم مِّنِّى هُدًى فَمَنِ ٱتَّبَعَ هُدَاىَ فَلَا يَضِلُّ وَلَا يَشْقَىٰ

"He said: Go down from it, both of you, together — some of you an enemy to some. But if there comes to you from Me guidance, then whoever follows My guidance will not go astray nor suffer."

Ṭā Hā (20:123)

Listen to how this ending echoes earlier words in Ṭā Hā. When God first pointed out the enemy, the warning ended with one word, fa-tashqā, "lest you suffer" (20:117). Now the sending down ends with the same word, but negated: whoever follows My guidance lā yaḍillu wa-lā yashqā, will not lose the way and will not suffer. So the sūrah closes the circle it opened, because the promise of guidance takes away the very suffering the warning was about. This is also the first thing ever said about life on earth. God does not tell them simply to survive, and He does not tell them they will be punished. He tells them that guidance will come, and that following it is the way home. The whole religion is that promise, spelled out in full.

Three questions

1. What is your sentence, and whose shape is it in? You have a story you tell about the wrong things you have done. Listen to it the way Module 3 taught you. Does it sound like 7:23, we wronged ourselves, where the wrong is owned and the speaker is inside the sentence? Or does it sound like 7:16, because of what was done to me, in some version of "they made me," "it made me," or "You made me"? Be honest and exact about it. The two kinds of sentence lead out of the same garden by different gates, and 28:63 shows that even the owning sentence can be said too late to matter.

2. What have you forgotten, rather than defied? The first sin came in through a warning that had faded from memory, and not through rebellion. Somewhere in your life there is something you know perfectly well but stop remembering at the moment it matters. Try to name it, because that is where your own version of this story is being written right now.

3. Are you waiting to deserve the return? Ādam was chosen after he fell, and the words he said were given to him rather than written by him. The Turner turns first, again and again, which is what the intensive form of His name means. If you are putting off turning back until you feel worthy of the welcome, you have the direction of this whole story backwards.

The close

Look back over what this course has taught. A creature was made for the earth and honoured by being taught. He was beaten once, by an enemy who used a false oath on a warning Ādam had forgotten. Everything after that depended on one sentence, and that sentence was a gift: we wronged ourselves. He owned the wrong and said it to his Lord while there was still time. Then he was chosen, and then he was sent down with a promise instead of a curse, to do the job that had been announced before the fall was ever part of the story.

Behind the whole story stands one name in the intensive form, al-Tawwāb, the One who turns toward us again and again, and keeps on turning. The first human being learned that name when he was forgiven for the first time, and it has not changed since.

The other being in this pair of courses stood in the same garden and broke his own command, but he came to the same fork from the other side. He refused to say the sentence and held on to his excuse instead. His story is its own course, and if you have not taken it yet, take it next, because the two stories are one lesson told with its two possible endings. On the last day, the Quran records him telling his followers, do not blame me, blame yourselves (14:22). In substance that is Ādam's sentence, that the fault lies with yourselves, except that he points it at everyone but himself. At the end of the world he hands out to others the sentence Ādam said in the garden about himself, while there was still time. For six modules the Quran has been showing you where the difference lies: in who the sentence is about, and in when it gets said.

Rabbanā ẓalamnā anfusanā. These words are still being given, and they were given to you six modules ago.
