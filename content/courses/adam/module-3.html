Module 3 — Two Sentences

Two beings were given one command each. Both broke it. One was cursed until the Day of Judgment and one was chosen and guided, and everything that followed for each of them came out of what he said next. This module is about those two sentences. It is also about what the Quran does with a single root to mark the difference.

The same word, pointed in two directions

Arabic builds families of words out of three-letter roots. The root gh-w-y (غ-و-ي) carries the meaning of straying, of losing the path. The question is where the Quran puts it, and in what form.

Of Ādam, at the lowest moment of his story:

فَأَكَلَا مِنْهَا فَبَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْءَٰتُهُمَا وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِن وَرَقِ ٱلْجَنَّةِ ۚ وَعَصَىٰٓ ءَادَمُ رَبَّهُۥ فَغَوَىٰ

"So they both ate of it, and their shame became apparent to them, and they began to fasten over themselves leaves of the garden. And Ādam disobeyed his Lord, and went astray."

Ṭā Hā (20:121)

Fa-ghawā means "and he went astray." This is the simple form of the verb, and Ādam is its subject. He strayed. The sentence gives the act to him, whole.

Iblīs says something else, twice, in al-Aʿrāf and again in al-Ḥijr. The same accusation opens two different vows.

قَالَ فَبِمَآ أَغْوَيْتَنِى لَأَقْعُدَنَّ لَهُمْ صِرَٰطَكَ ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ

"He said: Because You have made me stray, I will surely sit for them on Your straight path."

Al-Aʿrāf (7:16)

Bimā aghwaytanī means "because You made me stray." It is the same root in a different form, the causative, which is the form that hands the action to someone else. Here the someone else is God. This exact accusation occurs twice in the Quran, at 7:16 and 15:39, and both times it comes from him. A full scan of the root shows something colder. In his mouth the form is always the causative. He either receives it as blame, You made me stray, or aims it at others, I will make them all stray, which he says in the same breath at 15:39. He never uses the simple form of himself. The form Ādam's verse uses, ghawā, meaning I strayed and no one did this to me, never crosses his lips.

The scan turns up one more thing, and it belongs here even though it happens at the end of time. In Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ (28:63) the condemned misleaders finally say: we made them stray as we ourselves strayed. Who is speaking there is a recorded difference. Al-Ṭabarī rules that they are the devils who spent history leading people astray, and Qatāda, Muqātil and Ibn Kathīr read it the same way, while al-Baghawī and Ibn ʿĀshūr hear the human chiefs of misguidance. The point for this module stands either way, and on al-Ṭabarī's reading it lands harder. There, at last, is the simple form in the first person, ghawaynā, we strayed, spoken by the adversary's own party. So the sentence exists, and even they can say it. The question this course keeps asking is when, because they say it at the moment it stops mattering, and Ādam said his own version in time.

Put the two next to each other and see who is speaking in each. The first is the Quran's own narration about Ādam. He strayed, the verb is simple, the act is his. The second is Iblīs speaking for himself: You made me stray. One root, two grammatical forms, and those two forms are the two relationships a creature can have to its own wrong. It can be owned, or it can be assigned to somebody else. The Quran did not write an essay about responsibility. It conjugated it. Ādam's own spoken sentence comes two sections down, and it owns even more than the narration does.

The sentence said out loud

The grammar is the skeleton. The spoken sentence is the flesh, and al-Aʿrāf records it in the dual, because the two of them said it together.

قَالَا رَبَّنَا ظَلَمْنَآ أَنفُسَنَا وَإِن لَّمْ تَغْفِرْ لَنَا وَتَرْحَمْنَا لَنَكُونَنَّ مِنَ ٱلْخَـٰسِرِينَ

"They said: Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves, and if You do not forgive us and have mercy upon us, we will surely be among the losers."

Al-Aʿrāf (7:23)

Every part of it is doing work. Our Lord means the sentence is addressed upward, so it is a prayer rather than a comment. We wronged keeps the verb theirs, with no agent shifted and no whisperer mentioned, even though a whisperer existed and had deceived them with a false oath. They had a defence available and did not use it. Ourselves names the injured party, and in their accounting it is their own souls, so the sin is a self-inflicted wound rather than a broken rule to be counted. The second half then throws the whole weight onto God: if You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we are lost. There is no claim, no bargaining chip, and no promise of improvement offered in trade. The sentence has two contents. The wrong is ours, and the rescue is Yours.

Now hear the other being answer for his wrong once more: because You made me stray, I will sit for them on Your straight path. The blame goes to God, and the sentence also turns the wrong into a grievance and the grievance into a mission. A refusal that had a reason becomes a war that has a justification. Every later difference between the two stories is already inside these two sentences.

Chosen, afterwards

So what did the sentence of ownership open? Ṭā Hā answers in the very next verse, and the first word of it is the hinge.

ثُمَّ ٱجْتَبَـٰهُ رَبُّهُۥ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ وَهَدَىٰ

"Then his Lord chose him, and turned to him in forgiveness, and guided."

Ṭā Hā (20:122)

Thumma means "then." Arabic has words for one thing following another instantly, and thumma is the slower one, closer to "and after that." The verse is careful. The choosing came after the disobedience. Ijtabāhu means He chose him, elected him, drew him close. It does not mean He let him back in on probation, and it does not mean He restored him to the standing he had before. He was chosen after. The Quran also states the election a second time with another verb of choosing, iṣṭafā: indeed God chose Ādam, in Āl ʿImrān 3:33, first in a list with Nūḥ, the family of Ibrāhīm, and the family of ʿImrān, above all the worlds.

Sit with the order of events, because religion is often imagined the other way round. In the imagined version you are good, and so you are chosen. In the recorded version he was honoured, he fell, he owned it, and then he was chosen. In this story the sentence of ownership works as a doorway into a rank he did not hold before the fall.

The name behind the door

Here is the verse from Module 2 once more, because now you can read its ending.

فَتَلَقَّىٰٓ ءَادَمُ مِن رَّبِّهِۦ كَلِمَـٰتٍ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلتَّوَّابُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ

"Then Ādam received from his Lord words, and He turned to him in forgiveness. Indeed, it is He who is the Accepting of repentance, the Merciful."

Al-Baqarah (2:37)

The verb tāba literally means to turn. The Quran uses it for both parties in this exchange. The servant turns back to God, and God turns toward the servant. One word covers both directions of the same meeting.

The question is which side gets the intensive form. Al-Tawwāb uses the Arabic pattern that means again and again, by nature, in abundance. It is not the title of the repentant servant here. It is God's name. The grammar of maximum repetition sits with the One receiving the return, not with the one making it.

In plain words, God does more returning than we do. Every time anyone turns back, they find the Turner already turned. That is the name this course was walking toward, and the first human being learned it before he ever reached the earth, from inside the first forgiveness, on the day of the first wrong.
