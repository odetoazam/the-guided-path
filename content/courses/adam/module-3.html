Module 3 — Two Sentences

Two beings were given one command each. Both broke it. Everything that happened to each of them afterwards — one cursed until the Day of Judgment, one chosen and guided — follows from what each said next. This module is about those two sentences, and about something astonishing the Quran does with a single root to mark the difference.

The same word, pointed in two directions

Arabic builds families of words out of three-letter roots. The root gh-w-y (غ-و-ي) carries the meaning of straying — losing the path. Now watch where the Quran puts it, and in what form.

Of Ādam, at the lowest moment of his story:

فَأَكَلَا مِنْهَا فَبَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْءَٰتُهُمَا وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِن وَرَقِ ٱلْجَنَّةِ ۚ وَعَصَىٰٓ ءَادَمُ رَبَّهُۥ فَغَوَىٰ

"So they both ate of it, and their shame became apparent to them, and they began to fasten over themselves leaves of the garden. And Ādam disobeyed his Lord, and went astray."

Ṭā Hā (20:121)

Fa-ghawā — "and he went astray." The simple form of the verb, and Ādam is its subject. He strayed. The sentence gives the act to him, whole.

Now Iblīs, twice — in al-Aʿrāf and again in al-Ḥijr, with the same accusation at the head of two different vows:

قَالَ فَبِمَآ أَغْوَيْتَنِى لَأَقْعُدَنَّ لَهُمْ صِرَٰطَكَ ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ

"He said: Because You have made me stray, I will surely sit for them on Your straight path."

Al-Aʿrāf (7:16)

Bimā aghwaytanī — "because You made me stray." Same root. Different form — the causative, the form that hands the action to someone else — and the someone else is God. This exact accusation occurs twice in the Quran (7:16 and 15:39), both times from him. And a full scan of the root turns up something colder still: in his mouth it is always the causative — received as blame (You made me stray), or aimed at others (I will make them all stray, in the very same breath at 15:39). Not once does he use the simple form of himself. The form Ādam's verse uses — ghawā, I strayed, no one did this to me — never crosses his lips.

One more find from that scan, and it belongs here even though it happens at the end of time. In Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ (28:63), the condemned misleaders finally say: we made them stray as we ourselves strayed. Who exactly is speaking there is a recorded difference: al-Ṭabarī rules they are the devils who spent history leading people astray — Qatāda, Muqātil and Ibn Kathīr read it the same way — while al-Baghawī and Ibn ʿĀshūr hear the human chiefs of misguidance. Either way, the point for this module stands, and on al-Ṭabarī's reading it lands even harder: there, at last, is the simple form in the first person — ghawaynā, we strayed — spoken by the adversary's own party. The sentence exists. It can be said, even by them. The only question this course keeps asking is when — because it is said there at the moment it stops mattering, and Ādam said his own version in time.

Put them side by side — and notice who is speaking in each. The first is the Quran's own narration about Ādam: he strayed, the verb simple, the act his. The second is Iblīs's own mouth: You made me stray. One root, two grammatical forms, and the two forms are the two possible relationships a creature can have to its own wrong: owned, or assigned away. The Quran did not write an essay about responsibility. It conjugated it. (Ādam's own spoken sentence is coming two sections down — and when it comes, it will own even more than the narration does.)

The sentence said out loud

The grammar is the skeleton. The spoken sentence is the flesh — and al-Aʿrāf records it, in the dual, because they said it together:

قَالَا رَبَّنَا ظَلَمْنَآ أَنفُسَنَا وَإِن لَّمْ تَغْفِرْ لَنَا وَتَرْحَمْنَا لَنَكُونَنَّ مِنَ ٱلْخَـٰسِرِينَ

"They said: Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves, and if You do not forgive us and have mercy upon us, we will surely be among the losers."

Al-Aʿrāf (7:23)

Look at its parts, because every one is doing work. Our Lord — the sentence is addressed upward; it is prayer, not commentary. We wronged — the verb is theirs, no agent shifted, no whisperer mentioned, though a whisperer existed and had in fact deceived them with a false oath. They had a defence available and declined to file it. Ourselves — the wronged party, in their accounting, is their own souls: sin as self-inflicted wound, not rule-breaking bookkeeping. And then the second half throws the whole weight onto God: if You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we are lost. No claim, no bargaining chip, no promised improvement offered in trade. The sentence has exactly two contents: the wrong is ours, and the rescue is Yours.

Now hear the other being answer for his wrong, one more time: because You made me stray, I will sit for them on Your straight path. Not only is the blame assigned to God — the sentence turns the wrong into a grievance, and the grievance into a mission. The refusal that had a reason becomes the war that has a justification. Every downstream difference between the two stories is already inside these two sentences.

Chosen — afterwards

And what did the sentence of ownership open? Ṭā Hā answers in the very next verse, and the first word of it is the hinge:

ثُمَّ ٱجْتَبَـٰهُ رَبُّهُۥ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ وَهَدَىٰ

"Then his Lord chose him, and turned to him in forgiveness, and guided."

Ṭā Hā (20:122)

Thumma — "then." Arabic has words for instant sequence; thumma is the slower one, the "and after that." The verse is careful: the choosing came after the disobedience. Ijtabāhu — He chose him, elected him, drew him close. Not: readmitted him on probation. Not: restored him to his previous standing. Chosen — after. And the Quran states the election a second time with another verb of choosing — iṣṭafā: indeed God chose Ādam, Āl ʿImrān 3:33, first in a list with Nūḥ, the family of Ibrāhīm, and the family of ʿImrān, above all the worlds.

Sit with the order of events, because religion is often imagined the other way around. The imagined version: be good, and you will be chosen. The recorded version: he was honoured, he fell, he owned it — and then he was chosen. In this story the sentence of ownership works as a doorway — to a rank he did not hold before the fall.

The name behind the door

One more time, the verse from Module 2 — because now you can read its ending:

فَتَلَقَّىٰٓ ءَادَمُ مِن رَّبِّهِۦ كَلِمَـٰتٍ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلتَّوَّابُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ

"Then Ādam received from his Lord words, and He turned to him in forgiveness. Indeed, it is He who is the Accepting of repentance, the Merciful."

Al-Baqarah (2:37)

The verb tāba literally means to turn. The Quran uses it for both parties in this transaction — the servant turns back to God, and God turns toward the servant. One word, both directions of the same meeting.

But look at which side gets the intensive form. Al-Tawwāb — the pattern in Arabic that means again and again, by nature, in abundance — is not the title of the repentant servant here. It is God's name. The grammar of maximum repetition is attached to the One receiving the return, not the one making it.

Say that in plain words and let it land: God does more returning than we do. Every time anyone turns back, they find the Turner already turned. That is the name this course was walking toward, and the first human being learned it before he ever reached the earth — from inside the first forgiveness, on the day of the first wrong.
