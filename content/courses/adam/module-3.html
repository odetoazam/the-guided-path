Module 3 — Two Sentences

Two beings were each given one command, and both of them broke it. One was cursed until the Day of Judgment and the other was chosen and guided, and everything that happened to each of them afterwards came from what he said next. This module is about those two sentences, and about how the Quran uses a single Arabic root to show the difference between them.

The same word, pointed in two directions

Arabic builds families of words out of three-letter roots. The root gh-w-y (غ-و-ي) carries the meaning of straying, of losing the path. What matters here is where the Quran uses this root, and in which form.

Here is what it says about Ādam at the lowest moment of his story.

فَأَكَلَا مِنْهَا فَبَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْءَٰتُهُمَا وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِن وَرَقِ ٱلْجَنَّةِ ۚ وَعَصَىٰٓ ءَادَمُ رَبَّهُۥ فَغَوَىٰ

"So they both ate of it, and their shame became apparent to them, and they began to fasten over themselves leaves of the garden. And Ādam disobeyed his Lord, and went astray."

Ṭā Hā (20:121)

Fa-ghawā means "and he went astray." This is the simple form of the verb, and Ādam is its subject, so the sentence says plainly that he strayed and gives the whole act to him.

Iblīs says something different, and he says it twice, once in al-Aʿrāf and again in al-Ḥijr. Both times the same accusation begins a different vow.

قَالَ فَبِمَآ أَغْوَيْتَنِى لَأَقْعُدَنَّ لَهُمْ صِرَٰطَكَ ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ

"He said: Because You have made me stray, I will surely sit for them on Your straight path."

Al-Aʿrāf (7:16)

Bimā aghwaytanī means "because You made me stray." It is the same root in a different form, the causative, which is the form used when someone makes someone else do something. Here Iblīs says that the someone else is God. This exact accusation appears twice in the Quran, at 7:16 and 15:39, and both times it comes from him. When you look at every use of this root in the Quran, you find something even colder. Whenever Iblīs uses it, he uses the causative form. Either he uses it to blame God, You made me stray, or he aims it at others, I will make them all stray, which he says in the same breath at 15:39. He never uses the simple form about himself. The form in Ādam's verse, ghawā, meaning "I strayed, and no one did this to me," never comes out of his mouth.

Looking at every use of the root turns up one more thing, and it belongs here even though it happens at the end of time. In Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ (28:63), the condemned who led others astray finally say, we made them stray as we ourselves strayed. The scholars disagreed about who is speaking there. Al-Ṭabarī rules that they are the devils who spent all of history leading people astray, and Qatāda, Muqātil and Ibn Kathīr read it the same way. Al-Baghawī and Ibn ʿĀshūr understood them to be the human leaders of misguidance. The point for this module stands either way, though it is even stronger on al-Ṭabarī's reading. Here, at last, is the simple form in the first person, ghawaynā, "we strayed," spoken by the enemy's own side. So the sentence exists, and even they can say it. The question this course keeps asking is when it gets said, because they say it only once it can no longer help them, while Ādam said his own version in time.

Now put the two side by side and notice who is speaking in each. The first is the Quran itself telling us about Ādam: he strayed, the verb is simple, and the act is his. The second is Iblīs speaking for himself: You made me stray. The root is the same, and its two grammatical forms show the two ways a creature can relate to its own wrong. Either it owns the wrong, or it puts the blame on somebody else. The Quran does not write an essay about responsibility here. It shows it through the form of a single verb. Ādam's own spoken sentence comes in the next section, and it takes even more ownership than the narration does.

The sentence said out loud

The grammar gives us the outline, and the spoken sentence fills it in. Al-Aʿrāf records that sentence in the dual form, because the two of them said it together.

قَالَا رَبَّنَا ظَلَمْنَآ أَنفُسَنَا وَإِن لَّمْ تَغْفِرْ لَنَا وَتَرْحَمْنَا لَنَكُونَنَّ مِنَ ٱلْخَـٰسِرِينَ

"They said: Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves, and if You do not forgive us and have mercy upon us, we will surely be among the losers."

Al-Aʿrāf (7:23)

Every part of it matters. It begins with Our Lord, so it is spoken to God, which makes it a prayer rather than a remark. We wronged keeps the act theirs. They do not shift the blame, and they do not mention the whisperer, even though he was real and had deceived them with a false oath. They had an excuse ready and chose not to use it. Ourselves names who was hurt, and in their eyes it was their own souls, so they see the sin as a wound they gave themselves rather than a broken rule to be counted. The second half then puts the whole weight on God: if You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we are lost. They make no claim, offer nothing to bargain with, and promise nothing about doing better in exchange. The whole sentence says just two things: the wrong is ours, and the rescue is Yours.

Now listen again to how the other being answers for his wrong: because You made me stray, I will sit for them on Your straight path. He blames God, and in the same sentence he turns his wrong into a grievance and his grievance into a mission. What began as a refusal with a reason becomes a war with a justification. Every later difference between the two stories is already there in these two sentences.

Chosen, afterwards

So where did Ādam's sentence of ownership lead? Ṭā Hā answers in the very next verse, and everything turns on its first word.

ثُمَّ ٱجْتَبَـٰهُ رَبُّهُۥ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ وَهَدَىٰ

"Then his Lord chose him, and turned to him in forgiveness, and guided."

Ṭā Hā (20:122)

Thumma means "then." Arabic has other words for one thing following another straight away. Thumma is the slower one, closer to "and after that." The verse is careful to say that the choosing came after the disobedience. Ijtabāhu means He chose him, singled him out, and drew him close. It does not mean God let him back in on probation, and it does not mean God simply put him back where he stood before. He was chosen after he fell. The Quran states this choosing a second time with another verb of choosing, iṣṭafā, in Āl ʿImrān 3:33: indeed God chose Ādam. There he comes first in a list with Nūḥ, the family of Ibrāhīm, and the family of ʿImrān, chosen above all the worlds.

Think about that order for a moment, because people often imagine religion working the other way round. In the version people imagine, you are good, and so you are chosen. In the version the Quran records, Ādam was honoured, he fell, he owned his wrong, and then he was chosen. In this story, owning the wrong became his way into a rank he did not have before the fall.

The name behind the door

Here is the verse from Module 2 again, because now you are ready to read how it ends.

فَتَلَقَّىٰٓ ءَادَمُ مِن رَّبِّهِۦ كَلِمَـٰتٍ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلتَّوَّابُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ

"Then Ādam received from his Lord words, and He turned to him in forgiveness. Indeed, it is He who is the Accepting of repentance, the Merciful."

Al-Baqarah (2:37)

The verb tāba literally means to turn, and the Quran uses it for both sides of this meeting. The servant turns back to God, and God turns toward the servant, so one word covers both directions.

What matters is which side gets the intensive form. Al-Tawwāb uses the Arabic pattern for something done again and again, by nature, and in abundance. Here that word is God's name, and it does not describe the servant who repents. So the form that means the most repetition belongs to the One who receives the return, and not to the one who makes it.

Put simply, God does more turning than we do. Whenever anyone turns back to Him, they find the Turner has already turned toward them. This is the name the whole course has been leading to. The first human being learned it before he ever reached the earth, when he was forgiven for the first time, on the very day of the first wrong.
