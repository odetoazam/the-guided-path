Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You

Over time, every famous story picks up extra details that everyone knows but the original source never gave, and this story has picked up more than most. This module sets those details aside and looks at what the Quran actually left out. Then it asks why one story is told seven times, and it looks at the hardest verse in the course together with what the scholars said about it.

What is not there

The woman is never named. The name everyone knows her by appears nowhere in the Quran, and that has been checked against all 6,236 verses. Every time, she is zawjuka, "your wife." More important than the missing name is the missing blame. Nowhere in the Quran is she singled out for blame, and no scene shows her being tempted first or tempting him. In al-Aʿrāf the whisper is spoken to both of them, and both of them eat. The books of commentary do carry stories in which she tempts him first, eating before him and then handing it to him. Those reports come from the stories handed down by earlier religious communities rather than from the Quran, and this course leaves them where the scholars who checked the sources placed them. The claim here is strictly about the Quran's own text, and about that text it is completely solid. The one place the Quran does name someone in the failure, and Ādam disobeyed his Lord, it names him. And when the repentance comes, the verb is dual: qālā, "the two of them said, Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves." They were deceived together, they fell together, and they were forgiven together. The version in which the woman is the weak link and the doorway to sin is simply not the version this book tells.

The tree is never identified. The Quran points at it, this tree, and reports the name the tempter gave it, the tree of eternity, but it never says what kind of tree it was. The commentaries are full of guesses, such as wheat, the grapevine, and the fig. The Quran told the story seven times and could have named the tree in any of them, and it chose not to every time. Whatever the tree was, the story is complete without knowing, which quietly tells us that the tree itself was never the point. What mattered was the command, and any tree would have done.

There is no serpent in this story. No snake appears in any of the seven tellings, and there is no animal helping the tempter and no creature waiting at the gate. The Quran does have snakes elsewhere, since Mūsā's staff turns into one, but there is none here. In this book the tempter is a voice with a name and a motive, and he comes as an adviser, swearing that he means well. That difference matters in everyday life, because you will never be tempted by something that looks like a snake. You will be tempted by something that sounds like good advice.

There is no inherited guilt. Nowhere does the Quran say that the children of Ādam carry his sin. His repentance was accepted, fa-tāba ʿalayhi, and the matter was closed in his own lifetime. Only seven verses after his creation is announced, the second sūrah already says fa-tāba ʿalayhi (2:30 to 2:37). And after the fall, the Quran says of his descendants, We have certainly honoured the children of Ādam (17:70), so the honouring comes after the fall. Going down to the earth was announced as his task before the sin ever happened, as 2:30 shows: I will make upon the earth a khalīfa. The earth was always where the job was. The sin changed only the way he arrived there, and he arrived forgiven, taught, and carrying the words.

Why tell it seven times?

Each telling keeps its own part of the story. Al-Baqarah has the teaching of the names and the words of return. Al-Aʿrāf has the false oath and the repentance spoken aloud. Al-Ḥijr describes the clay and states the limit God set on the enemy. Al-Isrāʾ has the enemy's coldest vow. Al-Kahf shrinks the story to one verse aimed at the reader: will you take him and his offspring as allies? Ṭā Hā has the forgetting, the hardest verse, and the choosing that comes after it. Ṣād has the two hands and the enemy's oath by God's might.

No two of the seven tellings keep the same details. Each one shows a different side of the same event and leaves out what another telling already gave, a little like turning an object in your hand to see it from every angle. The repetition shows how much weight the Quran puts on this story. It is the pattern of every temptation and every return, and the Quran keeps bringing it back because its readers keep living it.

The hard verse

Now we come to the sentence this module cannot skip, in the verse where the story reaches its lowest point.

فَأَكَلَا مِنْهَا فَبَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْءَٰتُهُمَا وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِن وَرَقِ ٱلْجَنَّةِ ۚ وَعَصَىٰٓ ءَادَمُ رَبَّهُۥ فَغَوَىٰ

"So they both ate of it, and their shame became apparent to them, and they began to fasten over themselves leaves of the garden. And Ādam disobeyed his Lord, and went astray."

Ṭā Hā (20:121)

Wa ʿaṣā Ādamu rabbahu fa-ghawā. He disobeyed, and he went astray, and this is said about a prophet. The Quran chose those words, and a course that softened them or skipped them would be misleading you about the Quran itself. The question they raise is a real one, and scholars have been answering it for over a thousand years. Muslims hold that prophets are protected, maʿṣūm, in what they pass on from God. So how should we understand this verse?

Here are the main classical answers, along with the scholars who gave them.

Al-Qushayrī, quoted by al-Qurṭubī, who adds his own approval, "this is good," sets out the framework the tradition uses for every verse of this kind. Whatever wrong is said of a prophet falls into one of three kinds. It may be a minor slip, which does not betray their mission. It may be leaving the better choice, which only counts as wrong when measured against their own extraordinary rank. Or it may have happened before they became prophets.

Ibn Fūrak, whom al-Qurṭubī quotes with approval, takes the third answer, and he finds his evidence in the next verse of this very passage: thumma jtabāhu rabbuhu... wa-hadā, "then his Lord chose him and guided." Since the choosing and the guiding came after the disobedience, he argues that the event happened before God chose Ādam. Once God appointed him and protected him in his mission, what came before could not touch it. So on Ibn Fūrak's reading, the verse that raises the problem is answered one line later.

Al-Rāzī states his own view plainly. "The best position in this matter," he writes, is that the event happened before prophethood. He then adds a sharp argument that holds whichever view you take. Whatever the event was, the text allows us to use the verb but not the label. The Quran says that Ādam disobeyed, but that does not give anyone the right to call him "a disobedient one." Al-Rāzī quotes al-ʿUtbī's comparison: a man who once stitched a garment has sewn, but only a man who sews for a living is called a tailor. Something done once does not become who you are. After God accepted Ādam's repentance and honoured him with prophethood, using that label would be like calling a man who has embraced Islam an unbeliever because of what he was before.

Al-Ālūsī reports where most of the schools ended up. The majority hold that minor slips are possible for prophets, and he names al-Jubbāʾī as one who disagreed. Slips caused by forgetting are widely accepted, on the condition that the prophet is alerted and then stops. His own conclusion is careful. Given Ādam's rank, it is best to say that the event came before prophethood and that it was a slip. Even so, it weighed on Ādam enormously, precisely because his rank was so high. Al-Ālūsī quotes the saying that the good deeds of the righteous are the shortcomings of those brought near. The higher someone's rank, the smaller the thing that counts against them, because it is measured by love rather than by law.

So where does this course stand? With the text, read as a whole. The Quran says ʿaṣā without any embarrassment, and the same Quran says thumma jtabāhu in the very next breath and closes the matter. Nothing in the verse touches what prophethood protects, which is the delivery of God's message, and every scholar quoted here agrees on that. In the end, these categories protect the reader more than they protect Ādam. They keep us from drawing the one conclusion the Quran never draws, that the man was defined by his worst hour. One verse tells us about that hour and the next tells us God's verdict on him, so anyone who quotes the first without the second is quoting only half a sentence.

It is also worth seeing how the scholars dealt with weak ideas at the edges of the tradition. At some point, someone suggested that ghawā here really meant that Ādam got sick from eating too much. Al-Zamakhsharī records the suggestion and bluntly calls it a foul reading, and al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī pass that judgment along. The scholars' job was to read the verse exactly, while keeping Ādam's rank and their reverence for him intact, and they had no patience for easy ways out. They were never trying to make readers comfortable, and we should not try to either.

There is one module left. It turns the whole course toward you, starting with the fact that the sentence which saved Ādam was never one he made up himself.
