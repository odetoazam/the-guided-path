Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You

Over time, every famous story picks up extra details. Everyone knows them, but the original source never gave them. This story has picked up more than most. This module sets those details aside and looks at what the Quran actually left out. Then it asks why one story is told seven times. Last, it looks at the hardest verse in the course together with what the scholars said about it.

What is not there

The woman is never named. The name everyone knows her by appears nowhere in the Quran. That has been checked against all 6,236 verses. Every time she is zawjuka, "your wife." The missing blame matters even more than the missing name. Nowhere in the Quran is she singled out for blame. No scene shows her being tempted first or tempting him. In al-Aʿrāf the whisper is spoken to both of them, and both of them eat. The books of commentary do carry stories in which she tempts him first. In them she eats before him and then hands it to him. Those reports come from the stories handed down by earlier religious communities, not from the Quran. This course leaves them where the scholars who checked the sources placed them. The claim here is strictly about the Quran's own text, and about that text it is completely solid. The Quran names someone in the failure in only one place, and it names him: and Ādam disobeyed his Lord. When the repentance comes, the verb is dual. It is qālā, which means "the two of them said." Together they said: Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves. They were deceived together and fell together. Then they were forgiven together. In some versions the woman is the weak link and the doorway to sin. That is simply not the version this book tells.

The tree is never identified. The Quran points at it as this tree. It reports the name the tempter gave it, the tree of eternity. But it never says what kind of tree it was. The commentaries are full of guesses such as wheat, the grapevine and the fig. The Quran told the story seven times and could have named the tree in any of them. It chose not to every time. Whatever the tree was, the story is complete without knowing. That quietly tells us the tree itself was never the point. The point was the command, and any tree would have done.

There is no serpent in this story. No snake appears in any of the seven tellings. There is no animal helping the tempter and no creature waiting at the gate. The Quran does have snakes elsewhere, since Mūsā's staff turns into one. But there is none here. In this book the tempter is a voice with a name and a motive. He comes as an adviser, swearing that he means well. That difference matters in everyday life. You will never be tempted by something that looks like a snake. You will be tempted by something that sounds like good advice.

There is no inherited guilt. Nowhere does the Quran say that the children of Ādam carry his sin. His repentance was accepted (fa-tāba ʿalayhi), and the matter was closed in his own lifetime. The second sūrah announces his creation at 2:30. Only seven verses later (2:37) it already says fa-tāba ʿalayhi. After the fall the Quran says of his descendants, We have certainly honoured the children of Ādam (17:70). So the honouring comes after the fall. Going down to the earth was announced as his task before the sin ever happened. That is what 2:30 shows: I will make upon the earth a khalīfa. The earth was always where the job was. The sin changed only the way he arrived there. He arrived forgiven and taught, and he carried the words.

Why tell it seven times?

Each telling keeps its own part of the story. Al-Baqarah has the teaching of the names and the words of return. Al-Aʿrāf has the false oath and the repentance spoken aloud. Al-Ḥijr describes the clay and states the limit God set on the enemy. Al-Isrāʾ has the enemy's coldest vow. Al-Kahf shrinks the story to one verse aimed at the reader: will you take him and his offspring as allies? Ṭā Hā has the forgetting and the hardest verse, and then the choosing that comes after it. Ṣād has the two hands and the enemy's oath by God's might.

No two of the seven tellings keep the same details. Each one shows a different side of the same event and leaves out what another telling already gave. It is a little like turning an object in your hand to see it from every angle. The repetition shows how much weight the Quran puts on this story. It is the pattern of every temptation and every return. The Quran keeps bringing it back because its readers keep living it.

The hard verse

Now we come to the sentence this module cannot skip. It is in the verse where the story reaches its lowest point.

فَأَكَلَا مِنْهَا فَبَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْءَٰتُهُمَا وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِن وَرَقِ ٱلْجَنَّةِ ۚ وَعَصَىٰٓ ءَادَمُ رَبَّهُۥ فَغَوَىٰ

"So they both ate of it, and their shame became apparent to them, and they began to fasten over themselves leaves of the garden. And Ādam disobeyed his Lord, and went astray."

Ṭā Hā (20:121)

Wa ʿaṣā Ādamu rabbahu fa-ghawā. He disobeyed and he went astray, and this is said about a prophet. The Quran chose those words. A course that softened them or skipped them would be misleading you about the Quran itself. The question they raise is a real one, and scholars have been answering it for over a thousand years. Muslims hold that prophets are protected (maʿṣūm) in what they pass on from God. So how should we understand this verse?

Here are the main classical answers, along with the scholars who gave them.

Al-Qushayrī sets out the framework the tradition uses for every verse of this kind. Al-Qurṭubī quotes him and adds his own approval: "this is good." Whatever wrong is said of a prophet falls into one of three kinds. It may be a minor slip, which does not betray their mission. It may be leaving the better choice. That only counts as wrong when measured against their own extraordinary rank. Or it may have happened before they became prophets.

Ibn Fūrak takes the third answer, and al-Qurṭubī quotes him with approval. He finds his evidence in the next verse of this very passage: thumma jtabāhu rabbuhu... wa-hadā, "then his Lord chose him and guided." The choosing and the guiding came after the disobedience. So he argues that the event happened before God chose Ādam. Once God appointed him and protected him in his mission, what came before could not touch it. On Ibn Fūrak's reading, the verse that raises the problem is answered one line later.

Al-Rāzī states his own view plainly. He writes that "the best position in this matter" is that the event happened before prophethood. He then adds a sharp argument that holds whichever view you take. Whatever the event was, the text allows us to use the verb but not the label. The Quran says that Ādam disobeyed. That does not give anyone the right to call him "a disobedient one." Al-Rāzī quotes a comparison from al-ʿUtbī. A man who once stitched a garment has sewn, but only a man who sews for a living is called a tailor. Something done once does not become who you are. God accepted Ādam's repentance and honoured him with prophethood. After that, using the label would be like calling a man who has embraced Islam an unbeliever because of what he was before.

Al-Ālūsī reports where most of the schools ended up. The majority hold that minor slips are possible for prophets. He names al-Jubbāʾī as one who disagreed. Slips caused by forgetting are widely accepted, on the condition that the prophet is alerted and then stops. His own conclusion is careful. Given Ādam's rank, he says it is best to hold that the event came before prophethood and that it was a slip. Even so, it weighed on Ādam enormously. That was precisely because his rank was so high. Al-Ālūsī quotes the saying that the good deeds of the righteous are the shortcomings of those brought near. The higher someone's rank, the smaller the thing that counts against them. That is because it is measured by love rather than by law.

So where does this course stand? It stands with the text, read as a whole. The Quran says ʿaṣā without any embarrassment. The same Quran says thumma jtabāhu in the very next breath and closes the matter. Nothing in the verse touches what prophethood protects, which is the delivery of God's message. Every scholar quoted here agrees on that. In the end these categories protect the reader more than they protect Ādam. They keep us from drawing the one conclusion the Quran never draws: that the man was defined by his worst hour. One verse tells us about that hour, and the next tells us God's verdict on him. So anyone who quotes the first without the second is quoting only half a sentence.

It is also worth seeing how the scholars dealt with weak ideas at the edges of the tradition. At some point someone suggested that ghawā here really meant that Ādam got sick from eating too much. Al-Zamakhsharī records the suggestion and bluntly calls it a foul reading. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī pass that judgment along. The scholars' job was to read the verse exactly while keeping Ādam's rank and their reverence for him intact. They had no patience for easy ways out. They were never trying to make readers comfortable, and we should not try to either.

There is one module left. It turns the whole course toward you. It starts with the fact that the sentence which saved Ādam was never one he made up himself.
