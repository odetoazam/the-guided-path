Module 3 — She Believed the Word

This is the module the course is named for. It follows one command across sixty-three sūrahs to its verdict, watches one root turn into her title, and ends on the divine name that saturates the chapter bearing her name. By the end you will know what the Quran says Maryam was, in its own grammar, at both ends of the book.

Chosen twice in one verse

وَإِذْ قَالَتِ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ يَـٰمَرْيَمُ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ ٱصْطَفَىٰكِ وَطَهَّرَكِ وَٱصْطَفَىٰكِ عَلَىٰ نِسَآءِ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

"And when the angels said: O Maryam, indeed God has chosen you and purified you, and chosen you above the women of the worlds."

Āl ʿImrān (3:42)

Iṣṭafāki... wa-ṣṭafāki means chose you, and chose you. It is the same verb twice in a single verse, with the purification set between the two choosings like a stone in a ring. The early scholars read the two as different things: a choosing for something, meaning for His service and for the consecrated life her mother vowed, and then a choosing above others.

On that "above" there is a real discussion, and this course will not flatten it. Al-Ṭabarī explains it as above the women of her time, and he cites the Prophet's ﷺ words on the excellence of Maryam and Khadīja. Others read it absolutely. The four-women hadith, recorded by Aḥmad from Ibn ʿAbbās, sets her among the best women of Paradise: Khadīja, Fāṭima, Maryam, Āsiya. Module 4 lays that table properly. What no one disputes is the verb of election, doubled, over the girl the vow had not been shaped for.

The command that waits sixty-three sūrahs

The verse after the choosing gives her the job.

يَـٰمَرْيَمُ ٱقْنُتِى لِرَبِّكِ وَٱسْجُدِى وَٱرْكَعِى مَعَ ٱلرَّٰكِعِينَ

"O Maryam, be devoutly obedient to your Lord and prostrate and bow with those who bow."

Āl ʿImrān (3:43)

Uqnutī is a command, from the root of qunūt, which means devoted, continuous, humble obedience, and standing long before God. That is the assignment given to a girl in a prayer-room, in the third sūrah of the Quran.

Now travel sixty-three sūrahs forward, to at-Taḥrīm, and to the last verse in the entire Quran that names her. There God holds up two women as the example for all who believe, and closes her story with this.

وَمَرْيَمَ ٱبْنَتَ عِمْرَٰنَ ٱلَّتِىٓ أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِنَا وَصَدَّقَتْ بِكَلِمَـٰتِ رَبِّهَا وَكُتُبِهِۦ وَكَانَتْ مِنَ ٱلْقَـٰنِتِينَ

"And Maryam, the daughter of ʿImrān, who guarded her chastity — so We breathed into [the opening of her garment] of Our Spirit; and she believed in the words of her Lord and His scriptures, and was of the devoutly obedient."

At-Taḥrīm (66:12)

Wa-kānat mina l-qānitīn. That is the same root as the command, now in the past tense, as a verdict: she was of the qānitīn. The Quran opens her story with uqnutī, be devoted, and its final word about her, sixty-three sūrahs later, reports that she was. One girl gets an assignment, and her last verse files the completion certificate, with most of the book in between.

The verdict also carries a famous grammatical surprise. Qānitīn is the masculine plural. Al-Baghawī says the point in so many words: she is counted among the devout people, the whole company of the obedient, men included, "and that is why He did not say qānitāt." ʿAṭāʾ read the word as "the praying ones," and another reading takes the company to be her own righteous house, so the commentaries carry the options with their names. On al-Baghawī's reading, the grammar itself raises her. Her devotion is filed under devotion, not under devout women.

She believed, so she is called The Believing

Look one clause earlier in that same verse: wa-ṣaddaqat bi-kalimāti rabbihā wa-kutubih, she believed the words of her Lord and His scriptures. The verb is ṣaddaqat, from the root ṣ-d-q. She held God's words to be true, and that includes the Word spoken about her and into her own life, the one no human being would ever believe from her mouth.

Now take the title the Quran gives her in its fifth sūrah: ummuhu ṣiddīqa, his mother was a ṣiddīqa (5:75). It is the same root, in the most intensive form Arabic has for it. It does not mean someone who told the truth once. It means someone whose whole substance is confirming truth. That is the rank Abū Bakr holds in its masculine form. Al-Saʿdī, commenting on the verdict verse, welds the two ends together in a definition: her ṣaddaqat is the perfection of knowledge and her place among the qānitīn is the perfection of deed, "for she is a ṣiddīqa, and ṣiddīqiyya is the perfection of knowledge and deed."

See what the Quran has done across its whole span, because this is what the course is named for. In Sūrah 4 the Word is cast to her: kalimatuhu alqāhā ilā Maryam (4:171). In Sūrah 66 she believes the words, ṣaddaqat. In Sūrah 5 she is named for believing, ṣiddīqa. She received the Word, she believed the Word, and she became the title of believing. Her miracle was carried in her body for nine months. Her rank, the Quran keeps saying, was carried in her conviction. Of everything that happened to the only woman the book names, the thing it names her for is the believing.

The name over the whole chapter

The last piece is a divine name, and this course does not have to argue for it, because the Quran wove it into the fabric of her sūrah.

Count how often ar-Raḥmān, the Most Merciful, occurs across the chapters of the Quran, and one sūrah stands alone. Sūrah 19, Maryam, carries the name sixteen times, which is more than double any other sūrah in the book. The chapter that bears her name holds the Quran's densest concentration of the name of mercy.

Inside her own scenes, see who keeps reaching for it. At the moment of most fear, her refuge is I seek refuge in ar-Raḥmān from you (19:18). At the moment of most exposure, her vow is I have vowed to ar-Raḥmān a fast (19:26). The boy himself is announced as a mercy from Us (19:21). Her instincts run to that name the way other people's run to their mother tongue, because it was her mother tongue. The miḥrāb taught it to her first.

That is the shape of the whole module, said once and plainly. A girl was commanded to devote herself, and the end of the Quran certifies that she did. She believed a Word no one else would believe, and the Quran renamed her for the believing. The name of God she fled to in the dark is the name God wrapped her chapter in. Two modules remain: what the Quran deliberately withheld, and what her silence has to teach the part of your life no one believes.
