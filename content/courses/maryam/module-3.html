Module 3 — She Believed the Word

This is the module the course is named after. We follow one command given to Maryam across sixty-three sūrahs until the Quran tells us she fulfilled it. We watch one Arabic root become her title, and we end with the name of God that fills the chapter named after her. By the end you will know what the Quran says Maryam was, in its own words, at both ends of the book.

Chosen twice in one verse

وَإِذْ قَالَتِ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ يَـٰمَرْيَمُ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ ٱصْطَفَىٰكِ وَطَهَّرَكِ وَٱصْطَفَىٰكِ عَلَىٰ نِسَآءِ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

"And when the angels said: O Maryam, indeed God has chosen you and purified you, and chosen you above the women of the worlds."

Āl ʿImrān (3:42)

The angels say iṣṭafāki... wa-ṣṭafāki, which means He chose you, and He chose you. The same verb comes twice in one verse, and between the two choosings God says He purified her. The early scholars understood the two choosings as two different things. The first is being chosen for something, meaning for God's service and for the life given to Him that her mother had vowed. The second is being chosen above others.

The scholars genuinely discussed what "above" means here, and this course will not pretend they agreed. Al-Ṭabarī explains it as above the women of her own time, and he cites what the Prophet ﷺ said about how excellent Maryam and Khadīja were. Others take it with no limit at all. The hadith of the four women, recorded by Aḥmad from Ibn ʿAbbās, names her among the best women of Paradise: Khadīja, Fāṭima, Maryam and Āsiya. Module 4 sets out these views properly. What nobody disputes is that God used the verb of choosing twice, for the very girl her mother's vow had not been made for.

The command that waits sixty-three sūrahs

In the next verse, after telling her she is chosen, the angels tell her what she is to do.

يَـٰمَرْيَمُ ٱقْنُتِى لِرَبِّكِ وَٱسْجُدِى وَٱرْكَعِى مَعَ ٱلرَّٰكِعِينَ

"O Maryam, be devoutly obedient to your Lord and prostrate and bow with those who bow."

Āl ʿImrān (3:43)

Uqnutī is a command. It comes from the same root as qunūt, which means devoted, steady, humble obedience, and standing for a long time before God in prayer. That is the task given to a girl in a prayer room, in the third sūrah of the Quran.

Now move sixty-three sūrahs forward, to at-Taḥrīm, where we find the last verse in the whole Quran that names her. There God holds up two women as an example for all who believe, and He ends her story with these words.

وَمَرْيَمَ ٱبْنَتَ عِمْرَٰنَ ٱلَّتِىٓ أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِنَا وَصَدَّقَتْ بِكَلِمَـٰتِ رَبِّهَا وَكُتُبِهِۦ وَكَانَتْ مِنَ ٱلْقَـٰنِتِينَ

"And Maryam, the daughter of ʿImrān, who guarded her chastity — so We breathed into [the opening of her garment] of Our Spirit; and she believed in the words of her Lord and His scriptures, and was of the devoutly obedient."

At-Taḥrīm (66:12)

The verse says wa-kānat mina l-qānitīn, she was one of the qānitīn. This is the same root as the command, but now it is in the past tense, as a final judgment on her life. So the Quran begins her story by telling her uqnutī, be devoted, and its last word about her, sixty-three sūrahs later, tells us that she was. A girl is given a task, and in her last verse God confirms she completed it, with most of the Quran lying in between.

There is also a well-known surprise in the grammar of that last word. Qānitīn is the masculine plural. Al-Baghawī explains the point directly: she is counted among the devout people, the whole company of the obedient, men included, "and that is why He did not say qānitāt," which would be the feminine form. ʿAṭāʾ understood the word as "the praying ones", and another reading takes the group to be her own righteous family, and the commentaries record each view with the name of the scholar who held it. On al-Baghawī's reading, the grammar itself honours her, because her devotion is counted among all devoted people, not only among devoted women.

She believed, so she is called The Believing

Look at the clause just before that in the same verse: wa-ṣaddaqat bi-kalimāti rabbihā wa-kutubih, she believed the words of her Lord and His scriptures. The verb is ṣaddaqat, from the root ṣ-d-q. She accepted God's words as true, including the Word spoken about her and into her own life, which no one would ever have believed if it had come from her own mouth.

Now look at the title the Quran gives her in its fifth sūrah: ummuhu ṣiddīqa, his mother was a ṣiddīqa (5:75). This is the same root, in the strongest form Arabic has for it. It does not describe someone who told the truth once. It describes someone whose whole being is given to confirming the truth, and it is the same rank Abū Bakr holds in the masculine form. Al-Saʿdī, commenting on the verse in Sūrah 66, joins the two parts of that verse in one definition. Her ṣaddaqat, her believing, is the perfection of knowledge, and her place among the qānitīn is the perfection of action. As he puts it, "for she is a ṣiddīqa, and ṣiddīqiyya is the perfection of knowledge and deed."

Look at what the Quran has done across its whole length, because this is why the course has its name. In Sūrah 4, God's Word is sent to her: kalimatuhu alqāhā ilā Maryam (4:171). In Sūrah 66, she believes the words, ṣaddaqat. In Sūrah 5, she is given a title for believing, ṣiddīqa. So she received the Word, she believed the Word, and she came to be known for believing. Her miracle was carried in her body, but the Quran keeps telling us that her rank came from how firmly she believed. Of everything that happened to the only woman the book names, what it chooses to name her for is her believing.

The name over the whole chapter

The last part of this module is about one of God's names, and the course does not need to argue for it, because the Quran has woven it all through her sūrah.

If you count how often ar-Raḥmān, the Most Merciful, appears in each chapter of the Quran, one sūrah stands out from all the rest. Sūrah 19, Maryam, uses the name sixteen times, which is more than double any other sūrah in the book. So the chapter named after her has more of this name of mercy packed into it than anywhere else in the Quran.

Notice who keeps calling on that name in her own scenes. When she is most afraid, she says I seek refuge in ar-Raḥmān from you (19:18). When she is about to face everyone's accusations, her vow is I have vowed to ar-Raḥmān a fast (19:26). The boy himself is announced as a mercy from Us (19:21). She turns to that name as naturally as other people fall back on their mother tongue, because for her it was the first language she learned, and she learned it in the miḥrāb.

So here is the whole module, said simply. A girl was told to devote herself to God, and at the end of the Quran God confirms that she did. She believed a Word that no one else would have believed, and the Quran gave her a title for that believing. And the name of God she ran to when she was alone and afraid is the same name God filled her chapter with. Two modules are left: what the Quran chose not to tell us, and what her silence can teach you about the part of your life that nobody believes.
