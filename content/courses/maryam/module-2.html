Module 2 — The Stranger and the Palm Tree

This module looks closely at two scenes. In the first, Maryam faces the moment her whole life of worship had been preparing her for, and we see what a well-trained heart does in the first few seconds of fear. In the second, the Quran tells us about the hardest day of her life and keeps in the hardest thing she said. Together, the two scenes answer a question most of us have asked at some point: what does trusting God look like when you are the one going through it?

Scene one: the stranger behind the screen

Maryam has gone away from her family to a place in the east and put up a screen so she can be alone. She is a young woman who has chosen to be by herself for worship. God sends His Spirit to her, and he takes the form of basharan sawiyyā, a man, well-formed and complete. It is hard to imagine a more exact test. No one can see her and no one is with her, and as far as she knows, the man in front of her is exactly the kind of danger a chaste woman fears most.

This is the first thing the Quran records her doing.

قَالَتْ إِنِّىٓ أَعُوذُ بِٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ مِنكَ إِن كُنتَ تَقِيًّا

"She said: Indeed, I seek refuge in the Most Merciful from you, if you should be fearing of God."

Maryam (19:18)

She does two things in that verse, and we can learn from both. First, before anything else, she turns to God for protection. She runs to Him with her very first breath, and she calls on Him by the name ar-Raḥmān, the Most Merciful. Module 3 shows that this name marks her whole sūrah. Second, she says in kunta taqiyyā, "if you fear God." She speaks to whatever conscience the stranger has, trusting that the strongest thing she can appeal to in him is his fear of God, if he has any. So she turns to God first, and then she speaks to the God-fearing part of the other person. Years of worship in the miḥrāb had made this her instinct.

The stranger tells her he is only a messenger from her Lord, sent to give her a pure boy. Her question has no doubt in it. She is simply being honest about her situation: how can I have a boy when no man has touched me, and I have not been unchaste? The reply is the one these courses meet at every miracle: thus said your Lord, it is easy for Me. He gives two reasons for the boy. He will be a sign for the people, and he will be a mercy from Us (19:21), which is worth remembering in a sūrah built around the name ar-Raḥmān. Then the scene ends by making clear that the matter is settled: it was a matter decreed.

Scene two: the palm tree

فَأَجَآءَهَا ٱلْمَخَاضُ إِلَىٰ جِذْعِ ٱلنَّخْلَةِ قَالَتْ يَـٰلَيْتَنِى مِتُّ قَبْلَ هَـٰذَا وَكُنتُ نَسْيًا مَّنسِيًّا

"And the pains of childbirth drove her to the trunk of a palm tree. She said: Oh, I wish I had died before this and was in oblivion, forgotten."

Maryam (19:23)

Think about who this is. She is the most chosen woman in the book, told by angels that she was chosen twice, and she is carrying a child given through God's own Spirit. Yet here she is, alone and in labour, leaning against the trunk of a tree, and what she cries out is I wish I had died before this and been something forgotten.

The Quran kept those words. It could have shown us a Maryam who went through labour calmly, but instead it recorded the Maryam who wished she had never existed. Al-Saʿdī treats her cry gently, as the old books do at their best. He says the wish came from the crushing weight of the moment and from her fear of what people would say. He adds that there was no good for her in the wish, since all the good lay in what God had actually decreed. Nobody scolds her anywhere in the story. The answer she receives is do not grieve.

The great scholars disagreed about who called out to her from below. Al-Qurṭubī held that it was the angel, and Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿAlqama, al-Ḍaḥḥāk and Qatāda took that view. Al-Ṭabarī held that it was her newborn son himself, speaking his first words even before he spoke from the cradle, and Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and Ubayy ibn Kaʿb read it that way. Either way, the comfort reaches her from the very place her shame was pointing at. So being chosen by God does not take the pain away. Someone God has chosen can reach a moment so crushing that they wish they had died, and Heaven answers with a stream, with food, and with do not grieve.

Shake the trunk

وَهُزِّىٓ إِلَيْكِ بِجِذْعِ ٱلنَّخْلَةِ تُسَـٰقِطْ عَلَيْكِ رُطَبًا جَنِيًّا

"And shake toward you the trunk of the palm tree; it will drop upon you ripe, fresh dates."

Maryam (19:25)

Compare this with the room she grew up in, because the Quran has set the two scenes side by side on purpose. In the miḥrāb, food simply appeared without her asking or doing anything, bi-ghayri ḥisāb. Here at the palm tree, the stream is already given, but to get the dates she has to shake the trunk. A woman worn out by labour is told to pull on the trunk of a palm tree. Compared with what it really takes to shake a palm tree, what she can do is almost nothing. The dates were coming from God either way, and still she was asked to shake.

These two scenes of provision, one from her childhood and one from the day she gave birth, together teach how to receive from God. Sometimes the gift comes without you lifting a hand. Other times the same Giver asks you to move your hand first. That is not because the gift needs your effort, but because you need it. So do the small thing you have been given to do, and leave the dropping of the dates to God.

The vow of silence

فَكُلِى وَٱشْرَبِى وَقَرِّى عَيْنًا ۖ فَإِمَّا تَرَيِنَّ مِنَ ٱلْبَشَرِ أَحَدًا فَقُولِىٓ إِنِّى نَذَرْتُ لِلرَّحْمَـٰنِ صَوْمًا فَلَنْ أُكَلِّمَ ٱلْيَوْمَ إِنسِيًّا

"So eat and drink and cool your eye. And if you see from among humanity anyone, say: Indeed, I have vowed to the Most Merciful a fast, so I will not speak today to any person."

Maryam (19:26)

First she is told to eat, drink and let her eye be comforted, so the kindness comes before anything else. Then comes the strangest instruction in her whole story. She is about to walk back to face the worst accusation her people can make, and she is told to give up the thing everyone reaches for first, which is her own voice. She makes her vow of silence to ar-Raḥmān, which is the second time in these scenes that she herself calls on that name. In between those two moments, the same root appears once more, when the boy is given as raḥma, a mercy (19:21).

Why silence? The story answers that when she stands before her people, and Module 5 asks what the answer means for you. For now, notice what the command tells us about who will defend her. She is not told to prepare her case. She is told to keep a fast, because someone else will be defending her, and he is the baby in her arms.
