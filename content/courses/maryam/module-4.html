Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You

By now you know how these courses work. When the Quran leaves a detail out, that silence teaches us something. When the scholars disagreed, we give their names and their views. Maryam's story includes the most famous objection outsiders have raised against the Quran, and the Prophet ﷺ answered it himself. It also includes one question about her that is still genuinely open. We cover both here, but we start with what the Quran leaves out.

What is not there

There is no husband in this story. Other traditions place a man beside her, a carpenter she was engaged to. But he does not appear anywhere in the Quran. She becomes pregnant alone and gives birth alone. She returns to her people alone and answers to no man. He is completely absent, and that matters. The story is told so that God alone protects her, provides for her and defends her. Every human support a woman in her situation would normally lean on has been left out. That way we can see clearly who is really carrying her.

We are never told her age, and the place is never named. We only hear of a place in the east and a remote place, and of a palm tree. The Quran strips the scene down to what we need, as in every course in this series. That way its lesson can travel with us anywhere.

The Quran also rejects the slander against her without ever repeating it. Sūrah 4 condemns those who spoke against Maryam a tremendous calumny (4:156). The Arabic is buhtānan ʿaẓīmā, yet the verse never puts the lie into words. You will read her people's outburst below. Even there they only say you have brought a monstrous thing and point out that her parents were good people. The actual accusation is never written out, not even once. This is how honour is really defended. The Quran calls the accusation monstrous and refuses to give it the respect of repeating it. For fourteen centuries readers have known she was slandered. Yet they cannot learn the words of that slander from the one book that matters most.

"O sister of Hārūn", the question the Prophet ﷺ was asked

When she carries the child back to her people, they begin their accusation with a name that has sparked arguments for centuries.

يَـٰٓأُخْتَ هَـٰرُونَ مَا كَانَ أَبُوكِ ٱمْرَأَ سَوْءٍ وَمَا كَانَتْ أُمُّكِ بَغِيًّا

"O sister of Hārūn, your father was not a man of evil, nor was your mother unchaste."

Maryam (19:28)

Why would they call her the sister of Hārūn? The objection is easy to make, and critics have been making it for centuries. Hārūn is known in English as Aaron. He lived alongside Mūsā many centuries before Maryam. So has the Quran mixed up Maryam with Miriam, the sister of Aaron?

This is the easiest of the hard questions in the course. Someone asked it while the Prophet ﷺ was alive, and he answered it. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records the story from al-Mughīra ibn Shuʿba. When al-Mughīra went to Najrān, the Christians there raised exactly this objection with him. They said: you recite "O sister of Hārūn," and Mūsā was before ʿĪsā by such-and-such. He brought the question back to the Messenger of God ﷺ. The Prophet ﷺ said: "They used to name people after their prophets and the righteous who came before them". That answer comes from the very person through whom the Quran was given. People named their children after the prophets and righteous people of the past. So a man among her people could be called Hārūn a thousand years after the first Hārūn, and nobody would think anything of it.

The early scholars explain how this naming custom worked, and they give their judgments openly. Al-Ṭabarī holds that she was being linked to a righteous man of her own people who shared the name. He holds this precisely because the Prophet's ﷺ report settles the matter. Al-Baghawī reports that Qatāda held the same view. Al-Baghawī agrees in his own words: the intended sense is not brotherhood of lineage. The other views are still recorded with the names of those who held them. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥa and al-Suddī understood it as a way of naming her by family line. On their view she was one of Hārūn's descendants. A man from the tribe of Tamīm could be called "brother of Tamīm" in the same way. Ibn Kathīr gives the meaning as "O you who were like Hārūn in worship." Al-Qurṭubī begins his whole discussion by saying the people differed.

The harshest view recorded belongs here as well. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Baghawī both pass on a view that this Hārūn was a well-known sinner among her people. On that view, calling her his sister was an insult comparing her to him. So the crowd was either bitterly reminding her of her religious family, or openly insulting her. The next thing they say points clearly to the first meaning: your father was not an evil man, nor was your mother unchaste. Whichever view you take, one thing stays the same. The way they addressed her follows the naming custom of their culture, exactly as the Prophet ﷺ said. So the claim that the Quran confused two family trees falls apart.

Was she a prophetess?

This is the question that is still genuinely open. An angel brought her God's message directly. Sūrah 66 says she believed the words of her Lord. In Sūrah 3 angels speak to her by name. They tell her she is chosen and give her commands. So the scholars asked directly whether Maryam was a nabiyya, a prophetess.

One group says yes, and they are not a small minority. Al-Qurṭubī states it firmly: "the correct position is that Maryam is a nabiyya". His reason is that God sent revelation to her through the angel, just as He did with the prophets. Through the angel God spoke to her, gave her duties and brought her good news. Ibn ʿĀshūr reaches the same conclusion by drawing a distinction. He writes that prophethood can belong to women, but being a messenger cannot. The Arabic words are nubūʾa for prophethood and risāla for being a messenger.

The other group says no, and they too have a great scholar on their side in al-Rāzī. He says plainly that Maryam, peace be upon her, was not of the prophets. He cites the verse We sent not before you except men. He explains that her miracles do not need prophethood to explain them. They could be a karāma, an honour God gave her. They could also be an early sign announcing ʿĪsā, or a miracle that belonged to Zakariyyā. Al-Qurṭubī himself passes on a strong argument for this side. Seeing and hearing an angel does not on its own make someone a prophet. The Companions saw Jibrīl in the form of the man Diḥya al-Kalbī, and nobody calls them prophets. On this view the title ṣiddīqa in 5:75 tells us her actual rank. It is the highest rank below prophethood, and it is used precisely to make clear where she stands.

So the two judgments stand against each other. Al-Qurṭubī concludes that she was a prophetess. He claims in his own words that the greater number of scholars are on his side. Al-Rāzī concludes that she was not. This course reports both judgments and leaves the question open. But before you feel worried for her, notice what both sides agree on even while they disagree. Both agree that an angel of God spoke to her by name, and that a message from Heaven reached her. Both agree that the Quran confirms her as a ṣiddīqa and holds her up as an example for all believers, men and women. The debate is about her title. Her high standing with God was never in question.

"Above the women of the worlds", and how far

There is one more named disagreement, because Module 3 promised to explain it. Here too the judgments stand against each other. The angels say she was chosen above the women of the worlds. Al-Ṭabarī explains it as above the women of her own time. That was also the view of al-Ḥasan and Ibn Jurayj. Al-Ṭabarī bases this on the Prophet's ﷺ saying that the best of its women was Maryam bint ʿImrān, and the best of its women was Khadīja bint Khuwaylid. Al-Qurṭubī takes the other view and names the scholars who share it. For him the choosing has no limit and covers all women until the end of time. He writes that this is the correct view, "the position of al-Zajjāj and others". He then ranks the other great women after her.

There are hadith reports behind both views. Aḥmad records from Ibn ʿAbbās that the Prophet ﷺ drew four lines and named the best women of Paradise: Khadīja, Fāṭima, Maryam and Āsiya. Al-Qurṭubī passes on another chain from Ibn ʿAbbās, which he judges to be sound. That one does put them in order. The leader of the women of the worlds is Maryam, and after her come Fāṭima, then Khadīja and then Āsiya. This is a real question about how far the choosing reaches, and there are scholars on both sides. This course reports it rather than settling it. Just notice this much. Even on the narrowest view in the tradition, she is still the only woman the Quran names and the only one it gives a sūrah to. In her final verse, it holds her up beside no one except Pharaoh's believing wife.
