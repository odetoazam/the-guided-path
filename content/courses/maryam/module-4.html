Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You

You know this module's method by now. In this book the withheld detail is instruction, and a recorded disagreement is taught with the scholars' names attached. Maryam's story carries the Quran's most famous outside objection, which the Prophet ﷺ himself answered, and one genuinely open question about what she was. Both are here. The silences come first.

What is not there

There is no husband in this story. The figure other traditions place beside her, the carpenter and the betrothal, has no existence anywhere in the Quran. She conceives alone, labours alone, returns alone, and answers no man. The absence is total, and it carries weight. Her story is built so that the only protector, provider and defender in it is God. Every human support a woman in her situation would lean on has been written out, so that what carries her is visible.

Her age is never given, and the place is never named. We get an eastern place, a remote place, and a palm. The scene is stripped the way every course on this shelf has watched scenes be stripped, down to the portable diagram.

The slander is refuted without ever being spelled out. Sūrah 4 condemns those who spoke against Maryam a tremendous calumny, buhtānan ʿaẓīmā (4:156), and gives the calumny no words at all. Even where her people's outburst is recorded, which you will read below, it stops at you have brought a monstrous thing and the pointed mention of her upright parents. The explicit charge is never written out once. The Quran defends her honour the way honour is actually defended: the accusation is named as monstrous and denied the dignity of its own wording. Fourteen centuries of readers know she was slandered and cannot learn the slander's phrasing from the only book that mattered.

"O sister of Hārūn", the question the Prophet ﷺ was asked

When she carries the child to her people, their accusation opens with an address that has launched a thousand polemics.

يَـٰٓأُخْتَ هَـٰرُونَ مَا كَانَ أَبُوكِ ٱمْرَأَ سَوْءٍ وَمَا كَانَتْ أُمُّكِ بَغِيًّا

"O sister of Hārūn, your father was not a man of evil, nor was your mother unchaste."

Maryam (19:28)

Sister of Hārūn? The objection writes itself, and people have been writing it for centuries. Hārūn, or Aaron, lived with Mūsā, centuries before Maryam. Has the Quran confused Maryam with Miriam, Aaron's sister?

This is the easiest hard question in the course, because it was asked while the Prophet ﷺ was alive, and he answered it. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records it from al-Mughīra ibn Shuʿba. When he went to Najrān, the Christians there put exactly this objection to him: you recite "O sister of Hārūn," and Mūsā was before ʿĪsā by such-and-such. He brought the question back to the Messenger of God ﷺ, who said: "They used to name people after their prophets and the righteous who came before them". That is the answer, and it comes from the mouth the Quran came through. People were named after the prophets and the righteous of earlier times. So a boy among her people could carry the name Hārūn a thousand years after Hārūn, and no one counted the centuries.

The early scholars fill in how the convention was working, and here the rulings are on the table. Al-Ṭabarī rules that she was being ascribed to a man of her own people, a righteous namesake, and he rules that way precisely because the Prophet's ﷺ report settles it. Qatāda carries the same reading, according to al-Baghawī, and al-Baghawī rules the same way in his own words: the intended sense is not brotherhood of lineage. The alternatives stay recorded with their holders. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥa and al-Suddī read it as an address by lineage, meaning one of Hārūn's descendants, the way "brother of Tamīm" is said to a man of Tamīm. Ibn Kathīr renders the sense as "O you who resembled Hārūn in worship." Al-Qurṭubī opens his whole treatment with the words the people differed.

The harshest recorded reading belongs here too. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Baghawī both transmit a view that the Hārūn in question was a notorious sinner among their people, and that the address was a taunt likening her to him. So the crowd's words were either a bitter appeal to her devout pedigree or an open insult. Their next clause makes the first reading plain: your father was not an evil man, nor was your mother unchaste. One thing holds on every recorded reading. The address is the naming idiom of their culture, exactly as the Prophet ﷺ said, and the polemic that reads a genealogy error into it dissolves.

Was she a prophetess?

Here is the genuinely open question. An angel brought her the word of God directly. Sūrah 66 says she believed the words of her Lord, and Sūrah 3 has angels addressing her by name with election and command. So the scholars asked, in so many words, whether Maryam was a nabiyya, a prophetess.

One side says yes, and it is not a fringe. Al-Qurṭubī rules it outright: "the correct position is that Maryam is a nabiyya". His reason is that God conveyed revelation to her by means of the angel, with address, obligation and glad tidings, as He did to the prophets. Ibn ʿĀshūr draws the same line with a distinction. Prophethood, nubūʾa, can belong to women, he writes, though messengership, risāla, does not.

The other side says no, and it has its own great name in al-Rāzī. He states it flatly: Maryam, peace be upon her, was not of the prophets, and he cites the verse We sent not before you except men. Her wonders, he explains, need no prophethood to account for them. They can be a karāma honouring her, or an early sign heralding ʿĪsā, or a miracle running to Zakariyyā's account. Al-Qurṭubī himself transmits a sharp argument for this side. Seeing and hearing an angel does not by itself make a prophet. The Companions saw Jibrīl in the form of the man Diḥya al-Kalbī, and no one calls them prophets. On this side's reading, the ṣiddīqa of 5:75 is her stated station, the highest rank below prophethood, named precisely to settle where she stands.

So the rulings face each other. Al-Qurṭubī closes for prophetess, and claims in his own words that the greater number are with him. Al-Rāzī closes against. This course reports the facing rulings and leaves them facing. Before you feel any anxiety on her behalf, see what both sides agree on while they argue. Both agree an angel of God addressed her by name. Both agree a message from Heaven reached her. Both agree the Quran certifies her as ṣiddīqa and holds her up as the example for all believers, men and women. They are debating her title. Her standing was never the dispute.

"Above the women of the worlds", and how far

One more named difference, because Module 3 promised it, and here too the rulings face each other. When the angels say she was chosen above the women of the worlds, al-Ṭabarī explains it as above the women of her time, a reading al-Ḥasan and Ibn Jurayj held. He anchors it in the Prophet's ﷺ saying that the best of its women was Maryam bint ʿImrān, and the best of its women Khadīja bint Khuwaylid. Al-Qurṭubī rules the other way and names his side. The election is absolute, over all women to the end of time, and he writes that this is the correct view, "the position of al-Zajjāj and others". He then ranks the rest after her.

The hadith material sits under both rulings. Aḥmad records from Ibn ʿAbbās the four lines the Prophet ﷺ drew, naming the best women of Paradise: Khadīja, Fāṭima, Maryam, Āsiya. Al-Qurṭubī carries another route from Ibn ʿAbbās, which he grades sound, and that one does rank them: the mistress of the women of the worlds is Maryam, then Fāṭima, then Khadīja, then Āsiya. It is a real question of scope with rulings on both sides, and this course reports it rather than settling it. Note only this. On the narrowest reading in the tradition, she is still the only woman the Quran names, and the only one it gives a sūrah. In her final verse it holds her up beside no one but Pharaoh's believing wife.
