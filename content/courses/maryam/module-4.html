Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You

By now you know this module's method: in this book, the withheld detail is instruction, and the recorded disagreement is taught with the scholars' names attached. Maryam's story carries the Quran's most famous outside objection — answered, it turns out, by the Prophet ﷺ himself — and one genuinely open question about what she was. Both are here. The silences first.

What is not there

There is no husband in this story. Not a minor one — none. The figure other traditions place beside her — the carpenter, the betrothal — has no existence anywhere in the Quran. She conceives alone, labours alone, returns alone, and answers no man. The absence is total, and it is load-bearing: her story is constructed so that the only protector, provider, and defender in it is God. Every human support a woman in her situation would lean on has been deliberately written out, so that what carries her is visible.

Her age is never given, and neither is the place's name. An eastern place; a remote place; a palm. The scene is stripped the way every course on this shelf has now watched scenes be stripped — down to the portable diagram.

The slander is refuted without being spelled out. Sūrah 4 condemns those who spoke against Maryam a tremendous calumny — buhtānan ʿaẓīmā (4:156) — and gives the calumny no words at all. Even when her people's outburst is recorded (you will hear it below), it stops at you have brought a monstrous thing and the pointed mention of her upright parents; the explicit charge is never once written out. The Quran defends her honour the way honour is actually defended: the accusation is named as monstrous and denied the dignity of its own wording. Fourteen centuries of readers know she was slandered and cannot learn the slander's phrasing from the only book that mattered.

"O sister of Hārūn" — the question the Prophet ﷺ was asked

When she carries the child to her people, their accusation opens with an address that has launched a thousand polemics:

يَـٰٓأُخْتَ هَـٰرُونَ مَا كَانَ أَبُوكِ ٱمْرَأَ سَوْءٍ وَمَا كَانَتْ أُمُّكِ بَغِيًّا

"O sister of Hārūn, your father was not a man of evil, nor was your mother unchaste."

Maryam (19:28)

Sister of Hārūn? The objection writes itself, and people have been writing it for centuries: Hārūn — Aaron — lived with Mūsā, centuries before Maryam; has the Quran confused Maryam with Miriam, Aaron's sister?

Here is what makes this the easiest hard question in the course: it was asked while the Prophet ﷺ was alive, and he answered it. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records it from al-Mughīra ibn Shuʿba: when he went to Najrān, the Christians there put exactly this objection to him — you recite "O sister of Hārūn," and Mūsā was before ʿĪsā by such-and-such. He brought the question back to the Messenger of God ﷺ, who said: "They used to name people after their prophets and the righteous who came before them." That is the answer, from the mouth the Quran came through: people were named after the prophets and the righteous who came before — the way a boy in her people could carry the name Hārūn a thousand years after Hārūn, with no one checking the centuries.

The commentators fill in how the convention was working, and here the rulings are on the table. Al-Ṭabarī rules — precisely because the Prophet's ﷺ report settles it — that she was being ascribed to a man of her own people: a righteous namesake, the reading Qatāda also carries per al-Baghawī; and al-Baghawī rules the same way in his own words: the intended sense is not brotherhood of lineage. The alternatives stay recorded with their holders: ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥa and al-Suddī read a lineage-address — of Hārūn's descendants, the way "brother of Tamīm" is said to a Tamīmī — and Ibn Kathīr renders the sense "O you who resembled Hārūn in worship." Al-Qurṭubī opens the whole treatment with the people differed. And honesty requires the harshest recorded reading too: al-Ṭabarī and al-Baghawī both transmit a view that the Hārūn in question was a notorious sinner of their people, and the address a taunt — likening her to him. So the crowd's words were either a bitter appeal to her devout pedigree — their next clause says it outright: your father was not an evil man, nor was your mother unchaste — or an open insult. On every recorded reading, one thing holds: the address is the naming-idiom of their culture, exactly as the Prophet ﷺ said, and the polemic that reads a genealogy error into it dissolves.

Was she a prophetess?

Now the genuinely open question. An angel brought her the word of God directly. Sūrah 66 says she believed the words of her Lord; Sūrah 3 has angels addressing her by name with election and command. So the scholars asked, in so many words: was Maryam a nabiyya — a prophetess?

One side says yes, and it is not a fringe. Al-Qurṭubī rules it outright — "the correct position is that Maryam is a nabiyya" — because God conveyed revelation to her by means of the angel, with address, obligation, and glad tidings, as He did to the prophets. Ibn ʿĀshūr draws the same line with a distinction: prophethood (nubūʾa) can belong to women, he writes, though messengership (risāla) does not.

The other side says no, and it has its own great name: al-Rāzī. He states it flatly — Maryam, peace be upon her, was not of the prophets — citing the verse: We sent not before you except men. Her wonders, he explains, need no prophethood to account for: a karāma honouring her, or an early sign heralding ʿĪsā, or a miracle running to Zakariyyā's account. And the argument al-Qurṭubī himself transmits for this side is a sharp one: seeing and hearing an angel does not by itself make a prophet — the Companions saw Jibrīl in the form of the man Diḥya al-Kalbī, and no one calls them prophets. On this side's reading, 5:75's ṣiddīqa is read as her stated station: the highest rank below prophethood, named precisely to settle where she stands.

So the rulings face each other — al-Qurṭubī closing for prophetess (and claiming, in his own words, that the greater number are with him), al-Rāzī closing against — and this course reports the facing rulings and leaves them facing. And notice, before you feel any anxiety on her behalf, what both sides are agreeing on while they argue. Both agree an angel of God addressed her by name. Both agree a message from Heaven reached her. Both agree the Quran certifies her as ṣiddīqa and holds her up as the example for all believers, men and women. They are debating her title. Her standing was never the dispute.

"Above the women of the worlds" — how far?

One more named difference, because Module 3 promised it — and here too the rulings face each other. When the angels say she was chosen above the women of the worlds, al-Ṭabarī glosses: above the women of her time — al-Ḥasan and Ibn Jurayj held this — and he anchors it in the Prophet's ﷺ saying that the best of its women was Maryam bint ʿImrān, and the best of its women Khadīja bint Khuwaylid. Al-Qurṭubī rules the other way and names his side: the election is absolute, over all women to the end of time — "and this is the correct view," he writes, "the position of al-Zajjāj and others" — and he then ranks the rest after her. The hadith material sits under both: Aḥmad records from Ibn ʿAbbās the four lines the Prophet ﷺ drew — the best women of Paradise: Khadīja, Fāṭima, Maryam, Āsiya — and al-Qurṭubī carries another route from Ibn ʿAbbās, which he grades sound, that does rank: the mistress of the women of the worlds is Maryam, then Fāṭima, then Khadīja, then Āsiya. A real scope-question with rulings on both sides, and this course reports it rather than settling it — noting only that on the narrowest reading in the tradition, she is still the woman the Quran alone names, alone gives a sūrah, and alone holds up, in her final verse, beside no one but Pharaoh's believing wife.
