Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You

By now you know this module's method: in this book, the withheld detail is instruction, and the recorded disagreement is taught with the scholars' names attached. Maryam's story carries the Quran's most famous outside objection — answered, it turns out, by the Prophet ﷺ himself — and one genuinely open question about what she was. Both are here. The silences first.

What is not there

There is no husband in this story. Not a minor one — none. The figure other traditions place beside her — the carpenter, the betrothal — has no existence anywhere in the Quran. She conceives alone, labours alone, returns alone, and answers no man. The absence is total, and it is load-bearing: her story is constructed so that the only protector, provider, and defender in it is God. Every human support a woman in her situation would lean on has been deliberately written out, so that what carries her is visible.

Her age is never given, and neither is the place's name. An eastern place; a remote place; a palm. The scene is stripped the way every course on this shelf has now watched scenes be stripped — down to the portable diagram.

The slander is refuted without being repeated. Sūrah 4 condemns those who spoke against Maryam a tremendous calumny — buhtānan ʿaẓīmā (4:156) — and that is all. The accusation itself is never quoted, in a book that quotes its enemies constantly. The Quran defends her honour the way honour is actually defended: the charge is named as monstrous and denied the dignity of repetition. Fourteen centuries of readers know she was slandered and cannot learn the slander's wording from the only book that mattered.

"O sister of Hārūn" — the question the Prophet ﷺ was asked

When she carries the child to her people, their accusation opens with an address that has launched a thousand polemics:

يَـٰٓأُخْتَ هَـٰرُونَ مَا كَانَ أَبُوكِ ٱمْرَأَ سَوْءٍ وَمَا كَانَتْ أُمُّكِ بَغِيًّا

"O sister of Hārūn, your father was not a man of evil, nor was your mother unchaste."

Maryam (19:28)

Sister of Hārūn? The objection writes itself, and people have been writing it for centuries: Hārūn — Aaron — lived with Mūsā, centuries before Maryam; has the Quran confused Maryam with Miriam, Aaron's sister?

Here is what makes this the easiest hard question in the course: it was asked while the Prophet ﷺ was alive, and he answered it. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records it from al-Mughīra ibn Shuʿba: when he went to Najrān, the Christians there put exactly this objection to him — you recite "O sister of Hārūn," and Mūsā was before ʿĪsā by such-and-such. He brought the question back to the Messenger of God ﷺ, who said: "They used to name people after their prophets and the righteous who came before them." That is the answer, from the mouth the Quran came through: the address is a naming-convention, not a genealogy — the way a man of the Prophet's ﷺ own time could be called Abū Ibrāhīm without anyone checking the centuries.

The commentators fill in the ways the convention could be working, with names on each: ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥa and al-Suddī read it as lineage-address — she was of Hārūn's descendants, and "sister of Hārūn" works like "brother of Tamīm" said to a Tamīmī; Qatāda, per al-Baghawī, held it referred to a righteous man of her own people named Hārūn; Ibn Kathīr renders the sense "O you who resembled Hārūn in worship." Al-Qurṭubī opens his treatment with the people differed — and any of the readings dissolves the objection. What the crowd meant, on every reading, is what their next clause says: you come from devout stock — how could you, of all people, have done this? The address that polemicists hold up as the Quran's error is, inside the scene, the crowd's tribute to her family's piety — sharpening their accusation, and setting up the infant's answer.

Was she a prophetess?

Now the genuinely open question. An angel brought her the word of God directly. Sūrah 66 says she believed the words of her Lord; Sūrah 3 has angels addressing her by name with election and command. So the scholars asked, in so many words: was Maryam a nabiyya — a prophetess?

One side says yes, and it is not a fringe. Al-Qurṭubī rules it outright — "the correct position is that Maryam is a nabiyya" — because God conveyed revelation to her by means of the angel, with address, obligation, and glad tidings, as He did to the prophets. Ibn ʿĀshūr draws the same line with a distinction: prophethood (nubūʾa) can belong to women, he writes, though messengership (risāla) does not.

The other side says no, with its own strong argument. Seeing and hearing an angel does not by itself make a prophet — the Companions saw Jibrīl in the form of the man Diḥya al-Kalbī, asking the famous questions about islām and īmān, and no one calls them prophets. The Quran says of the messengers, We sent not before you except men — and on this side's reading, 5:75's ṣiddīqa is not merely a compliment but her stated station: the highest rank below prophethood, named precisely to settle where she stands.

This course leaves the question where the scholarship leaves it: live, with weighty names on both sides and the arguments recorded. And notice — before you feel any anxiety on her behalf — what both sides are agreeing on while they argue. Both agree an angel of God addressed her by name. Both agree revelation, in the plain sense of a message from Heaven, reached her. Both agree the Quran certifies her as ṣiddīqa and holds her up as the example for all believers, men and women. They are debating her title. Her standing was never the dispute.

"Above the women of the worlds" — how far?

One more named difference, because Module 3 promised it. When the angels say she was chosen above the women of the worlds, al-Ṭabarī glosses: above the women of her time — and he anchors it in the Prophet's ﷺ saying that the best of its women was Maryam bint ʿImrān, and the best of its women Khadīja bint Khuwaylid. Others read the election absolutely. And the four-women hadith recorded by Aḥmad from Ibn ʿAbbās names the best women without ranking the matter further: Maryam, Āsiya, Khadīja, Fāṭima. A real scope-question, held by named readers, and this course reports it rather than settling it — noting only that on the narrowest reading in the tradition, she is still the woman the Quran alone names, alone gives a sūrah, and alone holds up, in her final verse, beside no one but Pharaoh's believing wife.

One module left. It belongs to the part of your life no one believes.
