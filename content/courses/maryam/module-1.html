Module 1 — The Vow and the Girl

This course begins a generation before its subject — with a pregnant woman making a promise — because the Quran does, and because what happens to that promise is the first lesson of Maryam's whole story: God accepts what people did not think to offer.

A vow shaped for a boy

The wife of ʿImrān vows the child in her womb to God — muḥarraran, consecrated, freed of every claim except His service. It is a staggering thing for a parent to say, and it carried an assumption she never states: temple dedication was the work of sons. The vow was shaped, in every practical sense, for a boy.

Then the birth:

فَلَمَّا وَضَعَتْهَا قَالَتْ رَبِّ إِنِّى وَضَعْتُهَآ أُنثَىٰ وَٱللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا وَضَعَتْ وَلَيْسَ ٱلذَّكَرُ كَٱلْأُنثَىٰ ۖ وَإِنِّى سَمَّيْتُهَا مَرْيَمَ وَإِنِّىٓ أُعِيذُهَا بِكَ وَذُرِّيَّتَهَا مِنَ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنِ ٱلرَّجِيمِ

"Then when she delivered her, she said: My Lord, I have delivered a female. And God was most knowing of what she delivered. And the male is not like the female. And I have named her Maryam, and I seek refuge for her in You, and for her descendants, from the accursed Satan."

Āl ʿImrān (3:36)

Read the verse's middle carefully, because two voices are interleaved there. The mother speaks — I have delivered a female — with what the commentators hear as apology in it: the vow, she fears, has misfired. Then comes and God was most knowing of what she delivered — and al-Qurṭubī makes the grammatical case that this clause is God's own parenthesis stitched into her sentence: had it still been her speaking to God, the wording would have run "and You know best"; a transmitted reading from Ibn ʿAbbās points the same way. How far the parenthesis extends is itself discussed: al-Zamakhsharī and al-Ālūsī carry the male is not like the female inside God's aside too, while al-Qurṭubī reads that clause as the righteous mother's own words — and a canonical recitation (Abū Bakr's from ʿĀṣim, and Ibn ʿĀmir's: waḍaʿtu, "what I delivered") keeps the whole span as her speech. And the clause's sense runs in two directions with names on each — built, remarkably, on the same unpacking: the male [asked for] is not like the female [given]. Al-Jalālayn takes it as the realism of the vow — the male is the one temple service sought. Al-Zamakhsharī takes the identical construction the other way: the clause exalts what was delivered — the male you asked for does not measure up to the female you were given. The course does not need to pick: what no reading disputes is what happens next.

Accepted — beautifully

God takes the girl. Fa-taqabbalahā rabbuhā bi-qabūlin ḥasan — He accepted her with a beautiful acceptance, and grew her with a beautiful growth. The vow that was shaped for a boy is not downgraded or renegotiated; the female dedicate is received into the temple as if the vow had always meant her — because, the parenthesis has just told us, from where God sits it always did. The mother thought she was explaining a disappointment. She was delivering the most chosen woman in the book.

If your usefulness to God has ever been measured — by you or by anyone — against a template you do not fit, this verse pair was written over that measurement. The template belonged to the people. The acceptance belongs to God, and it is ḥasan.

The room where provision appears

She grows up consecrated, under the guardianship of the prophet Zakariyyā, in the miḥrāb — and the Quran gives us one recurring scene from those years: her guardian keeps finding food in her room that nobody brought. He asks. And the first words the Quran ever records from Maryam are these:

فَتَقَبَّلَهَا رَبُّهَا بِقَبُولٍ حَسَنٍ وَأَنۢبَتَهَا نَبَاتًا حَسَنًا وَكَفَّلَهَا زَكَرِيَّا ۖ كُلَّمَا دَخَلَ عَلَيْهَا زَكَرِيَّا ٱلْمِحْرَابَ وَجَدَ عِندَهَا رِزْقًا ۖ قَالَ يَـٰمَرْيَمُ أَنَّىٰ لَكِ هَـٰذَا ۖ قَالَتْ هُوَ مِنْ عِندِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَرْزُقُ مَن يَشَآءُ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ

"So her Lord accepted her with good acceptance and caused her to grow in a good manner, and put her in the care of Zakariyyā. Every time Zakariyyā entered upon her in the prayer chamber, he found with her provision. He said: O Maryam, from where is this for you? She said: It is from God. Indeed, God provides for whom He wills without account."

Āl ʿImrān (3:37)

Huwa min ʿindi llāh — it is from God. God provides for whom He wills without account. Notice what kind of sentence this is: not surprise, not explanation, not embarrassment at being asked. It is settled doctrine, stated calmly, by a girl, to a prophet — about the very thing prophets teach. The room she was raised in ran on a rule the rest of us spend a lifetime half-believing, and she states it the way you state the weather. Bi-ghayri ḥisāb — without account, without arithmetic, without a supply line anyone can audit.

Right there — a prophet's prayer changes

Now the hinge, and it is one word:

هُنَالِكَ دَعَا زَكَرِيَّا رَبَّهُۥ ۖ قَالَ رَبِّ هَبْ لِى مِن لَّدُنكَ ذُرِّيَّةً طَيِّبَةً ۖ إِنَّكَ سَمِيعُ ٱلدُّعَآءِ

"Right there did Zakariyyā call upon his Lord. He said: My Lord, grant me from Yourself a good offspring. Indeed, You are the Hearer of supplication."

Āl ʿImrān (3:38)

Hunālika — right there, in that place, at that moment. Zakariyyā is an old man with a barren wife, and the next sūrah-scenes tell us how completely he had filed that door as closed. And standing in a room where provision arrives outside every account, listening to a girl say God provides without reckoning, something reopens in him. Right there — not later, not elsewhere — he prays for the impossible child. And he gets Yaḥyā.

Sit with what the Quran has just shown you, because it is this module's quiet thesis: the girl who was almost an apology becomes the reason a prophet dares to ask again. Before Maryam carries the Word, before angels address her, her mere manner of trusting God re-teaches trust to her own teacher. That is what was growing, nabātan ḥasanan, in the miḥrāb — and the next module walks into it on the day everything is asked of her at once.
