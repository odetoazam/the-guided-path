Module 1 — The Vow and the Girl

This course begins a generation before its subject, with a pregnant woman making a promise, because that is where the Quran begins it. What happens to that promise is the first lesson of Maryam's whole story: God accepts what people did not think to offer.

A vow shaped for a boy

The wife of ʿImrān vows the child in her womb to God. The word is muḥarraran, consecrated, freed of every claim except His service. It is a large thing for a parent to say, and it carried an assumption she never states. Temple dedication was the work of sons, so in every practical sense the vow was shaped for a boy.

Then comes the birth.

فَلَمَّا وَضَعَتْهَا قَالَتْ رَبِّ إِنِّى وَضَعْتُهَآ أُنثَىٰ وَٱللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا وَضَعَتْ وَلَيْسَ ٱلذَّكَرُ كَٱلْأُنثَىٰ ۖ وَإِنِّى سَمَّيْتُهَا مَرْيَمَ وَإِنِّىٓ أُعِيذُهَا بِكَ وَذُرِّيَّتَهَا مِنَ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنِ ٱلرَّجِيمِ

"Then when she delivered her, she said: My Lord, I have delivered a female. And God was most knowing of what she delivered. And the male is not like the female. And I have named her Maryam, and I seek refuge for her in You, and for her descendants, from the accursed Satan."

Āl ʿImrān (3:36)

The middle of this verse holds two voices, so it is worth going slowly. The mother speaks first: I have delivered a female. The early scholars hear apology in it, because she fears the vow has misfired. Then comes the clause and God was most knowing of what she delivered. Al-Qurṭubī argues from the grammar that this clause is God's own aside, stitched into her sentence. Had she still been the one speaking to God, the wording would have run "and You know best", and a transmitted reading from Ibn ʿAbbās points the same way.

How far that aside runs is itself discussed. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Ālūsī carry the next clause, the male is not like the female, inside God's aside as well. Al-Qurṭubī reads that clause as the righteous mother's own words. A canonical recitation keeps the whole span as her speech: Abū Bakr's reading from ʿĀṣim, and Ibn ʿĀmir's, give waḍaʿtu, "what I delivered".

The sense of the clause also runs in two directions, with names on each side, and both are built on the same unpacking: the male [asked for] is not like the female [given]. Al-Jalālayn takes it as the realism of the vow, since the male is the one temple service sought. Al-Zamakhsharī takes the same construction the other way, so that the clause raises what was delivered: the male you asked for does not measure up to the female you were given. The course does not need to choose between them, because no reading disputes what happens next.

Accepted, beautifully

God takes the girl. Fa-taqabbalahā rabbuhā bi-qabūlin ḥasan: He accepted her with a beautiful acceptance, and grew her with a beautiful growth. The vow was shaped for a boy, and the female dedicate is still received into the temple as though the vow had always meant her. From where God sits, the aside has just told us, it always did. The mother thought she was explaining a disappointment. She was delivering the most chosen woman in the book.

If your usefulness to God has ever been measured against a template you do not fit, by you or by anyone else, this pair of verses was written over that measurement. The template belonged to the people. The acceptance belongs to God, and it is ḥasan.

The room where provision appears

She grows up consecrated in the miḥrāb, under the care of the prophet Zakariyyā. The Quran gives one recurring scene from those years. Her guardian keeps finding food in her room that nobody brought, and he asks about it. Her answer is the first thing the Quran records from Maryam.

فَتَقَبَّلَهَا رَبُّهَا بِقَبُولٍ حَسَنٍ وَأَنۢبَتَهَا نَبَاتًا حَسَنًا وَكَفَّلَهَا زَكَرِيَّا ۖ كُلَّمَا دَخَلَ عَلَيْهَا زَكَرِيَّا ٱلْمِحْرَابَ وَجَدَ عِندَهَا رِزْقًا ۖ قَالَ يَـٰمَرْيَمُ أَنَّىٰ لَكِ هَـٰذَا ۖ قَالَتْ هُوَ مِنْ عِندِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَرْزُقُ مَن يَشَآءُ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ

"So her Lord accepted her with good acceptance and caused her to grow in a good manner, and put her in the care of Zakariyyā. Every time Zakariyyā entered upon her in the prayer chamber, he found with her provision. He said: O Maryam, from where is this for you? She said: It is from God. Indeed, God provides for whom He wills without account."

Āl ʿImrān (3:37)

Huwa min ʿindi llāh: it is from God. God provides for whom He wills without account. That is settled doctrine, stated calmly, by a girl, to a prophet, about the very thing prophets teach. The room she was raised in ran on a rule the rest of us spend a lifetime half-believing, and she states it the way you would state the weather. Bi-ghayri ḥisāb means without account: no arithmetic, and no supply line anyone can audit.

Right there, a prophet's prayer changes

The hinge of the passage is one word.

هُنَالِكَ دَعَا زَكَرِيَّا رَبَّهُۥ ۖ قَالَ رَبِّ هَبْ لِى مِن لَّدُنكَ ذُرِّيَّةً طَيِّبَةً ۖ إِنَّكَ سَمِيعُ ٱلدُّعَآءِ

"Right there did Zakariyyā call upon his Lord. He said: My Lord, grant me from Yourself a good offspring. Indeed, You are the Hearer of supplication."

Āl ʿImrān (3:38)

Hunālika means right there, in that place, at that moment. Zakariyyā is an old man with a barren wife, and the scenes in later sūrahs tell us how completely he had filed that door as closed. Then he stands in a room where provision arrives outside every account, and he hears a girl say that God provides without reckoning. Something reopens in him. Right there, and not later or elsewhere, he prays for the impossible child, and he is given Yaḥyā.

That is this module's quiet thesis. The girl who was almost an apology becomes the reason a prophet dares to ask again. Before Maryam carries the Word, and before angels speak to her, the way she trusts God teaches trust back to her own teacher. That is what was growing in the miḥrāb, nabātan ḥasanan. The next module walks into it on the day everything is asked of her at once.
