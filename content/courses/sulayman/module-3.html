Module 3 — The Queen and the Question

This is the centre of the course. Everything before it was preparation and everything after it is consequence. It is worth saying at the start what is unusual about the passage we are entering. For around fifteen verses, the Quran hands its story to a woman who is, at the time, worshipping the sun, and lets her reason out loud, at length, on the page. It quotes her thinking and it quotes her strategy. It gives her the last word of the whole sequence. Somewhere in the middle of her story, at the moment of his greatest triumph, Sulaymān says the sentence this course is named after. By the end of this module you will see why the two things had to happen in the same scene.

One note first. The Quran never gives her a name. The tradition calls her Bilqīs, and we will use it once or twice for convenience, but you should know it is not in the text. The Quran is interested in her mind rather than her identity.

A letter, and a woman who reads it carefully

قَالَتْ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْمَلَؤُا۟ إِنِّىٓ أُلْقِىَ إِلَىَّ كِتَـٰبٌ كَرِيمٌ

"She said: O eminent ones, indeed a noble letter has been delivered to me."

An-Naml (27:29)

Her first recorded word is to her court, and her first judgement is about the quality of the letter, before she has told anyone what it says. She calls it karīm, which means noble, honourable, generous. That word comes back at the centre of this module, in the mouth of someone else, about Someone else.

Then she reads it out:

إِنَّهُۥ مِن سُلَيْمَـٰنَ وَإِنَّهُۥ بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ

أَلَّا تَعْلُوا۟ عَلَىَّ وَأْتُونِى مُسْلِمِينَ

"It is from Sulaymān, and it is: in the name of God, the Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful. Do not rise up against me, and come to me in submission."

An-Naml (27:30–31)

That is the whole letter. From the most powerful ruler alive, to a foreign monarch, on a matter of war and peace: two lines. No titles. He does not even call himself king. Just from Sulaymān, and then he gets out of the way, and the second thing in the letter is the name of God.

Al-Qurṭubī makes a precise point about that order. Sulaymān's name comes before the bismillāh not because he is putting himself first, but because that is simply how a letter is addressed. The sender's name is the envelope rather than the message. Which leaves the opening line of the message itself to be the mercy of God. He used the convention to get himself out of the doorway.

Then comes a sentence that deserves a hard look. Do not rise up against me. Against him, personally, not against God. He puts his own name in the position where you would expect God's. He is telling a pagan queen that if she is going to resist something here, she should resist him. He is the thing in her way, and he is standing in the road on purpose.

That is what it looks like when someone carrying a message refuses to let the message take the damage. Any friction in this exchange is going to hit him rather than the thing he is carrying.

A queen who will not decide alone

Her response is the part of this story people skip, and it is one of the finest portraits of leadership in the Quran.

قَالَتْ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْمَلَؤُا۟ أَفْتُونِى فِىٓ أَمْرِى مَا كُنتُ قَاطِعَةً أَمْرًا حَتَّىٰ تَشْهَدُونِ

"She said: O eminent ones, give me a ruling in my affair. I have never decided a matter until you were present with me."

An-Naml (27:32)

Two things. The word she uses for "give me a ruling" comes from the same root as fatwā, so she is not asking for opinions or a show of hands. She is asking for a considered judgement. And the second half is autobiography: I have never decided a matter until you were present with me. That describes a lifelong habit of governing rather than a reaction to one crisis.

Which is why what happens next actually works:

قَالُوا۟ نَحْنُ أُو۟لُوا۟ قُوَّةٍ وَأُو۟لُوا۟ بَأْسٍ شَدِيدٍ وَٱلْأَمْرُ إِلَيْكِ فَٱنظُرِى مَاذَا تَأْمُرِينَ

"They said: we are possessors of strength and possessors of great might — but the command is yours, so consider what you will command."

An-Naml (27:33)

Read that carefully, because it is very often misreported. Her generals do not advise war. They tell her what the nation has, which is real strength and serious military capacity, and then they hand the decision straight back to her. They are answering the question she asked, which was for a judgement rather than a mood. Nowhere do they say let us fight.

That is a court with genuinely good habits, honest about capability and disciplined about whose decision it is.

The sentence about kings

Then she gives her ruling, and it is one of the sharpest political observations in the Quran:

قَالَتْ إِنَّ ٱلْمُلُوكَ إِذَا دَخَلُوا۟ قَرْيَةً أَفْسَدُوهَا وَجَعَلُوٓا۟ أَعِزَّةَ أَهْلِهَآ أَذِلَّةً ۖ وَكَذَٰلِكَ يَفْعَلُونَ

"She said: indeed kings, when they enter a city, ruin it and render the honoured of its people humbled — and that is what they do."

An-Naml (27:34)

Notice that she does not say Sulaymān will ruin us. She states a general law about kings as a class, and refuses to name him. She is being careful with a man she has never met, and declines to accuse a specific person of something she only knows about the type.

Her analysis is precise as well. The verb is not destroy or burn. It is the word for corruption, meaning something whose inner order has been broken while the thing itself still stands. The city survives conquest. What dies is what held it together. Then she names the mechanism exactly: they render the honoured of its people humbled. That is an inversion rather than a massacre. You take the people a society respects and you put them at the bottom, and the society is still there, and it is finished.

That is a ruler who has thought hard about what actually happens to a conquered place.

The gift is an experiment

So she does something clever:

وَإِنِّى مُرْسِلَةٌ إِلَيْهِم بِهَدِيَّةٍ فَنَاظِرَةٌۢ بِمَ يَرْجِعُ ٱلْمُرْسَلُونَ

"And indeed I am going to send them a gift, and see with what the messengers return."

An-Naml (27:35)

She commits to neither surrender nor war. She runs a test, and she says plainly what the test is for: I will see with what the messengers return. If the gift buys him, she is dealing with a king, and she knows exactly what kings do. If it does not, she is dealing with something she has no category for yet, and she will need to think again.

The Quran does not tell us what the gift was. Later storytellers filled that gap with a great deal of colourful detail, and we are going to leave it empty, because the emptiness is doing work. If the contents were named, your attention would go to the objects. The verse keeps it on the act.

Here is what comes back:

فَلَمَّا جَآءَ سُلَيْمَـٰنَ قَالَ أَتُمِدُّونَنِ بِمَالٍ فَمَآ ءَاتَىٰنِۦَ ٱللَّهُ خَيْرٌ مِّمَّآ ءَاتَىٰكُم بَلْ أَنتُم بِهَدِيَّتِكُمْ تَفْرَحُونَ

"So when they came to Sulaymān he said: do you offer me wealth? But what God has given me is better than what He has given you. Rather, it is you who rejoice in your gift."

An-Naml (27:36)

He refuses to use her word. She said gift, and he says something closer to supply, the word for reinforcements sent to an army that is running short. That reframing is the whole rebuke. She has decided he is lacking something.

The line that matters most is the middle one, and it is easy to read past. What God has given me is better than what He has given you. Same verb, same Giver, in both halves.

Stop there. He has just credited a sun-worshipping kingdom's wealth to God. The claim is larger than saying his God gave him his and her idols gave her hers. His point is that the God who gave him his kingdom is the same one who gave her hers. Her gold is His. The gift she is trying to buy him with came from the Person he is trying to introduce her to.

Then comes the diagnosis: it is you who rejoice in your gift. The Arabic verb there is one the Quran uses again and again for a very specific failure, which is delight in a possession that has been mistaken for an achievement. It is the exact verb used of Qārūn, the man in Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ who said his wealth came from his own knowledge.

So this is a precise observation about her rather than a lecture about money. She is pleased with what she has in the way people are pleased with what they made. That is the disease, and the gift is only the symptom.

Two offers, and the difference between them

Now the scene shifts to his court. Knowing she is coming, he asks which of them can bring him her throne. The Quran gives us two volunteers, and lets you hear the difference between them.

قَالَ عِفْرِيتٌ مِّنَ ٱلْجِنِّ أَنَا۠ ءَاتِيكَ بِهِۦ قَبْلَ أَن تَقُومَ مِن مَّقَامِكَ ۖ وَإِنِّى عَلَيْهِ لَقَوِىٌّ أَمِينٌ

"A powerful one from among the jinn said: I will bring it to you before you rise from your place — and indeed I am, for this, strong and trustworthy."

An-Naml (27:39)

A serious offer. Look at the last clause: I am, for this, strong and trustworthy. He stamps his own credentials, with heavy emphasis, in his own mouth.

Here is a detail worth carrying with you. That exact pair of words, the strong, the trustworthy, appears one other notable place in the Quran. In Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ a young woman of Madyan says to her father about Mūsā that the best one you can hire is the strong and trustworthy. Same two qualities, in a completely different situation. There they are testimony given by someone else. Here they are advertising, given by the man himself.

Now the second volunteer:

قَالَ ٱلَّذِى عِندَهُۥ عِلْمٌ مِّنَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ أَنَا۠ ءَاتِيكَ بِهِۦ قَبْلَ أَن يَرْتَدَّ إِلَيْكَ طَرْفُكَ ۚ فَلَمَّا رَءَاهُ مُسْتَقِرًّا عِندَهُۥ قَالَ هَـٰذَا مِن فَضْلِ رَبِّى لِيَبْلُوَنِىٓ ءَأَشْكُرُ أَمْ أَكْفُرُ ۖ وَمَن شَكَرَ فَإِنَّمَا يَشْكُرُ لِنَفْسِهِۦ ۖ وَمَن كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ رَبِّى غَنِىٌّ كَرِيمٌ

"Said one who had knowledge of the Book: I will bring it to you before your glance returns to you. So when he saw it settled before him, he said: this is from the favour of my Lord, to test me — whether I am grateful or ungrateful. And whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself; and whoever is ungrateful — then indeed my Lord is Free of need, Generous."

An-Naml (27:40)

Both volunteers open with the identical words: I will bring it to you. The jinn then adds a paragraph about himself. The second man adds nothing at all. He states the timeframe and stops, and his timeframe is impossible. Before your glance returns to you, faster than the time it takes your eye to travel out and come back.

Who is he? The Quran says only one who had knowledge of the Book. No name, no rank, not even which book. And note the wording, knowledge of the Book, some of it, a portion. Various names circulate in the commentary tradition. We are not going to supply one, because the anonymity is clearly deliberate. The verse wants the contrast to be about what he had rather than who he was. Raw power announced itself and offered hours. A portion of scriptural knowledge said nothing about itself and beat it by a distance that cannot be measured.

The sentence at the summit

Now the moment the course exists for.

The throne is there. The Quran does not describe it arriving. There is no transit, no flash, no reaction from the court. Sulaymān simply sees it settled before him, as though it had always been in the room. The miracle is skipped entirely. What the verse cares about is the next thing out of his mouth.

He is at the peak of his life. Another sovereign's throne is standing in his hall, delivered across a desert faster than sight. Every king in history has a script for this moment. Here is his:

This is from the favour of my Lord, to test me, whether I am grateful or ungrateful.

Four things are happening in that sentence, and each of them is a decision.

He calls it a favour rather than an achievement. The word is faḍl, which means overflow, surplus, what is given beyond what anyone was owed. He does not say this is what he built, or even what he earned. It is something that spilled over onto him.

He calls a triumph a test. This is the rarest move in the passage and the reason this life is worth a course. We are all fluent in calling hardship a test: the illness, the loss, the closed door. Almost nobody looks at a moment of maximum success and identifies it as an examination in progress. And most people in the Quran are told they are being tested. He works it out himself, while it is happening, with the trophy in the room.

Why would a gift be a harder test than a loss? Because a loss sits you down. Grief bends you toward God without requiring any insight on your part. A gift leaves you standing. It puts you up high, and while you are up there it asks, very quietly, whether you remember where the ground is. A hardship announces itself as a spiritual situation. An abundance looks like ordinary good fortune.

He names both outcomes, including the bad one. Whether I am grateful or ungrateful. Two doors, and the Arabic offers no third. There is no option of simply not thinking about it, no neutral, no drifting. Which means something uncomfortable and clarifying. Taking a thing for granted is not a middle position. In the grammar of this verse it falls on the far side. The word he uses for the bad branch is from the same root the Quran uses for disbelief, because at bottom both are the same act of failing to recognise where something came from.

And he says it out loud, in front of his court. This is a public admission, at his highest moment, that he might fail.

One more thing worth knowing, and you can check it yourself. In this single verse the root for gratitude fires three times, and its opposite twice. Nowhere else in his story does the word cluster like that. The Quran is pointing.

The sentence that does not come

Now the finest thing in the verse, which is a thing that is not there.

Read the last two clauses and listen for the shape. Whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself; and whoever is ungrateful...

Your ear is now waiting for the matching half. ...and whoever is ungrateful, it is only against himself. That is the sentence the symmetry demands. Every instinct in the language expects it.

It does not come. Instead, exactly where the punishment should sit, God puts a description of Himself:

...then indeed my Lord is Free of need, Generous.

Where you expected a verdict, you get a self-portrait. That substitution is the theology of the whole course. God's answer to ingratitude is not, in the first instance, a threat. It is information about who He is.

Look at the order of the two names, because it is doing real work. Free of need comes first and Generous comes second. If it ran the other way round, generous and then free of need, you might read the generosity as a need being met, a giver who gives because giving does something for Him. The order rules that out. He is independent first, and the generosity comes out of a fullness that was never lacking anything.

Which yields the two halves of the hardest and most freeing fact in this course. Your ingratitude takes nothing from Him, because He is Free of need. And it does not stop His giving, because He is Generous. The sun rises on the person who thanks Him and the person who never once thought about it. The rain falls on both fields.

Now put that beside the other clause, the one that is there: whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself. The word "only" in the Arabic is a particle of restriction, meaning exclusively and nothing else. Your gratitude does not benefit God. Not a little. Not at all.

So the whole transaction runs in one direction. He gives regardless, and the thanks are entirely for your benefit. That is what makes gratitude a real test rather than a payment. If God needed it, thanking Him would be a transaction and you would be settling a debt. Because He does not, the only thing your gratitude changes is you. Which is why He can leave the choice genuinely open. Nothing of His is riding on it.

Here is a small thing you now have the pieces for. This sequence opened with a queen who did not yet believe in God, holding a letter, reaching for the highest word she had: karīm. Noble. Generous. Fifteen verses later, at the theological centre of the passage, that same word turns up as a name, al-Karīm, and it belongs to God. She reached for the right word before she knew Who it described.

Her ending

He has her throne disguised, and the reason is stated in the verse: let us see whether she will be guided. Two details there. He says the disguise is for her rather than against her, and the verb is not recognise or know but the verb of guidance. This is a test whose stated purpose is that she find her way.

Her answer, when she is asked if the throne is hers, is exact: it is as though it were it. Neither a claim nor a denial. She has been shown something impossible and she declines both to overstate and to dismiss. That is what an honest mind does at the edge of what it can explain.

Then the floor:

قِيلَ لَهَا ٱدْخُلِى ٱلصَّرْحَ ۖ فَلَمَّا رَأَتْهُ حَسِبَتْهُ لُجَّةً وَكَشَفَتْ عَن سَاقَيْهَا ۚ قَالَ إِنَّهُۥ صَرْحٌ مُّمَرَّدٌ مِّن قَوَارِيرَ ۗ قَالَتْ رَبِّ إِنِّى ظَلَمْتُ نَفْسِى وَأَسْلَمْتُ مَعَ سُلَيْمَـٰنَ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

"It was said to her: enter the palace. Then when she saw it she took it for a body of water and uncovered her shins. He said: it is a palace paved smooth with glass. She said: my Lord, I have wronged myself, and I submit, with Sulaymān, to God, Lord of the worlds."

An-Naml (27:44)

The verb for what she did with the floor comes from the root of reckoning, the word for a calculation you have completed and are confident in. She did not wonder whether it was water. She concluded it was, and acted. It is the same verb the Quran uses of a thirsty man in a desert who sees a mirage and is certain he is looking at a lake.

That is the whole conversion, compressed into one small humiliation. It was not an argument, and it was not the miracle either, since she had already seen the throne and it produced only a careful sentence. What finally moved her was discovering that she had been completely certain about something and completely wrong, over a matter as trivial as a floor. If she can be that sure and that mistaken about water, what else has she been sure about?

The order of her final words is exact. First, my Lord, I have wronged myself. Not "I was deceived," not "the floor tricked me," not "your court embarrassed me." She locates the error inside herself, and she does it before she submits. Diagnosis, then surrender. That order is the whole thing.

The very last preposition of the sequence deserves your attention too. I submit, with Sulaymān, to God. Alongside the man, and to God alone. She has walked into the hall of the most powerful person on earth, been outmatched at every turn, and her closing sentence still puts him beside her and God above them both. The only vertical word in it points past him.

That is the end of her story. The Quran says nothing further, not what she did next, not what became of her, not one word about her afterwards. Later tellers added a great deal. We will leave it exactly where the Quran leaves it, because look at what it chose for her final act. Not a marriage, not a treaty, not a submission to a king. A woman working out, on her own, in public, that she had been wrong, and saying so.

Why these two stories are the same story

Now step back and see why the Quran braided these together.

Two rulers, both described with the identical phrase, given of all things. He says it about himself in verse 16, and the hoopoe says it about her in verse 23. Same wording, same abundance.

Then the fork. One of them, at the height of everything, says this is from the favour of my Lord. The other is found bowing to the sun, which is to say giving her thanks to the gift instead of the Giver. That is what sun-worship actually is in this story. It is gratitude aimed one step short rather than stupidity. The sun really does give light and warmth and harvests. She was thanking something real. She just stopped at the thing she could see.

Two people handed the same abundance. One traced it back to its source. One thanked the delivery.

That is the test this course is about, and it is the reason her story and his had to be told together. He shows you what the grateful answer looks like from inside enormous power. She shows you how easy and how reasonable the other answer is, and then she shows you that it can be changed, in a moment, by a person honest enough to say I was wrong.

Which leaves one question standing, and the next module makes it much harder. Sulaymān passed this test in the most public way imaginable. So what happens in the passages where the Quran shows him at less than his best, and then refuses to tell us what happened?
