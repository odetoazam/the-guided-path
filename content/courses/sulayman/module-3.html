Module 3 — The Queen and the Question

This is the centre of the course. Everything before it was preparation, and everything after it follows from it. It is worth saying at the start what makes this passage so unusual. For around fifteen verses, the Quran hands its story to a woman who, at that point, worships the sun, and lets her reason out loud at length. It quotes her thinking and her strategy, and it gives her the last word of the whole sequence. Somewhere in the middle of her story, at the moment of his greatest triumph, Sulaymān says the sentence this course is named after. By the end of this module you will see why those two things had to happen in the same scene.

Before we begin, you should know that the Quran never gives her a name. The tradition calls her Bilqīs, and we will use that name once or twice for convenience, but it is not in the text. The Quran is interested in how she thinks, not in who she was.

A letter, and a woman who reads it carefully

قَالَتْ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْمَلَؤُا۟ إِنِّىٓ أُلْقِىَ إِلَىَّ كِتَـٰبٌ كَرِيمٌ

"She said: O eminent ones, indeed a noble letter has been delivered to me."

An-Naml (27:29)

The first words we hear from her are spoken to her court, and before she has told anyone what the letter says, she gives her opinion of its quality. She calls it karīm, which means noble, honourable or generous. Remember that word, because it comes back at the centre of this module, said by someone else and about Someone else.

Then she reads the letter aloud:

إِنَّهُۥ مِن سُلَيْمَـٰنَ وَإِنَّهُۥ بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ

أَلَّا تَعْلُوا۟ عَلَىَّ وَأْتُونِى مُسْلِمِينَ

"It is from Sulaymān, and it is: in the name of God, the Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful. Do not rise up against me, and come to me in submission."

An-Naml (27:30–31)

That is the whole letter. It comes from the most powerful ruler alive, it is addressed to a foreign queen, it concerns war and peace, and it is two lines long. There are no titles in it, and he does not even call himself king. It simply says from Sulaymān, and then he steps aside, so that the next thing in the letter is the name of God.

Al-Qurṭubī makes a precise point about that order. Sulaymān's name comes before the bismillāh not because he is putting himself first, but because that is simply how a letter is addressed. The sender's name works like the address on an envelope; it is not part of the message. That leaves the mercy of God as the first line of the message itself. Sulaymān used the normal custom of letter-writing to get himself out of the way.

Then comes a sentence that deserves a closer look: do not rise up against me. He says against him personally, not against God, so he puts his own name where you might expect God's. In effect he is telling a queen who worships the sun that if she is going to resist anything here, she should resist him. He deliberately makes himself the obstacle in her path.

This is what it looks like when a messenger refuses to let his message take the damage. If there is going to be any friction in this exchange, he makes sure it falls on him and not on what he is carrying.

A queen who will not decide alone

People often skip her response, but it is one of the finest pictures of leadership in the Quran.

قَالَتْ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْمَلَؤُا۟ أَفْتُونِى فِىٓ أَمْرِى مَا كُنتُ قَاطِعَةً أَمْرًا حَتَّىٰ تَشْهَدُونِ

"She said: O eminent ones, give me a ruling in my affair. I have never decided a matter until you were present with me."

An-Naml (27:32)

Two things stand out. The word she uses for "give me a ruling" comes from the same root as fatwā, so she is not asking for opinions or a show of hands; she wants a carefully considered judgement. And the second half of the verse tells us about her past: I have never decided a matter until you were present with me. This describes how she has always governed, rather than how she reacted to one crisis.

That is why what happens next works so well:

قَالُوا۟ نَحْنُ أُو۟لُوا۟ قُوَّةٍ وَأُو۟لُوا۟ بَأْسٍ شَدِيدٍ وَٱلْأَمْرُ إِلَيْكِ فَٱنظُرِى مَاذَا تَأْمُرِينَ

"They said: we are possessors of strength and possessors of great might — but the command is yours, so consider what you will command."

An-Naml (27:33)

Read that carefully, because it is very often misreported. Her generals do not advise war. They tell her what the nation has, which is real strength and serious military power, and then they hand the decision straight back to her. They answer the question she actually asked, which was for a judgement and not for their feelings, and nowhere do they say let us fight.

This is a court with genuinely good habits. Its members are honest about what they can do, and disciplined about whose decision it is.

The sentence about kings

Then she gives her ruling, and it is one of the sharpest political observations in the Quran:

قَالَتْ إِنَّ ٱلْمُلُوكَ إِذَا دَخَلُوا۟ قَرْيَةً أَفْسَدُوهَا وَجَعَلُوٓا۟ أَعِزَّةَ أَهْلِهَآ أَذِلَّةً ۖ وَكَذَٰلِكَ يَفْعَلُونَ

"She said: indeed kings, when they enter a city, ruin it and render the honoured of its people humbled — and that is what they do."

An-Naml (27:34)

Notice that she does not say Sulaymān will ruin them. She states a general rule about kings as a group and refuses to name him. She is being careful with a man she has never met, and will not accuse a particular person of something she only knows about kings in general.

Her analysis is also precise. The verb she uses does not mean destroy or burn. It is the word for corruption, for something whose inner order has been broken even though the thing itself is still standing. The city survives being conquered, but whatever held it together dies. Then she names exactly how this happens: they render the honoured of its people humbled. Rather than a massacre, she is describing a society turned upside down. You take the people it respects and put them at the bottom, and although the society is still there, it is finished.

These are the words of a ruler who has thought hard about what really happens to a conquered place.

The gift is an experiment

So she does something clever:

وَإِنِّى مُرْسِلَةٌ إِلَيْهِم بِهَدِيَّةٍ فَنَاظِرَةٌۢ بِمَ يَرْجِعُ ٱلْمُرْسَلُونَ

"And indeed I am going to send them a gift, and see with what the messengers return."

An-Naml (27:35)

She does not commit to surrender or to war. Instead she runs a test, and she says plainly what it is for: I will see with what the messengers return. If the gift buys him, she knows she is dealing with a king, and she knows exactly what kings do. If it does not, she is dealing with something she has no name for yet, and she will have to think again.

The Quran does not tell us what the gift was. Later storytellers filled that gap with plenty of colourful detail, but we are going to leave it empty, because the empty space has a purpose. If the verse listed the objects, your attention would go to them, and the verse wants you to look at what she was doing instead.

Here is what comes back:

فَلَمَّا جَآءَ سُلَيْمَـٰنَ قَالَ أَتُمِدُّونَنِ بِمَالٍ فَمَآ ءَاتَىٰنِۦَ ٱللَّهُ خَيْرٌ مِّمَّآ ءَاتَىٰكُم بَلْ أَنتُم بِهَدِيَّتِكُمْ تَفْرَحُونَ

"So when they came to Sulaymān he said: do you offer me wealth? But what God has given me is better than what He has given you. Rather, it is you who rejoice in your gift."

An-Naml (27:36)

He refuses to use her word. She called it a gift, but he uses a word closer to supply, the word for reinforcements sent to an army that is running low. That change of word is the whole rebuke, because it shows she has assumed he is short of something.

The most important line is the middle one, and it is easy to read past: what God has given me is better than what He has given you. Both halves use the same verb, and both name the same Giver.

Stop and think about that. He has just said that the wealth of a kingdom that worships the sun was given by God. He means more than that his God gave him his wealth while her idols gave her hers. His point is that the God who gave him his kingdom is the same God who gave her hers. Her gold belongs to God too, so the gift she is using to buy Sulaymān came from the very One he is trying to introduce her to.

Then he tells her what is really wrong: it is you who rejoice in your gift. The Arabic verb here is one the Quran uses again and again for a particular failing, which is taking delight in something you own as if you had achieved it yourself. It is exactly the verb used about Qārūn, the man in Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ who said his wealth came from his own knowledge.

So rather than lecturing her about money, he makes a precise observation about her. She is pleased with what she has in the way people are pleased with something they made themselves. That attitude is the real illness, and the gift is only a symptom of it.

Two offers, and the difference between them

Now the scene moves to his court. Knowing she is on her way, he asks which of them can bring him her throne. The Quran gives us two volunteers and lets us hear the difference between them.

قَالَ عِفْرِيتٌ مِّنَ ٱلْجِنِّ أَنَا۠ ءَاتِيكَ بِهِۦ قَبْلَ أَن تَقُومَ مِن مَّقَامِكَ ۖ وَإِنِّى عَلَيْهِ لَقَوِىٌّ أَمِينٌ

"A powerful one from among the jinn said: I will bring it to you before you rise from your place — and indeed I am, for this, strong and trustworthy."

An-Naml (27:39)

It is a serious offer, but look at the last part: I am, for this, strong and trustworthy. He vouches for himself, with heavy emphasis, in his own words.

Here is a detail worth remembering. That exact pair of words, strong and trustworthy, appears in one other well-known place in the Quran. In Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ, a young woman of Madyan tells her father that the best man to hire is Mūsā, because he is strong and trustworthy. The two qualities are the same, but the situation is completely different. There, someone else says it about Mūsā. Here, the jinn says it about himself, as if he were advertising.

Now the second volunteer:

قَالَ ٱلَّذِى عِندَهُۥ عِلْمٌ مِّنَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ أَنَا۠ ءَاتِيكَ بِهِۦ قَبْلَ أَن يَرْتَدَّ إِلَيْكَ طَرْفُكَ ۚ فَلَمَّا رَءَاهُ مُسْتَقِرًّا عِندَهُۥ قَالَ هَـٰذَا مِن فَضْلِ رَبِّى لِيَبْلُوَنِىٓ ءَأَشْكُرُ أَمْ أَكْفُرُ ۖ وَمَن شَكَرَ فَإِنَّمَا يَشْكُرُ لِنَفْسِهِۦ ۖ وَمَن كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ رَبِّى غَنِىٌّ كَرِيمٌ

"Said one who had knowledge of the Book: I will bring it to you before your glance returns to you. So when he saw it settled before him, he said: this is from the favour of my Lord, to test me — whether I am grateful or ungrateful. And whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself; and whoever is ungrateful — then indeed my Lord is Free of need, Generous."

An-Naml (27:40)

Both volunteers begin with exactly the same words: I will bring it to you. The jinn goes on to add a paragraph about himself, but the second man adds nothing. He states how quickly he will do it and then stops. What he promises is impossible: before your glance returns to you, which means faster than it takes your eye to look out and come back.

Who is he? The Quran only calls him one who had knowledge of the Book. It gives no name and no rank, and it does not even say which book. Notice, too, that it says knowledge of the Book, meaning some of it, a portion. Various names are passed around in the commentaries, but we will not supply one, because the Quran clearly left him unnamed on purpose. It wants the contrast to be about what he had, not who he was. Raw power praised itself and offered to do the job within hours. A portion of knowledge from scripture said nothing about itself and beat that offer by an amount no one can measure.

The sentence at the summit

Now we reach the moment this course exists for.

The throne is there, but the Quran does not describe it arriving. There is no journey, no flash of light and no reaction from the court. Sulaymān simply sees it settled before him, as though it had always been in the room. The Quran skips the miracle completely, because what it cares about is what he says next.

He is at the high point of his life. Another ruler's throne is standing in his hall, brought across a desert faster than the eye can move. Every king in history knows what he is supposed to say at a moment like this. Here is what Sulaymān says instead:

This is from the favour of my Lord, to test me, whether I am grateful or ungrateful.

Four things are happening in that sentence, and each one is a choice he makes.

First, he calls it a favour rather than an achievement. The word is faḍl, which means an overflow or surplus, something given beyond what anyone was owed. He does not say this is what he built, or even what he earned. He says it is something that spilled over onto him.

Second, he calls his triumph a test. This is the rarest thing he does in the whole passage, and it is the reason his life is worth a course. We all find it natural to call hardship a test, whether it is an illness, a loss or a door that closes on us. Almost nobody looks at a moment of great success and recognises that they are being examined right then. Most people in the Quran have to be told they are being tested, but Sulaymān works it out for himself, while it is happening, with the prize standing in the room.

Why would a gift be a harder test than a loss? Because a loss brings you down. Grief bends you toward God without your having to understand anything. A gift, on the other hand, leaves you standing and lifts you up high, and while you are up there it asks you, very quietly, whether you still remember where the ground is. A hardship feels obviously spiritual, while abundance just looks like ordinary good luck.

Third, he names both possible outcomes, including the bad one. Whether I am grateful or ungrateful. There are two doors, and the Arabic offers no third. There is no option of simply not thinking about it, no neutral ground and no drifting along. That tells us something uncomfortable but clarifying: taking something for granted is not a middle position. In the grammar of this verse it counts as ingratitude. The word he uses for the bad outcome comes from the same root the Quran uses for disbelief, because at bottom both are the same failure to recognise where something came from.

Fourth, he says it out loud in front of his court. At the highest moment of his life, he admits in public that he might fail.

There is one more thing worth knowing, and you can check it yourself. In this single verse, the root for gratitude appears three times and its opposite twice. Nowhere else in his story does the word gather like that, and the Quran is pointing us to it.

The sentence that does not come

The finest thing in the verse is actually something that is missing.

Read the last two clauses and listen to their shape: whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself; and whoever is ungrateful...

By now your ear is waiting for the matching half: ...and whoever is ungrateful, it is only against himself. The balance of the sentence calls for it, and every instinct in the language expects it.

But it never comes. Instead, in exactly the place where the punishment should be, God describes Himself:

...then indeed my Lord is Free of need, Generous.

Where you expected a verdict, God tells you who He is, and that swap contains the theology of this whole course. God's first answer to ingratitude, before any threat, is to show you what He is like.

Look at the order of the two names, because it matters. Free of need comes first and Generous second. If it were the other way round, you might think His generosity met some need of His own, as if He gave because giving did something for Him. This order rules that out. He is free of need first, and His generosity comes from a fullness that has never lacked anything.

That gives us the two halves of the hardest and most freeing fact in this course. Your ingratitude takes nothing away from Him, because He is Free of need. And it does not stop Him from giving, because He is Generous. The sun rises on the person who thanks Him and on the person who has never once thought about it, and the rain falls on both of their fields.

Now put that next to the clause that is there: whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself. The word "only" is a particle of restriction in the Arabic, meaning exclusively and nothing else. Your gratitude does not benefit God at all, not even a little.

So the whole exchange runs in one direction. He gives no matter what, and the thanks are entirely for your own good. That is what makes gratitude a real test rather than a payment. If God needed it, thanking Him would be like settling a debt. Because He does not need it, the only thing your gratitude changes is you, and that is why He can leave the choice truly open. Nothing of His depends on it.

You now have the pieces for one small observation of our own. This sequence began with a queen who did not yet believe in God, holding a letter and reaching for the highest word she knew: karīm, noble and generous. Fifteen verses later, at the heart of the passage, that same word appears as a name, al-Karīm, and it belongs to God. She reached for the right word before she knew Who it described.

Her ending

He has her throne disguised, and the verse tells us why: let us see whether she will be guided. Two details matter here. He says the disguise is meant to help her, not to trap her, and the verb he uses is not "recognise" or "know" but the verb of guidance. The stated purpose of this test is that she should find her way.

When she is asked if the throne is hers, her answer is exact: it is as though it were it. She neither claims it nor denies it. She has been shown something impossible, and she refuses either to overstate it or to brush it aside, which is what an honest mind does when it reaches the edge of what it can explain.

Then comes the floor:

قِيلَ لَهَا ٱدْخُلِى ٱلصَّرْحَ ۖ فَلَمَّا رَأَتْهُ حَسِبَتْهُ لُجَّةً وَكَشَفَتْ عَن سَاقَيْهَا ۚ قَالَ إِنَّهُۥ صَرْحٌ مُّمَرَّدٌ مِّن قَوَارِيرَ ۗ قَالَتْ رَبِّ إِنِّى ظَلَمْتُ نَفْسِى وَأَسْلَمْتُ مَعَ سُلَيْمَـٰنَ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

"It was said to her: enter the palace. Then when she saw it she took it for a body of water and uncovered her shins. He said: it is a palace paved smooth with glass. She said: my Lord, I have wronged myself, and I submit, with Sulaymān, to God, Lord of the worlds."

An-Naml (27:44)

The verb for what she thought about the floor comes from the root for reckoning, the word for a calculation you have finished and feel sure about. She did not wonder whether it was water. She decided it was, and acted on it. It is the same verb the Quran uses for a thirsty man in the desert who sees a mirage and is certain he is looking at a lake.

Her whole conversion is packed into that one small, embarrassing moment. It was not an argument that changed her mind, and it was not the miracle either, since she had already seen the throne and it only drew a careful sentence from her. What finally moved her was finding out that she had been completely sure about something and completely wrong, over something as trivial as a floor. If she could be that sure and that mistaken about water, what else had she been sure about?

The order of her final words is exact. First she says my Lord, I have wronged myself. She does not say she was deceived, or that the floor tricked her, or that his court embarrassed her. She places the mistake inside herself, and she does this before she submits. First she names what went wrong, and then she surrenders, and that order is the heart of it.

The very last preposition deserves your attention too: I submit, with Sulaymān, to God. She stands alongside the man and submits to God alone. She has walked into the hall of the most powerful person on earth and been outmatched at every step, yet her closing sentence still places him beside her and God above them both. The only word in it that points upward points past him.

That is where her story ends. The Quran says nothing more about her, not what she did next, not what became of her, not a single word about her afterwards. Later storytellers added a great deal, but we will leave her exactly where the Quran leaves her, because look at what it chose as her final act. Instead of a marriage, a treaty or a surrender to a king, it shows a woman realising on her own, in public, that she had been wrong, and saying so.

Why these two stories are the same story

Now step back and see why the Quran weaves these two stories together.

Both rulers are described with the same phrase, given of all things. He says it about himself in verse 16, and the hoopoe says it about her in verse 23, so they share the same wording and the same abundance.

Then their paths split. At the height of everything, he says this is from the favour of my Lord, while she is found bowing to the sun, which means she is thanking the gift instead of the Giver. That is what sun-worship really is in this story. It is gratitude that stops one step too early, and there is nothing stupid about it. The sun really does give light, warmth and harvests, so she was thanking something real. She just stopped at the thing she could see.

Both of them were handed the same abundance, but he traced it back to where it came from, while she thanked the thing that delivered it.

That is the test this course is about, and it is why her story and his had to be told together. He shows you what the grateful answer looks like from inside enormous power. She shows you how easy and reasonable the other answer is, and then she shows you that it can change in a moment, for anyone honest enough to say I was wrong.

That leaves one question open, and the next module makes it much harder. Sulaymān passed this test in the most public way imaginable. So what do we do with the passages where the Quran shows him at less than his best, and then refuses to tell us what happened?
