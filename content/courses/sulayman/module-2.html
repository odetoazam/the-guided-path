Module 2 — The Ant and the Hoopoe

Module 1 left you with a question. What is left to test a man who has been given everything? The Quran answers it in the two scenes we are about to look at closely. It does not test him with a war or a rival or a loss. It tests him with an insect and a bird. Both scenes are about the same thing. They show what happens to a man with enormous power when something very small turns out to be right.

An army held in rank

First, look at what he is standing in the middle of.

وَحُشِرَ لِسُلَيْمَـٰنَ جُنُودُهُۥ مِنَ ٱلْجِنِّ وَٱلْإِنسِ وَٱلطَّيْرِ فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ

"And gathered for Sulaymān were his forces of jinn and men and birds, and they were held in ranks."

An-Naml (27:17)

Three kinds of creature are marching under one command, and the last two words of the verse are where this module begins. The Arabic word is yūzaʿūn. It does not simply mean arranged or lined up. It comes from the root w-z-ʿ, which the classical Arabic dictionaries define as holding back. More exactly, it means keeping the front of the line from running ahead of the rest. It is the word for an officer whose job is not to push the army forward. His job is to stop the eager ones from breaking formation.

Think about what that means. An army that size does not fall apart because it lacks energy. It falls apart because everyone in it wants to go faster than the person beside them. So keeping order means holding the crowd back against its natural pull to scatter.

Keep that word in mind. It means held back and kept in formation, and it comes back two verses later.

The smallest voice in the story

Then, in the middle of the march, the Quran turns away from all that size and shows us this.

حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَآ أَتَوْا۟ عَلَىٰ وَادِ ٱلنَّمْلِ قَالَتْ نَمْلَةٌ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّمْلُ ٱدْخُلُوا۟ مَسَـٰكِنَكُمْ لَا يَحْطِمَنَّكُمْ سُلَيْمَـٰنُ وَجُنُودُهُۥ وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

"Until, when they came upon the valley of the ants, an ant said: O ants, enter your dwellings so that Sulaymān and his soldiers do not crush you while they do not perceive it."

An-Naml (27:18)

There are two things about what she says that deserve your attention.

The first is that she never speaks to him at all. She is talking to her own people about him, as you would talk about someone who is out of the room. She is not making a request to a king. She calls out a warning between neighbours, the kind of thing you say when you assume the powerful person cannot hear you. Normally he could not have.

The second is her last phrase, while they do not perceive it. She has just told her colony that an army is about to crush them. In the same breath she explains that it would not be on purpose. Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī all read that phrase the same way. They say she is protecting the honour of a prophet even while she warns her people to run from him. She could have said only run, they will crush you. But she added the words that clear his name.

So the smallest creature in the story is the one who thinks most carefully and fairly about the greatest man in it. She does something difficult and generous on a day when no one was watching.

What he does with it

What would you expect a man with three armies to do when he overhears an insect discussing whether he is dangerous?

فَتَبَسَّمَ ضَاحِكًا مِّن قَوْلِهَا وَقَالَ رَبِّ أَوْزِعْنِىٓ أَنْ أَشْكُرَ نِعْمَتَكَ ٱلَّتِىٓ أَنْعَمْتَ عَلَىَّ وَعَلَىٰ وَٰلِدَىَّ وَأَنْ أَعْمَلَ صَـٰلِحًا تَرْضَىٰهُ وَأَدْخِلْنِى بِرَحْمَتِكَ فِى عِبَادِكَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحِينَ

"So he smiled, amused at her speech, and said: My Lord, enable me to be grateful for Your favour which You have bestowed upon me and upon my parents, and to do righteousness of which You approve. And admit me, by Your mercy, among Your righteous servants."

An-Naml (27:19)

Start with the smile. The Arabic verb is tabassama, and the dictionaries are precise about it. Of all the words for laughing, it is the most restrained. It is the quietest way pleasure can show on a face. Then the verse adds ḍāḥikan, "amused," so we know the amusement was real. He was genuinely delighted, but he let only the smallest trace of it show. That is a man being careful with his own reaction in front of a creature much smaller than himself.

The prayer that follows, though, is the real point of the scene.

The word that ties the whole course together

Look at how he asks. He does not simply say make me grateful. He says awziʿnī. It comes from the same root, w-z-ʿ, that described his army being held in its ranks two verses earlier.

Put plainly, this man has just been shown commanding jinn, men and birds in formation. Now he turns to God and asks:

hold me in rank too.

The army outside him in verse 17 and the life inside him in verse 19 are described with the same word. He has seen the discipline it takes to keep tens of thousands of creatures from breaking formation. So he asks God to do that same thing to him, so that he stays grateful.

That tells you how he sees gratitude. For him it is less like a warm feeling that shows up when things go well. It is more like a formation. It has to be held in place against a constant pull the other way, by a power stronger than the thing being held. Left to itself, his soul will break ranks just as any army left to itself will. That happens not out of bad intent but simply because everything keeps pulling forward.

Perhaps you know perfectly well that your life is full of gifts, and you still walk around feeling flat about it. If you have ever wondered how that can be, this verse is the answer. You have not failed at feeling something. You were trying to hold the formation on your own.

Three requests, and who does the work in each

The prayer has three parts, and the way it is built is the most practical thing in this module.

One: enable me to be grateful for Your favour which You have bestowed upon me and upon my parents. This is gratitude for what has already been given. It reaches back into the past, because he includes his parents. By every worldly measure he has gone further than his father. He has a bigger kingdom and the wind, and the jinn and the speech of animals. Yet in the prayer where he asks to be made grateful, he still names Dāwūd's household. Gratitude that stops at your own achievements is not finished.

Two: and to do righteousness of which You approve. Here the gratitude has to become something he does. It cannot stay a feeling of thankfulness. It has to come out as action, because thanks that never changes how you behave has not really happened.

Three: and admit me, by Your mercy, among Your righteous servants. The man who has everything asks to be let in by mercy, not because of his record. He does not point to his armies or his prophethood. He does not point to his kingdom or his good deeds. He asks to be let in the same way anyone else is let in.

Now look at who does what across the three parts. The words of effort belong to him: being grateful and doing good. The words of making it possible belong to God. God holds him in rank, is pleased with the work and lets him in. That is how the whole spiritual life works in one sentence: I will do the work, and You make the doing possible. And it is said by the most capable human being in the Quran.

Notice too where he is standing when he says it. He is at the top, not in a crisis. An ant has just shown that his name is known and his power is feared even among insects. That is exactly the moment when people are least likely to pray, and it is the moment he chooses. So the main idea of this course arrives early and quietly, in a valley. The dangerous hour is the good one.

The bird who was missing

The second scene tests something different. The question is no longer whether he can hear a small voice. It is whether he can be corrected by one.

وَتَفَقَّدَ ٱلطَّيْرَ فَقَالَ مَا لِىَ لَآ أَرَى ٱلْهُدْهُدَ أَمْ كَانَ مِنَ ٱلْغَآئِبِينَ

"And he inspected the birds and said: what is it with me that I do not see the hoopoe? Or is he among the absent?"

An-Naml (27:20)

There are two small details here, and both are worth knowing.

The verb for inspecting, tafaqqada, is not the ordinary word for losing something. Its form suggests effort, actively going to look for what is missing. Ibn Kathīr notes that the tense shows this was a habit that Sulaymān kept up regularly. He checked. A king who takes a roll call of the birds is a king who notices when one bird is missing from a crowd of thousands.

The second detail is the way he asks the question. He does not say where is the hoopoe? or the hoopoe is absent. Word for word, he says what is it with me that I do not see him? He questions his own eyes first. He leaves open the possibility that the problem is in his looking rather than in the bird.

Then comes a hard sentence, and this course does not skip hard sentences.

لَأُعَذِّبَنَّهُۥ عَذَابًا شَدِيدًا أَوْ لَأَا۟ذْبَحَنَّهُۥٓ أَوْ لَيَأْتِيَنِّى بِسُلْطَـٰنٍ مُّبِينٍ

"I will surely punish him with a severe punishment, or slaughter him, or he must bring me a clear authorisation."

An-Naml (27:21)

He names three possible outcomes. In the Arabic all three carry the same heavy emphasis, the grammar used for a binding oath. That matters, because it means the third option is not an afterthought. Or he brings me a clear reason is sworn to just as firmly as the punishment is. So the way out is promised with the same force as the threat.

That is what a fair standard looks like. The penalty is real and he says so openly, but the door out is held open just as firmly.

"I have encompassed what you have not"

Then the bird comes back. It says something to a prophet-king that almost no one in the Quran says to anyone.

فَمَكَثَ غَيْرَ بَعِيدٍ فَقَالَ أَحَطتُ بِمَا لَمْ تُحِطْ بِهِۦ وَجِئْتُكَ مِن سَبَإٍۭ بِنَبَإٍ يَقِينٍ

"But he stayed not long, and said: I have encompassed that which you have not encompassed, and I have come to you from Sabaʾ with certain news."

An-Naml (27:22)

Read that first phrase again. In plain terms it means I know something you don't. And it is said by a bird to the man who was taught the speech of birds.

The bird chooses its words carefully, though, and it is not being reckless. The Arabic says I have encompassed that which you have not encompassed. It does not say "I know more than you" or "there are things you don't know." It limits its claim to one particular piece of news. That is the narrowest true thing it could have said.

Al-Rāzī draws from this scene the principle that makes it important. By God's choice a lesser creature can have knowledge that a greater one lacks. That is not because the greater one is deficient. It is because God is the one who hands out knowledge, and He does not hand it out according to rank. At some point you will need a fact that sits with someone far below you by every measure you care about. You will only get it if they can bring it to you and you are able to hear it.

That is exactly the test here, and this is his answer.

۞ قَالَ سَنَنظُرُ أَصَدَقْتَ أَمْ كُنتَ مِنَ ٱلْكَـٰذِبِينَ

"He said: we shall see whether you told the truth or were of the liars."

An-Naml (27:27)

He does not punish the bird, but he does not simply believe it either. Al-Qurṭubī reads his response as careful examination rather than anger. He is not reacting. He is checking.

There is a small imbalance in his sentence that is worth seeing. On the truthful side he uses a verb about one action: did you speak the truth this time? On the other side he uses a phrase about belonging. He asks whether the bird is of the liars, meaning a whole kind of creature. Al-Zamakhsharī explains why the sentence is built this way. The balanced version would have been did you speak truly or did you lie. The phrase about belonging was chosen because it is sharper. A single lie might be a slip, but someone who belongs among the liars cannot be trusted about anything. So the check the bird is about to face will decide more than whether this one report is true. It will decide what kind of creature brought it.

Now notice how he handles stakes that high. He says openly what is at stake. Then he puts the whole matter on hold until the evidence comes back. He does not punish on suspicion, and he does not trust because the bird is charming. The report gets checked first, and the bird's standing depends on what the check shows. Most of us do it the other way round. We decide what kind of person someone is, and then decide whether their facts are worth checking.

What the two scenes are doing together

Put the two scenes side by side and the pattern is clear.

In the first, a creature far beneath him says something he was never meant to hear. It leads him to the deepest prayer of his life. In the second, a creature far beneath him tells him he has missed something. It leads him to investigate.

Both scenes are the same test, given twice. Can a man with enormous power still receive something from below? Being kind to those beneath you is easy and costs a powerful man nothing. The real question is whether he can actually take something in from them. It might be information he lacked or a correction he did not ask for. It might be a warning that sounds like a complaint.

Notice what makes that possible. Here humility is not just part of his personality. It holds the whole structure up, and it is exactly what he asked for in the valley when he said hold me in rank. A man who truly believes that everything he has was given to him has no reason to feel threatened when a bird knows something he does not. The same Hand that gave him his kingdom also gives out knowledge. So why would He not put a piece of it with a hoopoe?

An ungrateful version of this man could not have come through either scene. He would have heard the ant as an insult and the bird as disobedience. When you believe you built your kingdom yourself, every small voice that knows better threatens the story you tell about yourself.

That is the connection this module is here to make. In these scenes, gratitude is what keeps a powerful man able to listen. In the next module we watch it being tested on a scale that makes the ant and the bird look like a warm-up.
