Start Here — The Story

Before any analysis, you should be able to tell this story out loud from start to finish, the way you would tell it to a friend. That is easy here, because the Quran itself tells it that way. If you took the course on his father, you will remember the warning we had to give there. Dāwūd's life is scattered across nine chapters with no continuous account anywhere, so we had to piece it together. His son is different. Sulaymān is given one long, unbroken story of around thirty verses in a single chapter, An-Naml, with a beginning, a middle and an ending. It is one of the most sustained narratives in the Quran. So we can simply read it. Two scenes near the end come from other chapters, and we will say so when we get there.

A son who inherits

The story opens with two men, father and son, speaking one sentence together.

وَلَقَدْ ءَاتَيْنَا دَاوُۥدَ وَسُلَيْمَـٰنَ عِلْمًا ۖ وَقَالَا ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ ٱلَّذِى فَضَّلَنَا عَلَىٰ كَثِيرٍ مِّنْ عِبَادِهِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

"And We had certainly given to Dāwūd and Sulaymān knowledge, and they said: praise be to God, who has favoured us over many of His believing servants."

An-Naml (27:15)

The first words the Quran puts in their mouths are praise be to God. These are two kings saying thank you before a single test has come to them, and the whole course keeps coming back to that sentence.

Then the son steps forward and speaks on his own:

وَوَرِثَ سُلَيْمَـٰنُ دَاوُۥدَ ۖ وَقَالَ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ عُلِّمْنَا مَنطِقَ ٱلطَّيْرِ وَأُوتِينَا مِن كُلِّ شَىْءٍ ۖ إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَهُوَ ٱلْفَضْلُ ٱلْمُبِينُ

"And Sulaymān inherited Dāwūd. He said: O people, we have been taught the speech of birds, and we have been given of all things. Indeed, this is the clear favour."

An-Naml (27:16)

He announces his inheritance in public. He says we have been given, and then he calls it what it is, a favour, and an obvious one. The first gift he mentions is a strange one to lead with. He does not talk about armies or gold. He tells people he can understand what birds are saying.

An army, and one ant

The Quran shows how big his kingdom was with a single picture. His forces are gathered and marching in their ranks, and they include jinn, human beings and birds, three different kinds of creature all under one man. Then the story turns away from all of that to something very small:

حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَآ أَتَوْا۟ عَلَىٰ وَادِ ٱلنَّمْلِ قَالَتْ نَمْلَةٌ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّمْلُ ٱدْخُلُوا۟ مَسَـٰكِنَكُمْ لَا يَحْطِمَنَّكُمْ سُلَيْمَـٰنُ وَجُنُودُهُۥ وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

"Until, when they came upon the valley of the ants, an ant said: O ants, enter your dwellings so that Sulaymān and his soldiers do not crush you while they do not perceive it."

An-Naml (27:18)

Listen to how carefully she speaks. She warns her people, and in the same breath she defends the man she is warning them about. When she says while they do not perceive it, she means he would never do it on purpose. So the smallest creature in the story is the one who gives the king the benefit of the doubt.

Then comes the moment the whole course depends on. The most powerful man alive overhears an ant, and this is how he responds:

فَتَبَسَّمَ ضَاحِكًا مِّن قَوْلِهَا وَقَالَ رَبِّ أَوْزِعْنِىٓ أَنْ أَشْكُرَ نِعْمَتَكَ ٱلَّتِىٓ أَنْعَمْتَ عَلَىَّ وَعَلَىٰ وَٰلِدَىَّ وَأَنْ أَعْمَلَ صَـٰلِحًا تَرْضَىٰهُ وَأَدْخِلْنِى بِرَحْمَتِكَ فِى عِبَادِكَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحِينَ

"So he smiled, amused at her speech, and said: My Lord, enable me to be grateful for Your favour which You have bestowed upon me and upon my parents, and to do righteousness of which You approve. And admit me, by Your mercy, among Your righteous servants."

An-Naml (27:19)

He smiles, and then he prays, and what he asks for is the ability to be grateful. Keep that word in mind, because it comes back again and again until the very end of the story.

A missing bird

When he inspects the birds, he notices that one is missing, and asks where the hoopoe is. He speaks sharply, saying he will punish it severely, or even slaughter it, unless it brings him a clear reason for being away.

When the bird arrives, it does not apologise. It tells him that it knows something he does not, because it has come from a land called Sabaʾ with news he can be certain of. A woman rules there, it says. She has been given everything and has a magnificent throne, and she and her people bow down to the sun instead of to God.

How Sulaymān responds tells you what kind of man he is. He does not punish the bird, but he does not simply believe it either. He says we will see whether you told the truth or lied. Then he sends the hoopoe back with a letter and tells it to deliver the letter, step back, and watch what they do.

A queen who thinks

Now the Quran turns the story over to her, and for several verses we get to hear her thinking out loud.

She gathers her advisers and tells them a noble letter has been delivered to me, and then she reads it to them. It is from Sulaymān, and it opens in the name of God, the Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful. Its message is short: do not be arrogant with me, and come to me in submission.

Then she asks them for advice. Advise me in my affair; I would never decide a matter until you are present with me. Her generals answer the way generals do. They tell her they are strong and have serious military power, but that the decision is hers and they will follow her command. In other words, they offer her a war if she wants one.

She does not want one. Instead she makes a quiet observation about how kings behave:

قَالَتْ إِنَّ ٱلْمُلُوكَ إِذَا دَخَلُوا۟ قَرْيَةً أَفْسَدُوهَا وَجَعَلُوٓا۟ أَعِزَّةَ أَهْلِهَآ أَذِلَّةً ۖ وَكَذَٰلِكَ يَفْعَلُونَ

"She said: indeed kings, when they enter a city, ruin it and render the honoured of its people humbled — and that is what they do."

An-Naml (27:34)

So she decides to test him instead. She sends him a gift, and she says openly why: I will see with what the messengers return. It is an experiment. If he takes the money, then he is a king like any other and can be bought. If he refuses it, then he is something different.

He refuses it. When the gift arrives he sends it back, and his answer shows that he understood exactly what she was testing. Do you offer me wealth? What God has given me is better than what He has given you. It is you who are delighted with your gift. Then he sends her messengers home with a warning about forces they could not stand against.

A throne in the blink of an eye

Knowing that she is on her way, he asks his court which of them will bring him her throne before she arrives in submission.

A powerful jinn offers to do it. I will bring it before you get up from where you are sitting, and I am strong enough for it, and trustworthy. That is a serious offer from a being with real power.

Then someone else speaks. The Quran does not give his name. It only calls him one who had knowledge of the Book, and he offers to do it even faster: I will bring it to you before your gaze returns to you. That means faster than a glance, and it happens just as he says.

So now the throne of another ruler is standing in Sulaymān's hall, brought across a great distance by a power that makes his own jinn look slow. Any king in history would know what to say at a moment like this. Here is what Sulaymān says:

قَالَ ٱلَّذِى عِندَهُۥ عِلْمٌ مِّنَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ أَنَا۠ ءَاتِيكَ بِهِۦ قَبْلَ أَن يَرْتَدَّ إِلَيْكَ طَرْفُكَ ۚ فَلَمَّا رَءَاهُ مُسْتَقِرًّا عِندَهُۥ قَالَ هَـٰذَا مِن فَضْلِ رَبِّى لِيَبْلُوَنِىٓ ءَأَشْكُرُ أَمْ أَكْفُرُ ۖ وَمَن شَكَرَ فَإِنَّمَا يَشْكُرُ لِنَفْسِهِۦ ۖ وَمَن كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ رَبِّى غَنِىٌّ كَرِيمٌ

"Said one who had knowledge of the Book: I will bring it to you before your glance returns to you. So when he saw it settled before him, he said: this is from the favour of my Lord, to test me — whether I am grateful or ungrateful. And whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself; and whoever is ungrateful — then indeed my Lord is Free of need, Generous."

An-Naml (27:40)

This verse is the centre of the course, and Module 3 spends most of its time inside it. For now, just notice how it is built. At the very height of his power he does not say look what I can do. He says this is a test, and he names both ways it could turn out, out loud, including the bad one.

A floor of glass

He has her throne disguised, and when she arrives he asks her whether her throne looks like this one. She answers carefully: It is as though it were it. She will not let a trick question catch her out in either direction.

Then she is invited to enter the palace. When she sees the floor she thinks it is water, so she lifts the hem of her dress to wade through it. He tells her that it is a palace paved smooth with glass.

In that small, embarrassing moment, when she has misread a floor, something opens up inside her:

قِيلَ لَهَا ٱدْخُلِى ٱلصَّرْحَ ۖ فَلَمَّا رَأَتْهُ حَسِبَتْهُ لُجَّةً وَكَشَفَتْ عَن سَاقَيْهَا ۚ قَالَ إِنَّهُۥ صَرْحٌ مُّمَرَّدٌ مِّن قَوَارِيرَ ۗ قَالَتْ رَبِّ إِنِّى ظَلَمْتُ نَفْسِى وَأَسْلَمْتُ مَعَ سُلَيْمَـٰنَ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

"It was said to her: enter the palace. Then when she saw it she took it for a body of water and uncovered her shins. He said: it is a palace paved smooth with glass. She said: my Lord, I have wronged myself, and I submit with Sulaymān to God, Lord of the worlds."

An-Naml (27:44)

That is where her story ends. The Quran tells us nothing more about her, neither what she did next nor what became of her. It gives her the last word, and her last word is a statement of faith. Later storytellers added a great deal to her story, but we will not. What the Quran chose to keep is better than anything they added.

Two scenes from elsewhere, and an ending

The story in An-Naml ends there. The Quran gives us three more pieces of Sulaymān's life in other chapters, and we should be clear that we are adding them from outside.

In Sūrat Ṣād there is a scene with horses, where he says something about himself that no other prophet in the Quran says: that he loved good things more than the remembrance of his Lord. A few verses later comes the strangest sentence in his whole story: We tested Sulaymān, and cast upon his throne a body — then he returned. That is all the Quran tells us. There was a test, there was a body, and he returned, but the Quran never says what the test was. Module 4 is about that silence, and about why we are going to leave it alone.

In Sūrat Sabaʾ we are told how he died, and it is as quiet and as strange as anything in the Quran. He dies standing up, leaning on his staff, and nobody notices. His court does not notice, and neither do the jinn who are labouring for him, so they keep on working. Only when a small creature of the earth eats through the staff does he fall, and only then does anyone realise that the king has been dead all along. He was the most powerful man in the world, and the only one who noticed was a little creature chewing through his stick.

So the story begins with an ant that nobody would have noticed, and it ends with a creature that nobody noticed. That is not an accident, and we will come back to it.

How this course works

That is the story. Now for the plan, because this course does something deliberate with it. Instead of walking through the timeline once, we go through the same life five times, and each time we go a little deeper, like climbing the same mountain by a higher path.

Module 1 — Everything and Everyone. A full list of everything this man was given, and a question the course keeps returning to: what is left to test a person who has been given everything?
Module 2 — The Ant and the Hoopoe. Two scenes where a man of enormous power listens to creatures far below him, and what happens to a king who is willing to be corrected by a bird.
Module 3 — The Queen and the Question. Her whole story, and, at its high point, the sentence Sulaymān says about himself that tells us what his life was really for.
Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You. The hard parts: the horses, the body on the throne, a prayer that sounds wrong to modern ears, and the accusation of sorcery that the Quran answers directly. It also asks why this life is told in one long piece when his father's was scattered.
Module 5 — Your Own Kingdom. The whole story turned toward you, with three questions and one last discovery about the rarest kind of people in the Quran.

We make you two promises before you go on. First, nothing here requires Arabic, and every term will be explained in plain words the moment it appears. If a sentence ever sends you to a dictionary, that is our failure, not yours. Second, where the Quran stays silent, we will stay silent too, and we will tell you when we are doing it. That turns out to be one of the main lessons this particular life has to teach.
