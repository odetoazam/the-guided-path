Start Here — The Story

Before any analysis, you should be able to tell this story out loud. Start to finish, the way you would tell it to a friend. And this time that is easy — because the Quran itself tells it that way. If you took the course on his father, you will remember the warning we had to give: Dāwūd's life is scattered across nine chapters with no continuous account anywhere, and we had to gather it. His son is different. Sulaymān is given one long, unbroken story — around thirty verses in a single chapter, An-Naml, with a beginning, a middle and an ending. It is one of the most sustained narratives in the whole Quran. So we can simply read it. Two scenes at the end come from other chapters, and we will say so when we get there.

A son who inherits

The story opens with two men and one sentence between them.

وَلَقَدْ ءَاتَيْنَا دَاوُۥدَ وَسُلَيْمَـٰنَ عِلْمًا ۖ وَقَالَا ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ ٱلَّذِى فَضَّلَنَا عَلَىٰ كَثِيرٍ مِّنْ عِبَادِهِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

"And We had certainly given to Dāwūd and Sulaymān knowledge, and they said: praise be to God, who has favoured us over many of His believing servants."

An-Naml (27:15)

Notice what the very first quoted words out of their mouths are. Not a claim, not a plan — praise be to God. Two kings, before a single test has arrived, saying thank you. Keep that. The whole course is about to walk back to it.

Then the son steps forward on his own:

وَوَرِثَ سُلَيْمَـٰنُ دَاوُۥدَ ۖ وَقَالَ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ عُلِّمْنَا مَنطِقَ ٱلطَّيْرِ وَأُوتِينَا مِن كُلِّ شَىْءٍ ۖ إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَهُوَ ٱلْفَضْلُ ٱلْمُبِينُ

"And Sulaymān inherited Dāwūd. He said: O people, we have been taught the speech of birds, and we have been given of all things. Indeed, this is the clear favour."

An-Naml (27:16)

An inheritance, announced in public. He does not say I earned this. He says we have been given, and then names it for what it is: a favour, and an obvious one. And what a strange list of gifts to open with — not armies, not gold. He can understand what birds are saying.

An army, and one ant

The Quran shows us the scale of his kingdom in a single image: his forces gathered — jinn, human beings, and birds — held in their ranks and moving. Three separate orders of creation under one man. And then the story cuts away from all of it, to this:

حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَآ أَتَوْا۟ عَلَىٰ وَادِ ٱلنَّمْلِ قَالَتْ نَمْلَةٌ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّمْلُ ٱدْخُلُوا۟ مَسَـٰكِنَكُمْ لَا يَحْطِمَنَّكُمْ سُلَيْمَـٰنُ وَجُنُودُهُۥ وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

"Until, when they came upon the valley of the ants, an ant said: O ants, enter your dwellings so that Sulaymān and his soldiers do not crush you while they do not perceive it."

An-Naml (27:18)

Read her line again, because it is remarkably careful. She warns her people — and in the same breath she defends the man she is warning them about. While they do not perceive it. She is saying: he would not do it on purpose. The smallest creature in the story is the one who gives the king the benefit of the doubt.

And here is the moment the entire course hangs on. The most powerful man alive overhears an ant. What does he do with it?

فَتَبَسَّمَ ضَاحِكًا مِّن قَوْلِهَا وَقَالَ رَبِّ أَوْزِعْنِىٓ أَنْ أَشْكُرَ نِعْمَتَكَ ٱلَّتِىٓ أَنْعَمْتَ عَلَىَّ وَعَلَىٰ وَٰلِدَىَّ وَأَنْ أَعْمَلَ صَـٰلِحًا تَرْضَىٰهُ وَأَدْخِلْنِى بِرَحْمَتِكَ فِى عِبَادِكَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحِينَ

"So he smiled, amused at her speech, and said: My Lord, enable me to be grateful for Your favour which You have bestowed upon me and upon my parents, and to do righteousness of which You approve. And admit me, by Your mercy, among Your righteous servants."

An-Naml (27:19)

He smiles. And then he prays — and the thing he asks for is not more. It is the ability to be grateful. Put a mark next to that word. We are going to follow it all the way to the end of the story.

A missing bird

He inspects the birds and finds a gap in the ranks: where is the hoopoe? He says something sharp — that he will punish it severely, or slaughter it, unless it brings him a clear reason for its absence.

The bird arrives, and it does not apologise. It says, in effect: I know something you don't. It has come from a land called Sabaʾ with news he can be certain of — a woman rules there, she has been given everything, she has a magnificent throne, and she and her people bow to the sun instead of to God.

Sulaymān's response is the mark of the man. He does not punish the bird, and he does not simply believe it either. He says: we will see whether you told the truth or lied. Then he sends the hoopoe back with a letter, with instructions to deliver it, withdraw, and watch what they do.

A queen who thinks

Now the Quran does something unusual: it hands the story to her, and lets her think out loud for several verses.

She gathers her advisers. A noble letter has been delivered to me, she says — and she reads it to them: it is from Sulaymān, and it opens in the name of God, the Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful, and its message is short — do not be arrogant with me, and come to me in submission.

Then she asks for counsel. Advise me in my affair; I would never decide a matter until you are present with me. Her generals answer like generals: we are strong, we have serious military power — but the command is yours; say the word. They hand her a war she could have had.

She does not take it. Instead she gives one of the most quietly devastating political observations in the Quran:

قَالَتْ إِنَّ ٱلْمُلُوكَ إِذَا دَخَلُوا۟ قَرْيَةً أَفْسَدُوهَا وَجَعَلُوٓا۟ أَعِزَّةَ أَهْلِهَآ أَذِلَّةً ۖ وَكَذَٰلِكَ يَفْعَلُونَ

"She said: indeed kings, when they enter a city, ruin it and render the honoured of its people humbled — and that is what they do."

An-Naml (27:34)

So she tests him instead. She sends a gift, and says plainly what the gift is for: I will see with what the messengers return. It is an experiment. If he takes the money, he is a king like other kings and can be bought. If he doesn't, he is something else.

He doesn't. The gift arrives and he refuses it, and his answer names exactly what she was testing: do you offer me wealth? What God has given me is better than what He has given you. It is you who are delighted with your gift. Then he sends her messengers home with a warning about forces they could not withstand.

A throne in the blink of an eye

Knowing she is coming, he turns to his court with a question: which of you will bring me her throne before she arrives in submission?

A powerful jinn volunteers: I will bring it before you get up from where you are sitting — and I am strong enough for it, and trustworthy. A serious offer, from serious power.

And then someone else speaks. The Quran describes him only as one who had knowledge of the Book — no name — and he offers something else entirely: I will bring it to you before your gaze returns to you. Faster than a glance. And it is done.

Here the throne of another monarch is standing in his hall, fetched across a distance by a power that makes his own jinn look slow. Any king in history knows what to say at a moment like this. Here is what he says:

قَالَ ٱلَّذِى عِندَهُۥ عِلْمٌ مِّنَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ أَنَا۠ ءَاتِيكَ بِهِۦ قَبْلَ أَن يَرْتَدَّ إِلَيْكَ طَرْفُكَ ۚ فَلَمَّا رَءَاهُ مُسْتَقِرًّا عِندَهُۥ قَالَ هَـٰذَا مِن فَضْلِ رَبِّى لِيَبْلُوَنِىٓ ءَأَشْكُرُ أَمْ أَكْفُرُ ۖ وَمَن شَكَرَ فَإِنَّمَا يَشْكُرُ لِنَفْسِهِۦ ۖ وَمَن كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ رَبِّى غَنِىٌّ كَرِيمٌ

"Said one who had knowledge of the Book: I will bring it to you before your glance returns to you. So when he saw it settled before him, he said: this is from the favour of my Lord, to test me — whether I am grateful or ungrateful. And whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself; and whoever is ungrateful — then indeed my Lord is Free of need, Generous."

An-Naml (27:40)

That is the centre of this course, and we will spend a whole module inside it. For now, just hear the shape of it: at the highest point of his power he does not say look what I can do. He says this is a test — and he names both possible outcomes out loud, including the bad one.

A floor of glass

He has her throne disguised, and when she arrives he asks: is your throne like this? Her answer is beautifully hedged — it is as though it were it — a woman refusing to be caught out by a trick question either way.

Then she is told to enter the palace. She sees the floor, takes it for water, and lifts the hem of her garment to wade. He tells her: it is a palace paved smooth with glass.

And in that small, embarrassing moment — misreading a floor — something opens in her:

قِيلَ لَهَا ٱدْخُلِى ٱلصَّرْحَ ۖ فَلَمَّا رَأَتْهُ حَسِبَتْهُ لُجَّةً وَكَشَفَتْ عَن سَاقَيْهَا ۚ قَالَ إِنَّهُۥ صَرْحٌ مُّمَرَّدٌ مِّن قَوَارِيرَ ۗ قَالَتْ رَبِّ إِنِّى ظَلَمْتُ نَفْسِى وَأَسْلَمْتُ مَعَ سُلَيْمَـٰنَ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

"It was said to her: enter the palace. Then when she saw it she took it for a body of water and uncovered her shins. He said: it is a palace paved smooth with glass. She said: my Lord, I have wronged myself, and I submit with Sulaymān to God, Lord of the worlds."

An-Naml (27:44)

That is the end of her story. The Quran does not tell us one thing more about her — not what she did next, not what became of her. It gives her the last word, and the last word is her own faith. Later storytellers could not leave it alone and added a great deal. We will not. What the Quran chose to keep is better than what they added.

Two scenes from elsewhere — and an ending

The An-Naml story closes there. But the Quran gives us three more pieces of this life, in other chapters, and honesty requires us to say that we are joining them on.

In Sūrat Ṣād there is a scene with horses, in which he says something no other prophet in the Quran says about himself — that he loved good things more than the remembrance of his Lord. And a few verses later there is the strangest sentence in his whole story: We tested Sulaymān, and cast upon his throne a body — then he returned. That is all the Quran tells us. A test, a body, and a return. What the test was, it never says. Module 4 is about that silence, and about why we are going to leave it alone.

And in Sūrat Sabaʾ, his ending — as quiet and as odd as anything in the book. He dies standing, leaning on his staff, and nobody notices. Not his court, not the jinn labouring for him. They keep working. It takes a small creature of the earth eating through the staff for him to fall, and only then does anyone realise the king has been dead the whole time. The most powerful man in the world, and the only creature paying attention was a worm in a stick.

The story began with an ant nobody would have noticed. It ends with a creature nobody noticed. That is not an accident, and we will come back to it.

How this course works

That is the story. Now the plan — because this course does something deliberate with it. Instead of walking the timeline once, we walk the same life five times, going deeper each pass. Same mountain, higher path each time round.

Module 1 — Everything and Everyone. The full inventory of what this man was handed, and a question the whole course hangs on: what is left to test a person who has been given everything?
Module 2 — The Ant and the Hoopoe. Two scenes of enormous power listening downward — and what happens to a king who can be corrected by a bird.
Module 3 — The Queen and the Question. The heart of the course. Her whole arc, and at the peak of it, the sentence Sulaymān says about himself that names what his life was actually for.
Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You. The hard parts: the horses, the body on the throne, a prayer that sounds wrong to modern ears, the charge of sorcery the Quran answers directly — and why this life gets one long telling when his father's was scattered.
Module 5 — Your Own Kingdom. The whole thing turned toward you. Three questions, and one last discovery about the rarest category of people in the Quran.

Two promises before you go on. Nothing here requires Arabic, and no term will be used without being explained in plain words the moment it appears — if a sentence ever needs a dictionary, that is our failure, not yours. And where the Quran keeps a silence, we will keep it with it, and tell you honestly that we are doing so. That turns out to be one of the main things this particular life has to teach.
