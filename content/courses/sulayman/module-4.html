Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You

So far the Quran has told Sulaymān's story generously. This module is about the places where it stops. There are four of them in his life, and they are deliberate choices, not gaps in the record. The Quran was never an unfinished document that later writers were invited to complete. Learning to stand at the edge of one of these silences without filling it in is one of the most useful things a believer can learn. It matters here because nearly all the folklore about this man grew out of these silences. So we will walk up to each one, look at it, and tell you clearly what we are choosing not to tell you, and why. We also make you one promise: where the scholars genuinely disagree, you will hear both sides, with the names of who held each view, rather than whichever version is most comfortable to read.

The horses

We begin with the one scene in the Quran where Sulaymān says something about himself that no other prophet says.

إِذْ عُرِضَ عَلَيْهِ بِٱلْعَشِىِّ ٱلصَّـٰفِنَـٰتُ ٱلْجِيَادُ

فَقَالَ إِنِّىٓ أَحْبَبْتُ حُبَّ ٱلْخَيْرِ عَن ذِكْرِ رَبِّى حَتَّىٰ تَوَارَتْ بِٱلْحِجَابِ

"When there were displayed to him in the late afternoon the poised, swift horses — he said: indeed I have loved the love of good things over the remembrance of my Lord, until it disappeared behind the veil."

Ṣād (38:31–32)

Read his confession carefully, because it is more interesting than the version people usually repeat. He does not say he loved something evil. The word for what he loved is al-khayr, which means the good. These were fine, well-bred horses standing poised and ready, and it is completely legitimate for a king to own them and enjoy them.

What he admits is that one thing pushed another out of its place. The Arabic puts a small word between the two halves of the sentence that means "away from," so he loved the good in a way that drew him away from the remembrance of his Lord. Something legitimate grew until it took up space that was meant for something else.

That is by far the most common way a good life quietly goes wrong. Almost nobody chooses evil over God. Instead, people choose good things, like their work, their family, their home, or a skill they are rightly proud of. Then that good thing keeps growing, until one day they notice that the remembrance of God has been squeezed to the edges of their day, even though they never once decided to let that happen.

Two silences in one verse

Now look at two things the verse does not say.

First, it never mentions prayer. The words are the remembrance of my Lord. If you have heard this story told as "he missed the ʿaṣr prayer," you should know that this detail was supplied by commentators, and we can name them. Qatāda, al-Suddī, al-Jalālayn and al-Baghawī all read it as the ʿaṣr prayer, and Ibn Kathīr follows them, taking care to add that Sulaymān missed it by forgetting and not by choice. But other scholars disagreed, and seriously. Al-Qushayrī held that it was a voluntary prayer and not an obligatory one. Al-Zamakhsharī allowed that it may not have been a prayer at all, but a litany, a set of words of remembrance that Sulaymān used to recite at that hour. And Abū Jaʿfar al-Naḥḥās read the scene completely the other way round. In his view Sulaymān was in the middle of praying when the horses were brought, and waved them away with his hand because he was praying, so nothing was missed at all.

Second, it never says what disappeared. The phrase is until it vanished behind the veil, and the Arabic uses a pronoun, "it," without saying what "it" refers to. By a wide margin, most of the tradition reads it as the sun setting. Al-Ṭabarī, Qatāda, al-Suddī, al-Zamakhsharī and al-Baghawī all take it that way, and al-Qurṭubī records that this is what most commentators hold. A smaller group reads it as the horses moving out of sight.

And then the hard line

رُدُّوهَا عَلَىَّ ۖ فَطَفِقَ مَسْحًۢا بِٱلسُّوقِ وَٱلْأَعْنَاقِ

"Bring them back to me. And he set about the shanks and the necks."

Ṣād (38:33)

The key word here is masḥ. In everyday use it means wiping, passing your hand over a surface, and it belongs to the same family as the word for wiping your head in wuḍūʾ. At this point the tradition splits clearly into two camps, so here is the disagreement, with the names of who held each side.

The first reading is that he put the horses to the sword. On this view, masḥ is a polite way of saying he struck them, so he cut the tendons of their legs and struck their necks, giving up the thing that had distracted him. This is the larger camp. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, Qatāda, al-Suddī, Muqātil, al-Jalālayn, al-Muyassar and al-Saʿdī all read it this way, and al-Baghawī reports it as the view of most commentators.

If you find yourself recoiling, do not skip over what this camp actually says, because they never describe it as an act of rage. They describe it as a sacrifice, and they place clear limits around it. Al-Jalālayn specifies that the meat was given away in charity. Al-Muyassar states that sacrificing horses as an offering was allowed in the law given to Sulaymān, which was a different law from ours. Al-Qushayrī puts it most sharply: he did not punish the horses; he punished himself. On this reading, a man gives up the thing he can least afford to lose, at his own cost, and feeds people with it.

The second reading is that he simply wiped them, which is what the word normally means. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that Sulaymān passed his hand over their manes and legs out of love for them, and al-Zuhrī and Ibn Kaysān describe him brushing the dust off them. This is the reading al-Ṭabarī himself preferred, and that matters, because al-Ṭabarī usually collects every view and declines to choose between them. Here he does choose, and he gives his reason. A prophet of God would not torture an animal or destroy his own wealth for no reason, and the horses were not to blame for his being distracted by looking at them.

Al-Rāzī goes further and makes an argument you can check without knowing any Arabic. The Quran uses this same verb when it tells believers how to do wuḍūʾ: wipe your heads and your feet. If masḥ meant cutting when it is used of legs and necks, he says, then that verse would be telling you to cut off your own feet, which nobody has ever suggested. In his view the word only carries the sense of striking when a sword is actually mentioned, and no sword is mentioned here.

Because this tradition argues back, al-Rāzī's argument gets an answer. Al-Ālūsī takes him on directly. Nobody ever claimed that masḥ always means cutting, he says; the claim is only that in this verse, with these objects, it does. A word does not have to mean something everywhere in order to mean it here. Al-Ālūsī also adds a report, which he grades as sound enough to use, that traces the cutting reading back to the Prophet ﷺ himself. After the Prophet's word, he says, no one else has a say. Whether that report really holds is a question for the specialists. We pass it on to you exactly as al-Ālūsī gives it, because we promised you both sides at full strength.

So there are two readings of this one word, each held seriously and argued back and forth by serious scholars.

Notice what happens if you stay still instead of picking one. The shape of the passage stays the same either way. He admitted out loud that he had drifted, and then he did something about it straight away. Whatever his hands actually did, the order is the same: first he confessed, then he corrected himself. What the Quran holds back is exactly what the correction was, which means you cannot copy his action. What you can copy is the way he noticed.

The body on the throne

Now we come to the deepest silence in Sulaymān's life. Here is the entire verse:

وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا سُلَيْمَـٰنَ وَأَلْقَيْنَا عَلَىٰ كُرْسِيِّهِۦ جَسَدًا ثُمَّ أَنَابَ

"And We certainly tested Sulaymān, and cast upon his throne a body — then he returned."

Ṣād (38:34)

In Arabic it is only nine words long. It tells us there was a test, a body and a return, and nothing more. It does not say what the test was, what the body was, how long it lasted, or whether he did anything wrong.

The commentators found this just as hard as you do, and they filled in the gap. But here is the fact that settles the matter: they do not agree with each other, and their accounts are not different angles on one event but different stories that cannot all be true. Al-Ṭabarī passes on reports that the body was a devil in human form, and those reports cannot even agree on the devil's name; they give four different ones. Al-Muyassar gives something completely different, a half-formed child born after Sulaymān made an oath. Al-Zamakhsharī reports a third version, in which a son of his died and was laid on the throne. Al-Rāzī rejects all of these and suggests instead that Sulaymān had a severe illness that left him sitting on his throne like a body without a soul.

So we have a devil, a half-formed child, a dead son and an illness. These cannot be blended into one picture. Anyone who presents all four as different views of the same event is doing exactly what this material does not allow.

The story we will not tell you

The most famous version is the story of the ring. In it, Sulaymān's power lived in a signet ring. A jinn stole the ring and sat on his throne pretending to be him, while Sulaymān wandered about without anyone recognising him, until he got the ring back from the belly of a fish.

We will not tell that story, and our reason is the verdict of the classical tradition itself, not squeamishness. The scholars put it unusually bluntly:

  • Abū Ḥayyān says the prophets must be cleared of stories like these, and that they are among what it is not permissible to transmit. He also gives the reason: if a devil could take on a prophet's form so convincingly that people accepted the impostor, then no one could have confidence in any prophet's mission at all.
  • Al-Zamakhsharī tells the story in full, and then says that the careful scholars refused it and called it a fabrication, and that giving devils that kind of power over God's servants is repugnant.
  • Ibn Kathīr says "all of these are isrāʾīliyyāt," meaning material taken in from earlier religious communities, some of whom did not even accept that Sulaymān was a prophet.
  • Al-Rāzī puts the whole cycle of stories among the talk of storytellers, as opposed to the people who verify their sources, and points out that nothing in the Quran's own wording suggests any of it.

Here is the detail that should stay with you longest, because it shows how this tradition keeps itself honest. One version of the ring story has a strong chain of narrators going back to Ibn ʿAbbās. Ibn Kathīr accepts that the chain is strong, but he still rejects what the story says, because he judges that Ibn ʿAbbās himself must have received it from the People of the Book. So even a sound chain back to a great Companion was not, on its own, enough to accept a story. The scholars still asked where the information first came from.

There is one more reason to leave this gap alone. Ibn Kathīr's own commentary on this verse tells none of these stories. The most widely read tafsir in the world reaches 38:34, says that Sulaymān was tested, says that he turned back and asked for forgiveness, and moves on to the next verse.

What the ṣaḥīḥ report actually says, and what it doesn't

There is one authentic narration connected to this area, and it is worth handling carefully, because it shows exactly how a gap gets filled in.

In al-Bukhārī and Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ tells us that Sulaymān once said he would go round to ninety women that night. Each of them, he said, would give birth to a horseman who would fight in God's path. His companion told him to say if God wills, but he did not say it. He went to all of them, and none became pregnant except one, who gave birth to half a child. The Prophet ﷺ then added that if Sulaymān had said if God wills, all of those sons would have fought in God's path.

This report has been very carefully authenticated, and it teaches a plain, useful lesson about the limits of even a prophet's intentions.

But notice what it does not contain. There is no throne in it, no body placed on anything, and not a single word linking it to 38:34. The verse talks about a jasad, a body, on a kursī, a throne. The ḥadīth talks about an oath and a child, and the two texts do not share a single scene.

Al-Muyassar connects them, and other commentators do too. They may be right, because it is a reasonable and respectable inference. But it is still an inference: the Quran never makes the connection, and the commentators did. Once you can see that join, with an authentic text on one side, a verse on the other and a human scholar linking them, you have learned the main skill this module is here to teach.

The word he shares with his father

Now look at the last word of that verse: thumma anāb, "then he returned."

Even this word is disputed, and the dispute proves the point. Al-Saʿdī and Ibn Kathīr read it as returning to God in repentance. But al-Ṭabarī, al-Jalālayn, Muqātil and Qatāda read it as returning to his kingdom, being restored to power once whatever happened was over. So even the verse's very last word leaves a gap, and each school filled it according to the version of the story it had already settled on.

That is why the honest thing to do is to take the word to the one place where its meaning is clear. Ten verses earlier, in the same chapter, at the high point of his father's story, Dāwūd realises that he has been tested, so he sought his Lord's forgiveness, and fell down bowing, and returned. There is nothing unclear about that. It describes a man down on the floor before God.

You can check this next point for yourself. In that exact form, anāba, "he returned," said of one man in the past tense, the word appears in only four places in the whole Quran. Two are elsewhere, and the other two are Ṣād verse 24 and Ṣād verse 34, which describe this father and this son, ten verses apart, each at the moment he was tested. (If you repeat this, be precise: the plural form appears once more, at 39:17. It is the singular form that appears four times.)

That turns the two silences into a single point. The Quran holds back what both tests were. We are never told exactly what Dāwūd's slip amounted to, and we are never told what happened to Sulaymān at all. In both cases the text skips over the failure and keeps the return. That choice is made twice, in one chapter, about one family, and it tells you that, in a person's worst moment, God treats the turning back as worth reporting rather than the fall.

Then the chapter does something quiet that almost nobody notices. Compare the line God gives Dāwūd straight after forgiving him with the line God gives Sulaymān at the end of his whole passage:

فَغَفَرْنَا لَهُۥ ذَٰلِكَ ۖ وَإِنَّ لَهُۥ عِندَنَا لَزُلْفَىٰ وَحُسْنَ مَـَٔابٍ

"So We forgave him that. And indeed, for him with Us is nearness, and a beautiful place of return."

Ṣād (38:25) — after Dāwūd's test

وَإِنَّ لَهُۥ عِندَنَا لَزُلْفَىٰ وَحُسْنَ مَـَٔابٍ

"And indeed, for him with Us is nearness, and a beautiful place of return."

Ṣād (38:40) — after Sulaymān's whole kingdom

These are the same six words in Arabic. Verse 40, in its entirety, is the closing phrase of verse 25.

Think about what that means. One man has just been forgiven, and the other has just been given the wind, the jinn and power over the created world. Yet God closes both of their stories with the same sentence, word for word. The father at his lowest point and the son at his highest are promised the same nearness and the same beautiful homecoming.

That line is the real family inheritance, and it is not the kingdom. These two men faced completely different tests and were given the same ending, because in the reckoning that really matters, being forgiven and being given everything lead to exactly the same place.

The prayer that sounds wrong

قَالَ رَبِّ ٱغْفِرْ لِى وَهَبْ لِى مُلْكًا لَّا يَنۢبَغِى لِأَحَدٍ مِّنۢ بَعْدِىٓ ۖ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْوَهَّابُ

"He said: my Lord, forgive me, and grant me a kingdom that will not belong to anyone after me. Indeed, You are the Bestower."

Ṣād (38:35)

If you read this quickly, it sounds like the opposite of everything this course has said. A man who has just been tested asks for more, and asks that nobody else should ever have it.

So before looking at the middle of the prayer, look at how it begins and ends. It opens with my Lord, forgive me, so he admits his need before he asks for anything. It closes with a name of God, You are al-Wahhāb, the Bestower, the One who gives freely without needing a reason. So his request sits between an admission of his own need and an acknowledgement of who God is.

There is also a detail you can hear. The verb for "grant me" is hab, and the name he ends on is al-Wahhāb. Both come from the same root, which means giving something as a pure gift. That root has already appeared in this passage. Five verses earlier, the Quran introduced Sulaymān with it: and We gifted to Dāwūd, Sulaymān. So in this passage Sulaymān is himself a gift, he asks for a gift, and he calls God the Giver of gifts. He was a present from God, asking the One who gives presents.

As for the strange phrase not for anyone after me, the commentators read it in several ways. Al-Jalālayn takes it to mean that the kingdom should belong to no one but him. ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ, and al-Ṭabarī with him, read it as a request that this kingdom should not be taken away from him again. Ibn Kathīr thinks the plain meaning is that no one after him would have the right to ask for such a thing.

There is one more report that belongs with this verse.

In al-Bukhārī and Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ describes how an ʿifrīt, a powerful jinn, came at him in the night to break his prayer, and God gave him power over it. He said: I wanted to tie it to one of the pillars of the mosque so that you could all look at it in the morning. Then I remembered the supplication of my brother Sulaymān, "grant me a kingdom that will not belong to anyone after me." So I let it go.

The Prophet ﷺ had the creature in his hands, and he let it go rather than step on a prayer that God had granted to a prophet who had died centuries earlier. That is what respecting someone else's gift looks like when it costs you something. It is also a clear confirmation that Sulaymān's prayer was answered.

The charge of sorcery

There is one more silence to look at, except that this one works the other way, because here the Quran speaks up and defends him.

In the centuries around his life and after it, a whole body of magical writing circulated under his name, including books of spells said to come from Solomon. The claim behind them was simple: his kingdom, with the jinn, the wind and the impossible knowledge, ran on sorcery.

The Quran answers that claim inside a long verse in al-Baqarah. We quote the whole verse, because a disputed verse deserves to be seen in full:

وَٱتَّبَعُوا۟ مَا تَتْلُوا۟ ٱلشَّيَـٰطِينُ عَلَىٰ مُلْكِ سُلَيْمَـٰنَ ۖ وَمَا كَفَرَ سُلَيْمَـٰنُ وَلَـٰكِنَّ ٱلشَّيَـٰطِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ يُعَلِّمُونَ ٱلنَّاسَ ٱلسِّحْرَ وَمَآ أُنزِلَ عَلَى ٱلْمَلَكَيْنِ بِبَابِلَ هَـٰرُوتَ وَمَـٰرُوتَ ۚ وَمَا يُعَلِّمَانِ مِنْ أَحَدٍ حَتَّىٰ يَقُولَآ إِنَّمَا نَحْنُ فِتْنَةٌ فَلَا تَكْفُرْ ۖ فَيَتَعَلَّمُونَ مِنْهُمَا مَا يُفَرِّقُونَ بِهِۦ بَيْنَ ٱلْمَرْءِ وَزَوْجِهِۦ ۚ وَمَا هُم بِضَآرِّينَ بِهِۦ مِنْ أَحَدٍ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ وَيَتَعَلَّمُونَ مَا يَضُرُّهُمْ وَلَا يَنفَعُهُمْ ۚ وَلَقَدْ عَلِمُوا۟ لَمَنِ ٱشْتَرَىٰهُ مَا لَهُۥ فِى ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ مِنْ خَلَـٰقٍ ۚ وَلَبِئْسَ مَا شَرَوْا۟ بِهِۦٓ أَنفُسَهُمْ ۚ لَوْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْلَمُونَ

"And they followed what the devils recited during the reign of Sulaymān. And Sulaymān did not disbelieve — but the devils disbelieved, teaching people magic, and that which was sent down to the two angels at Babylon, Hārūt and Mārūt. And the two would not teach anyone until they said: we are only a trial, so do not disbelieve. And they learned from them that by which they cause separation between a man and his wife. But they harm no one through it except by God's permission. And they learned what harms them and does not benefit them. And they certainly knew that whoever purchased it would have no share in the Hereafter. And wretched is that for which they sold themselves, if only they had known."

Al-Baqarah (2:102)

Four words in the middle carry the answer: wa mā kafara Sulaymān, "and Sulaymān did not disbelieve." God says this flatly, in His own speech, without any qualification. The Quran does not often stop to answer a specific accusation against a prophet by name, but it does here.

In the same breath, it puts the blame where it belongs: but the devils disbelieved, teaching people magic. The magic was real and it was spreading, but it belonged to the devils. What people attached to Sulaymān's name was never his.

The verse itself settles two further things, and we will stay strictly within them. First, it condemns the practice without any ambiguity, since those who learn it learn what harms them and does not benefit them, and have no share in the Hereafter. Second, it puts the whole subject under God's control: they harm no one through it except by God's permission. Nothing here works outside God's authority.

Notice also what the verse does not do. It tells you what magic does, but it never tells you how it works. It gives no method and no technique. The Quran names the harm and holds back the method, and we follow its example exactly. The commentaries contain many stories about the two angels at Babylon, but the verse gives only their two names and one sentence of theirs, and we go no further than that.

The death nobody noticed

فَلَمَّا قَضَيْنَا عَلَيْهِ ٱلْمَوْتَ مَا دَلَّهُمْ عَلَىٰ مَوْتِهِۦٓ إِلَّا دَآبَّةُ ٱلْأَرْضِ تَأْكُلُ مِنسَأَتَهُۥ ۖ فَلَمَّا خَرَّ تَبَيَّنَتِ ٱلْجِنُّ أَن لَّوْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْلَمُونَ ٱلْغَيْبَ مَا لَبِثُوا۟ فِى ٱلْعَذَابِ ٱلْمُهِينِ

"Then when We decreed death for him, nothing indicated to them his death except a creature of the earth eating his staff. So when he fell, it became clear to the jinn that if they had known the unseen, they would not have remained in the humiliating labour."

Sabaʾ (34:14)

The most powerful ruler in the Quran dies leaning on a stick, and nobody notices, neither his court nor the unseen beings labouring for him. The work simply carries on in front of a dead king.

Since this module is about separating what the text says from what the commentators added, here are two quick notes. The verse says a creature of the earth, and the idea that it was a termite comes from outside the text, although nearly everyone accepts it, including Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid and Qatāda. The famous detail that he stood there dead for a year is also added. Al-Ṭabarī and Qatāda say a year, Ibn Kathīr says nearly a year, and al-Saʿdī carefully adds "according to what has been said." The Quran itself gives no length of time.

Now read the conclusion the verse draws for itself, because it tells you what the scene is for, and it turns out to be about the jinn rather than about him: it became clear to the jinn that if they had known the unseen, they would not have remained in the humiliating labour.

That is the point of the scene. It shows, publicly and for all time, that the jinn do not know the unseen. They could not even tell that the man at the front of the room had died, so they kept working because they did not know.

Now put that next to the charge of sorcery and see what happens to it. The whole appeal of the magic books written in Solomon's name was hidden knowledge gained through unseen beings: secrets, special access and power behind the curtain. Yet the Quran's final picture of Sulaymān's life shows those same beings unable to notice a dead body a few feet away from them.

So the Quran does more than deny the charge. It takes away the very thing that made the charge attractive in the first place.

For our purposes, there is one more thing to notice. Everything he was given lasted after his death only as long as it took for a stick to give way. He had said so himself, decades earlier, with a queen's throne standing in his hall: this is from the favour of my Lord. He was right, because none of it had ever really been his, and the way his life ended simply made that visible.

How the tradition polices itself

Before the last question, here is one scene that shows everything this module has asked you to do being done, far better, thirteen centuries ago.

Ibn ʿAbbās once asked ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib about the verse on the horses, and ʿAlī asked him what he had heard about it. Ibn ʿAbbās repeated an account he had received from Kaʿb al-Aḥbār. It was a detailed story, with numbers in it, in which Sulaymān wronged the horses and had his kingdom taken from him as a punishment.

ʿAlī answered in two words: Kaʿb lied.

Then he gave his reason, and it is the sentence to take away from this module: the prophets of God do not commit wrong, because they are protected.

Here is a Companion of the Prophet ﷺ hearing a colourful account of a prophet's failure and rejecting it. He did not have a rival chain of narrators to set against it. He measured the story against what he already knew to be true about prophets, and it fell short. So the habit of refusing a good story on principle goes back at least as far as ʿAlī.

Why this life gets told, and his father's does not

There is one last question, and it is about how the Quran arranges these two lives.

Dāwūd's story is scattered across sixteen verses in nine chapters, with no continuous narrative and no timeline. Sulaymān gets around thirty verses in a row, with a beginning, a middle and an end. They are from the same family, yet the Quran treats them in opposite ways. Why?

Think about what each life has to show.

Dāwūd's lesson fits in a single word: he was someone who returned to God. A word like that can be shown anywhere. You can place it in Ṣād, in Sabaʾ or in al-Anbiyāʾ, and each fragment carries the whole point, because the point is a quality of his character. Scattering it actually helps, because it makes the quality read as something permanent about him rather than something that happened on one particular afternoon.

Sulaymān's lesson, on the other hand, can only be shown as a sequence. You cannot show gratitude in a fragment, because the whole nature of gratitude is that it has to survive things happening. You have to watch a man be given something, then more, and then more again, and keep saying the same sentence about where it all came from. He is tested in a small way by an ant, then more seriously by a bird who knows something he does not, and then to the fullest extent with another ruler's throne standing in his hall. And then you have to watch him die and lose all of it, to find out whether that sentence was true.

You cannot cut a story like that into pieces. If you take any one scene out on its own, it becomes just a nice moment, but when the scenes are kept together they build an argument.

So the way these two lives are arranged is not an accident. The scattered telling went to the man whose lesson was a quality, and the continuous telling went to the man whose lesson was a process. And the two men were given the same word for the moment each was tested, and the same six words for where each of them ended up. That way you would know they were one family after all.

That leaves the final question, which is what Module 5 is for: what was all of this doing in a book that was sent to you?
