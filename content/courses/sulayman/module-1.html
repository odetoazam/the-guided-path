Module 1 — Everything and Everyone

You have the story. Now we slow down and take an inventory, because you cannot feel the weight of this man's test until you have felt the weight of what he was handed. There is a particular reason to do it carefully. The Quran does not present his gifts the way we would. We would lead with the kingdom. It leads with something else, and that opening gift explains everything that follows.

The one word

Here is the opening of the whole sequence again, and the question is which gift gets named:

وَلَقَدْ ءَاتَيْنَا دَاوُۥدَ وَسُلَيْمَـٰنَ عِلْمًا ۖ وَقَالَا ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ ٱلَّذِى فَضَّلَنَا عَلَىٰ كَثِيرٍ مِّنْ عِبَادِهِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

"And We had certainly given to Dāwūd and Sulaymān knowledge, and they said: praise be to God, who has favoured us over many of His believing servants."

An-Naml (27:15)

Think about everything the Quran could have said in that first clause. Between them these two men have a kingdom, a scripture and armies. They have a voice that moves mountains, iron that softens in the hand, control of the wind, the service of unseen creatures, and the ability to understand animals. Any of those would have been a reasonable opening line.

What it says is We gave them knowledge. One word, and it is left unspecified. Not knowledge of judgement, not knowledge of the psalms, not knowledge of birds, though all three are attested elsewhere for these two men. Just knowledge, with no limit put on it.

That sets the order of the whole inventory. The kingship is the fruit and knowledge is the root. Everything else on the list grows out of the fact that these two men were given to understand something, and the Quran mentions it first because the other gifts depend on it.

Their first recorded reaction

Look at what happens in the same verse, because this is a pattern, and it shows up four more times before the course ends.

God gives them knowledge, and the next thing in the verse is what they said: al-ḥamdu lillāh. Praise belongs to God. That is a statement about the Giver rather than a thank-you for the gift. It would still be true if you had received nothing.

Two details in their sentence are worth having.

The first is that the Arabic verb is dual. That means one utterance, spoken jointly by the two of them. Father and son, quoted together, in a single line. It is the only time we hear them speak as one voice, and this is what they say with it.

The second is the last phrase, which shows real restraint. Who has favoured us over many of His believing servants. They do not say over all of them, and they do not say simply over His servants, which would cover the whole set. They put in the word many, and so they leave room above themselves. Two prophet-kings, at the top of the human world, carefully declining to claim the top.

There is a structural detail you can check by eye. Their response takes more words than the gift did. The giving is one clause and the praise is longer. So even the shape of the verse puts more weight on the thanks than on the thing received.

A man who will not use the active voice

Then the son speaks on his own, and the grammar of what he says is the whole of his character:

وَوَرِثَ سُلَيْمَـٰنُ دَاوُۥدَ ۖ وَقَالَ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ عُلِّمْنَا مَنطِقَ ٱلطَّيْرِ وَأُوتِينَا مِن كُلِّ شَىْءٍ ۖ إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَهُوَ ٱلْفَضْلُ ٱلْمُبِينُ

"And Sulaymān inherited Dāwūd. He said: O people, we have been taught the speech of birds, and we have been given of all things. Indeed, this is the clear favour."

An-Naml (27:16)

Take the two verbs at the centre of his announcement. We have been taught. We have been given. Both are passive, which is to say somebody did this to us.

He had the other option available and did not take it. He could have said we know the speech of birds. He could have said we have all these things. Instead the most powerful man alive stands in front of his people to announce his own extraordinary abilities, and builds the sentence so that he is not the subject of either verb.

In his whole announcement there are only two things he does himself. He inherited, and he said. Receiving and acknowledging. That is the complete list of Sulaymān's own actions in his own introduction.

That is accuracy. A man who built none of this is describing it in the only grammar that is true.

The order of the two gifts he names is worth having as well. The speech of birds comes first and of all things comes second. The smaller, stranger gift is named before the vast one. He leads with the ability to hear, before the ability to rule. Module 2 shows why that order matters.

The wind

Now the inventory proper. Start with the most cinematic item:

وَلِسُلَيْمَـٰنَ ٱلرِّيحَ عَاصِفَةً تَجْرِى بِأَمْرِهِۦٓ إِلَى ٱلْأَرْضِ ٱلَّتِى بَـٰرَكْنَا فِيهَا ۚ وَكُنَّا بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَـٰلِمِينَ

"And to Sulaymān, the wind, blowing forcefully, running by his command to the land which We had blessed. And We are, of all things, Knowing."

Al-Anbiyāʾ (21:81)

The word describes a strong, driving gust, the kind of wind you would normally shelter from. And it runs by his command, in the present tense, so it is a standing arrangement rather than a one-off miracle.

The verse also names where it runs to: the land which We had blessed. Al-Ṭabarī identifies that as al-Shām, the Levant. Al-Muyassar names it more precisely as the region of Bayt al-Maqdis. So the wind does not go anywhere he fancies. It has a stated destination, and that destination is a blessed place. The fastest transport ever given to a human being is pointed somewhere that was chosen for him.

The unseen workforce

Then comes the item that needs the most care in the whole inventory:

وَمِنَ ٱلشَّيَـٰطِينِ مَن يَغُوصُونَ لَهُۥ وَيَعْمَلُونَ عَمَلًا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ ۖ وَكُنَّا لَهُمْ حَـٰفِظِينَ

"And of the devils were those who dived for him and did work other than that — and We were of them Guardians."

Al-Anbiyāʾ (21:82)

Three things here, and each one is a fence.

First: "of the devils." The Arabic has a small word, min, which means "some of," a portion. The verse does not say the devils dived for him. It says some of them did. Al-Muyassar reads it exactly this way: God put a portion of them to work for him, in tasks other creatures could not perform. So his authority was over a subset, on assignment, and not over the unseen world.

Second: what they did. They dived. The verb is specific, the word for going down into water to bring something back up, the pearl-diver's word. Beyond that comes work other than that, which is left unspecified. The Quran declines to give an inventory of what unseen creatures do, and that refusal is itself instruction.

Third, and this is the one that matters most: and We were of them Guardians. God kept them in line, not Sulaymān. On this clause the commentators offer two readings and both are worth having. Al-Muyassar reads it as God holding the workers, so that refusal was simply not available to them. Al-Jalālayn reads it as God guarding the work, since left to themselves they would undo what they had finished.

Either way the point stands, and it is the most important sentence in this module. What kept the unseen workforce in place was God, continuously, and not Sulaymān. If that guarding lifted for a moment, the arrangement would dissolve. So his command over them was not a power he possessed. It was a loan that Someone else was actively maintaining.

Keep that in mind, because in Module 4 we meet a very old slander built on this material, the claim that Sulaymān's kingdom ran on sorcery, and the Quran answers it directly. This verse is where the answer starts. A man who commands spirits by his own art is a magician. A man for whom God restrains a portion of them, and keeps restraining them, is something else. That distinction carries the whole weight.

There is one more detail here, and it rewards slow reading. The verse about the wind carries no guardian clause. The verse about the devils does. The wind has no will, so it cannot rebel. The other thing can.

What the list adds up to

Put the whole inventory in one place.

He inherits a prophet-king's household. He is given knowledge, named first and left unbounded. He is taught the speech of birds. He is given, in his own words, of all things. He commands a force of three kinds of creature, jinn and human and bird, held in ranks. He is given the wind, driving hard, running by his command to a blessed land. He is given the labour of unseen beings who dive for him and build for him, held in place by God. Later chapters add more: molten copper, sanctuaries, vast basins, cauldrons that do not move.

Now say plainly what that is. As far as the Quran shows us, it is the most any human being has ever been given. Spiritually is a different measure. But in sheer command over the created world, no one else in the book comes close. Other prophets are given a miracle. He is given a standing arrangement with the elements.

The question this course hangs on

Which brings us to the problem that makes his life worth a course.

We know how testing usually works in scripture. Someone is given a hardship and we watch what they do with it. Ibrāhīm is asked for his son. Ayyūb loses everything. Yūsuf goes into a well and then a prison. Mūsā is sent, alone, at a tyrant. His own father Dāwūd is caught out by a mistake he makes in a moment of confidence. In every case the test arrives as something taken away, and the drama is whether the person holds.

Now try to apply that to Sulaymān and watch it fail. What do you take from a man who has the wind? What hardship do you send at someone who commands armies of three species? What could you withhold from a man who says, accurately, that he has been given of all things?

So here is the question, and the remaining modules exist to answer it:

If a life has been handed everything, and there is no lack anywhere in it, what is left to test?

The Quran's answer is stranger than a later loss, and much more useful to you. The abundance itself is the test.

If that is true, it changes what you are looking at in your own life, because most of us are running the old model. We treat hardship as the exam and everything else as the time in between, the good stretch, the years when nothing is wrong, the ordinary Tuesday. On the reading this course is going to make, those are the paper itself, and it is open on the desk right now.

He was given more than anyone, and the Quran keeps one word over him for what he did with it. In the next module we go and find it, in a valley, in the mouth of an insect.
