Module 1 — Everything and Everyone

You have the story. Now we slow down and list everything this man was given. You cannot feel how heavy his test was until you feel how much he was handed. There is a particular reason to do this carefully. The Quran does not present his gifts in the order we would. We would start with the kingdom. The Quran starts with something else, and that first gift explains everything that comes after it.

The one word

Here is the opening verse of the story again. As you read it, notice which gift the Quran names.

وَلَقَدْ ءَاتَيْنَا دَاوُۥدَ وَسُلَيْمَـٰنَ عِلْمًا ۖ وَقَالَا ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ ٱلَّذِى فَضَّلَنَا عَلَىٰ كَثِيرٍ مِّنْ عِبَادِهِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

"And We had certainly given to Dāwūd and Sulaymān knowledge, and they said: praise be to God, who has favoured us over many of His believing servants."

An-Naml (27:15)

Think about everything the Quran could have mentioned first. Between them, these two men have a kingdom, a scripture and armies. They have a voice that moves mountains and iron that softens in the hand. They control the wind, unseen creatures work for them, and they can understand animals. Any of those would have made a reasonable first line.

Instead the verse says We gave them knowledge. It is a single word, and the Quran does not say what kind of knowledge it means. It does not say knowledge of judging or of the psalms or of birds. Yet other verses show these two men had all three. It just says knowledge and puts no limit on it.

That tells us how to read the whole list. The kingdom is the fruit, and knowledge is the root. Everything else these two men were given grows out of the fact that they were allowed to understand things. The Quran mentions knowledge first because all the other gifts depend on it.

Their first recorded reaction

Now look at what happens next in the same verse. It sets a pattern that shows up four more times before the course ends.

As soon as God gives them knowledge, the verse tells us what they said. They said al-ḥamdu lillāh, praise belongs to God. That is a statement about the One who gives, not a thank-you for one particular gift. It would still be true even if they had been given nothing at all.

Two small details in their sentence are worth noticing.

The first is that the Arabic verb is in the dual form. That means the two of them say it together as one voice. Father and son are quoted speaking a single line jointly. It is the only time we hear them speak as one, and this is what they choose to say.

The second is the last phrase, which shows real humility: who has favoured us over many of His believing servants. They do not say God favoured them over all of His servants. Nor do they say simply over His servants, which would include everyone. They add the word many, and so they leave room for people above them. These are two prophet-kings at the top of the human world. Yet they carefully avoid claiming to be at the very top.

There is also something you can see just by looking at the verse. Their response takes more words than the gift did. The giving takes one short clause, and the praise that follows is longer. So even the shape of the verse puts more weight on the thanks than on what they received.

A man who will not use the active voice

Then the son speaks on his own. The grammar of his sentence tells you almost everything about his character.

وَوَرِثَ سُلَيْمَـٰنُ دَاوُۥدَ ۖ وَقَالَ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ عُلِّمْنَا مَنطِقَ ٱلطَّيْرِ وَأُوتِينَا مِن كُلِّ شَىْءٍ ۖ إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَهُوَ ٱلْفَضْلُ ٱلْمُبِينُ

"And Sulaymān inherited Dāwūd. He said: O people, we have been taught the speech of birds, and we have been given of all things. Indeed, this is the clear favour."

An-Naml (27:16)

Look at the two verbs at the centre of what he announces. They are we have been taught and we have been given. Both of them are passive, which means someone else did this to him.

He could easily have said it the other way. He could have said we know the speech of birds, or we have all these things. Instead the most powerful man alive stands before his people to announce his own extraordinary abilities. And he builds the sentence so that he is not the one doing either action.

In the whole announcement he does only two things himself. He inherited, and he said. In other words, he received and he acknowledged. That is the complete list of his own actions in the way he introduces himself.

This is not false modesty. He did not build any of this, so he describes it in the only grammar that is actually true.

The order of the two gifts he names is worth noticing as well. He mentions the speech of birds first and of all things second. So the smaller and stranger gift comes before the enormous one. He talks about his ability to hear before his ability to rule, and Module 2 shows why that order matters.

The wind

Now we come to the list itself. We will start with the most dramatic item on it.

وَلِسُلَيْمَـٰنَ ٱلرِّيحَ عَاصِفَةً تَجْرِى بِأَمْرِهِۦٓ إِلَى ٱلْأَرْضِ ٱلَّتِى بَـٰرَكْنَا فِيهَا ۚ وَكُنَّا بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَـٰلِمِينَ

"And to Sulaymān, the wind, blowing forcefully, running by his command to the land which We had blessed. And We are, of all things, Knowing."

Al-Anbiyāʾ (21:81)

The word used here describes a strong and driving gust. It is the kind of wind you would normally take shelter from. Yet it runs by his command. The verb is in the present tense, so this is an ongoing arrangement rather than a single miracle on a single day.

The verse also says where the wind goes: to the land which We had blessed. Al-Ṭabarī says this land is al-Shām, the Levant. Al-Muyassar names it more precisely as the region of Bayt al-Maqdis in Jerusalem. So the wind does not simply go wherever he likes. It has a stated destination, and that destination is a blessed place. The fastest transport any human being has ever been given is pointed toward a place that was chosen for him.

The unseen workforce

The next item needs more care than anything else on the list.

وَمِنَ ٱلشَّيَـٰطِينِ مَن يَغُوصُونَ لَهُۥ وَيَعْمَلُونَ عَمَلًا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ ۖ وَكُنَّا لَهُمْ حَـٰفِظِينَ

"And of the devils were those who dived for him and did work other than that — and We were of them Guardians."

Al-Anbiyāʾ (21:82)

There are three things to notice here, and each one sets a limit on how we should read it.

First, "of the devils." The Arabic has the small word min, which means "some of." So the verse does not say that the devils dived for him. It says that some of them did. Al-Muyassar reads it exactly this way. God put a portion of them to work for Sulaymān, doing tasks that other creatures could not do. So his authority covered a group of them who had been assigned to him, not the whole unseen world.

Second, what they did. They dived, and the verb is a precise one. It is the word for going down under water to bring something back up, the word you would use for a pearl diver. Beyond that, the verse only says they did work other than that. It does not say what the work was. The Quran chooses not to give us a list of what unseen creatures do, and that choice is itself a lesson.

Third, and most important, and We were of them Guardians. It was God who kept them in line, not Sulaymān. The commentators read this clause in two ways, and both are worth knowing. Al-Muyassar reads it as God holding the workers themselves, so that refusing to work was simply not possible for them. Al-Jalālayn reads it as God protecting the work. If the devils had been left to themselves, they would have undone what they had just finished.

On either reading the main point is the same, and it is the most important idea in this module. God kept these unseen workers in place all the time, not Sulaymān. If that protection had stopped for even a moment, the whole arrangement would have fallen apart. So his command over them was more like a loan than a power he owned. Someone else was keeping it going the entire time.

Keep that in mind for Module 4. There we will meet a very old slander built on this material. It is the claim that Sulaymān's kingdom ran on sorcery, and the Quran answers it directly. This verse is where that answer begins. A man who controls spirits through his own secret arts is a magician. God held back a portion of them for Sulaymān and kept holding them back. That makes him something completely different, and the whole answer rests on that difference.

There is one more detail here, and you only see it if you read slowly. The verse about the wind says nothing about God guarding it, but the verse about the devils does. That is because the wind has no will of its own and cannot rebel, while the devils can.

What the list adds up to

Now put everything in one place.

He inherits the household of a prophet who was also a king. He is given knowledge, which is named first and given no limit, and he is taught the speech of birds. In his own words, he is given of all things. He commands an army of three kinds of creature, all held in their ranks. They are jinn, humans and birds. He is given the wind, blowing hard and running by his command to a blessed land. He is given unseen beings who dive and build for him, while God keeps them in place. Later chapters add even more. They add molten copper and sanctuaries, and huge basins and cauldrons too heavy to move.

So let us say plainly what this means. As far as the Quran shows us, this is the most any human being has ever been given. Spiritual rank is measured differently. But in sheer control over the created world, no one else in the Quran comes close. Other prophets are given a miracle, while Sulaymān is given an ongoing arrangement with the forces of nature.

The question this course hangs on

That brings us to the problem that makes his life worth a whole course.

We know how testing usually works in scripture. Someone faces a hardship, and we watch what they do with it. Ibrāhīm is asked to give up his son. Ayyūb loses everything. Yūsuf is thrown into a well and later into prison. Mūsā is sent alone to face a tyrant. Even Dāwūd, Sulaymān's own father, is caught out by a mistake he makes in a moment of confidence. In every one of these cases the test comes as something taken away, and the drama is whether the person stays firm.

Now try to apply that to Sulaymān, and you will see that it does not work. What could you take away from a man who has the wind? What hardship could you send to someone who commands armies of three kinds of creature? What could you hold back from a man who truthfully says he has been given of all things?

So here is the question, and the rest of the course exists to answer it.

If a life has been handed everything, and there is no lack anywhere in it, what is left to test?

The Quran's answer is stranger than some loss that comes later, and much more useful to you. The abundance itself is the test.

If that is true, it changes how you should look at your own life. Most of us think about tests the old way. We treat hardship as the exam and everything else as the time in between. That means the good stretches, the years when nothing goes wrong and the ordinary Tuesday. On the reading this course is going to make, those ordinary days are the exam itself. The paper is open on your desk right now.

He was given more than anyone, and the Quran uses one particular word about what he did with it. In the next module we go and find that word, in a valley, right after an ant speaks.
