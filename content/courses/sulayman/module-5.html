Module 5 — Your Own Kingdom

For five modules you have been watching a man who was given more than anyone else. This last module is short, and it is about you. There is one fact about the sentence we have followed all through the course that we have kept back until now, and it changes who this story was written for. Before we get to it, here are three questions. They carry the practical weight of everything we have done, so give each of them some time.

What is your ant?

Go back to the valley. A whole army of jinn, men and birds is marching in its ranks, and a single insect says something the king was never supposed to overhear. Far from flattering him, she was warning her people to get out of his way.

Think about what that scene cost him. In one sense it cost him nothing at all, since he lost no battle, no land and no dignity. In another sense it cost a great deal, because a man that powerful had to be listening in the first place, down at ground level, to a voice that had no rank. The words were not addressed to him, and frankly they were not very complimentary. Most people at the top of anything have quietly stopped hearing voices like that. They never decided to stop listening; it is just that the noise up there is louder, and the small voices no longer reach them.

You have ants too. They might be the junior colleague whose objection you heard as a complaint, or the child who told you something in a rude tone, so that you only heard the tone. They might be the spouse who mentioned something three times before you took it seriously, or a quiet worry of your own that you keep talking over. None of these voices come with any authority, and that is exactly why they are a test.

So: who is your ant, the voice with no standing that has been trying to set you on a better course? And underneath the way it said it, what did it actually say?

What would you refuse?

Now think about the queen's experiment, because it is sharper than it looks. She did not send a gift to be polite. She sent it to find out what kind of man he was. Her reasoning was cold and correct: kings can be bought, and the cheapest way to learn what a man is made of is to offer him something. I will see with what the messengers return.

When the gift arrived, he did not refuse it as a show of piety. Read his answer again, because it is almost blunt:

فَلَمَّا جَآءَ سُلَيْمَـٰنَ قَالَ أَتُمِدُّونَنِ بِمَالٍ فَمَآ ءَاتَىٰنِۦَ ٱللَّهُ خَيْرٌ مِّمَّآ ءَاتَىٰكُم بَلْ أَنتُم بِهَدِيَّتِكُمْ تَفْرَحُونَ

"So when they came to Sulaymān he said: do you offer me wealth? What God has given me is better than what He has given you. Rather, it is you who rejoice in your gift."

An-Naml (27:36)

He is saying something more precise than that he is above money: you are delighted with what you were given, and I am not, because I know where mine came from. Her gift could not move him, because he never held his kingdom as something he owned in the first place. You cannot outbid a man who already believes that everything he has is on loan.

That is the practical difference gratitude makes, and it is worth being clear about, because gratitude is usually sold to us as a warm feeling. Here it is a way of holding things, and it changes what anyone can offer you. The ungrateful man thinks he owns his life, so every offer gives someone real leverage over it. The grateful man knows he is holding something that was handed to him, so most offers simply have nothing to grip.

So: what would you refuse? Answer for real life, not just in principle. What offer would actually get to you? It might be money, a title, entry to a room you have long wanted to be let into, or the approval of one particular person. Name it honestly, because that is where you are still holding something as though you had made it yourself.

Which of the two are you, today?

The third question is the whole course turned toward you. At the highest moment of his life, with another ruler's throne standing in his hall after being brought faster than a glance, Sulaymān does not celebrate and does not give himself the credit. He says out loud that this is a test, and he names both ways it could go: am I grateful, or ungrateful?

Think about how unusual that is. Most people in the Quran are told they are being tested. He works it out for himself, in the middle of his triumph, at the very moment when nobody would have blamed him for simply enjoying it. And he asks the question in the present tense, about himself, leaving the bad answer openly on the table.

Here is why that matters for you. We tend to imagine that the test of a good life comes as hardship, as illness, loss or a door that closes, and sometimes it does. But this whole course has been about the other kind of test, the one almost nobody talks about, which comes when everything goes right: the promotion, the healthy child, the house, the years when nothing is wrong. In this story those good times are the test itself rather than the gaps between tests, and they are harder, because hardship turns you toward God almost on its own, while abundance quietly turns you away.

So: think of the best thing in your life right now, the thing that is going well. Then ask his question, in his words and in the present tense: am I grateful, or ungrateful, about this? Do not ask whether you should be. Ask whether you are.

The sentence was never his alone

Now for the fact we have been keeping back.

All through the course, the sentence at 27:40 has seemed to belong to an extraordinary man. It was spoken at the high point of the largest kingdom ever given to a human being, with the wind, the jinn, the speech of birds, and a throne carried across a desert in an instant. If you read it that way, it is easy to conclude, without quite saying so, that it belongs to a kind of life you will never have.

But the Quran says the same thing somewhere else, and look who it gives it to:

وَلَقَدْ ءَاتَيْنَا لُقْمَـٰنَ ٱلْحِكْمَةَ أَنِ ٱشْكُرْ لِلَّهِ ۚ وَمَن يَشْكُرْ فَإِنَّمَا يَشْكُرُ لِنَفْسِهِۦ ۖ وَمَن كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَنِىٌّ حَمِيدٌ

"And We had certainly given Luqmān wisdom: be grateful to God. And whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself; and whoever is ungrateful — then indeed God is Free of need, Praiseworthy."

Luqmān (31:12)

Put the two side by side. Sulaymān says at 27:40: whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself; and whoever is ungrateful, then my Lord is Free of need, Generous. And at 31:12, in the passage about Luqmān, we read: whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself; and whoever is ungrateful, then God is Free of need, Praiseworthy.

This is more than a similar idea. In Arabic, the heart of both verses is one identical, unbroken run of words, is grateful only for himself; and whoever is ungrateful, then indeed..., and the differences come only at the edges. There are three of them: the tense of one verb, Sulaymān's my Lord where the other verse says God, and the closing name, which is Generous when Sulaymān says it and Praiseworthy in the other verse. It is the same sentence said twice, fitted to two different settings.

Now look at the two men connected with it. Sulaymān has a kingdom that no one else in the Quran is given, power over the wind, an army of three kinds of creature, and a throne brought from another country in less than the blink of an eye. Luqmān has none of that. The Quran gives him no kingdom, no army and no miracle, nothing supernatural at all. It says only that he was given wisdom, and then it shows him sitting down to talk with his son. That is his whole scene: a father giving advice to his boy.

So the greatest king in the Quran and a man with nothing but good sense and a child are tied to the same sentence about gratitude. That tells you what the sentence was measuring all along. It was never the size of the kingdom. It was the way the kingdom is held.

Then the Quran takes away the last excuse, and speaks to everybody:

وَإِذْ تَأَذَّنَ رَبُّكُمْ لَئِن شَكَرْتُمْ لَأَزِيدَنَّكُمْ ۖ وَلَئِن كَفَرْتُمْ إِنَّ عَذَابِى لَشَدِيدٌ

"And when your Lord proclaimed: if you are grateful, I will certainly increase you — but if you are ungrateful, indeed My punishment is severe."

Ibrāhīm (14:7)

This verse sets two words against each other, shakartum and kafartum, "you are grateful" and "you are ungrateful." They come from the same two roots Sulaymān used about himself on his throne, but now they are plural and addressed to everyone who reads them. The choice he saw at the height of the greatest kingdom in the Quran is the same choice you face over something much smaller, and the Quran does not treat them as different questions.

Notice too what is promised to those who choose well. The promise is increase, which is something different from comfort, or even exactly a reward. Gratitude is the one response in the Quran that is promised more in return, which is a strange kind of arithmetic, because the thing that costs you nothing is the very thing that grows.

The rarest category in the book

There is one last verse, and it is the one that should keep you honest. In Sūrat Sabaʾ, God lists what the jinn built for Sulaymān: the sanctuaries, the huge basins and the cauldrons fixed in place. Then, in the middle of that list of abundance, He gives a command and a verdict:

يَعْمَلُونَ لَهُۥ مَا يَشَآءُ مِن مَّحَـٰرِيبَ وَتَمَـٰثِيلَ وَجِفَانٍ كَٱلْجَوَابِ وَقُدُورٍ رَّاسِيَـٰتٍ ۚ ٱعْمَلُوٓا۟ ءَالَ دَاوُۥدَ شُكْرًا ۚ وَقَلِيلٌ مِّنْ عِبَادِىَ ٱلشَّكُورُ

"They made for him what he willed of sanctuaries, statues, basins like reservoirs, and fixed cauldrons. Work, O family of Dāwūd, in gratitude — and few of My servants are grateful."

Sabaʾ (34:13)

Look at where that last line is placed. It does not come after a passage about suffering. It comes after a list of everything a person could want: work in gratitude, and few of My servants are grateful. The warning hits hardest exactly where the giving is greatest, and that is the main idea of this course in a single verse.

And the word it uses is few. Of all the groups the Quran could have called rare, it chooses this one. It does not say the brave are few, or the learned. It says the grateful are few.

That is a hard sentence, but it is also an unusually hopeful one. Every rare group has an entry requirement, and look at what this one asks of you. You do not have to acquire anything, whether a throne, wisdom like Luqmān's, or any gift you were not given. You only need a particular way of holding what is already in your hands. It is the rarest group in the Quran, and its door is unlocked.

What the course was for

So here is why the Quran gave us this particular man, on this particular scale. It built the biggest possible version of the test, with every gift at once and power over creation itself, so that the question underneath could not be mistaken for anything else. If you take away every hardship from a life and hand it everything, what is left to test? What is left is gratitude. That is the experiment An-Naml runs, and it runs it at the largest possible size so that the result is easy to read.

Your version is smaller, but no less real, because it is the same choice over a smaller kingdom: a body that mostly works, people who are still with you, a roof over your head, a skill, an ordinary Tuesday on which nothing went wrong. The man who commanded the wind prayed to be grateful when he heard an ant, and a man with no kingdom at all is tied to the same sentence about the same thing.

And remember how Sulaymān's story ends, because the Quran chose that ending on purpose. He dies leaning on his staff, and not one of the jinn working around him notices, so the kingdom keeps running for a while under a dead king. Everything he was given lasted after him only as long as it took a small creature to eat through a stick. None of it had ever been his. It had been on loan the whole time, which is exactly what he had been saying ever since the ant.

The course ends here, on purpose, before the practice begins. What we have tried to do across these six modules is change what you see, by teaching you to find the Giver behind the gifts, first in his life and then in yours. Building that into a habit is slower work, and we have a separate place for it. Take the three questions with you. There is nothing to hand in; they are simply the way in.

His story opens before any test has come, with two kings saying praise be to God, and it closes on the reason that mattered. God gives to the grateful and the ungrateful alike, so His giving never depended on anyone's thanks. That is exactly what makes thanking Him a real test, and exactly what makes your thanks worth something when you offer them.
