Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You

So far this course has been about what the Quran says about Dāwūd. This module is about two things it deliberately does not do. It never opens the book it gave him, and it never tells his life in one place. You only got the story in order back in Module 0 because we put it together from sixteen verses spread across nine different chapters. Most readers pass right over both of these facts. But both are choices, and if we read them carefully they teach us as much as the verses do.

The first silence: a book nobody gets to read

Dāwūd is the only person in the Quran with a scripture named for him alone. It is the Zabūr, and the Quran mentions it as plainly as any fact can be stated:

وَرَبُّكَ أَعْلَمُ بِمَن فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۗ وَلَقَدْ فَضَّلْنَا بَعْضَ ٱلنَّبِيِّـۧنَ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ ۖ وَءَاتَيْنَا دَاوُۥدَ زَبُورًا

"And your Lord knows best whoever is in the heavens and the earth. And We have certainly favored some of the prophets over others — and We gave Dāwūd a Zabūr."

Al-Isrāʾ (17:55)

After that, the Quran names the book and moves on without saying anything more about it. Compare this with the way it treats the Tawrāt and the Injīl. It describes what is in them and argues about them, and it corrects what people did with them. The Zabūr gets a name and then silence.

There is one verse near the end of Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ that comes close to being an exception:

وَلَقَدْ كَتَبْنَا فِى ٱلزَّبُورِ مِنۢ بَعْدِ ٱلذِّكْرِ أَنَّ ٱلْأَرْضَ يَرِثُهَا عِبَادِىَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحُونَ

"And We have certainly written in the Zabūr, after the Reminder, that the earth — My righteous servants shall inherit it."

Al-Anbiyāʾ (21:105)

This is the closest the Quran ever comes to quoting Dāwūd's book, and even here it holds a lot back. It tells us what was written without giving us the actual words. The early scholars did not even agree that "the Zabūr" in this verse means his book. Some of them read it as a name for all the revealed books together. And the one line we are given is a promise about righteous people who would live generations after him, so it is not about Dāwūd himself. The book given to a man who was given so much gives us at most a single sentence, and that sentence is about somebody else.

So what does the Quran give us in place of his book? It gives us sound: the mountains returning his praise, the birds gathered overhead, and a whole landscape praising along with one voice. That is the Quran's answer to the question of what was in the Zabūr. It shows us what the book did to the man who carried it, and leaves the contents unsaid. This teaches us something about revelation itself, because the real measure of scripture is what it makes of you, more than how much of it you can recite.

The second silence: the man who never speaks

Let us go back to the prayer room one more time, because there is a silence in that scene so quiet that most readers walk straight past it. Here is how the scene begins:

إِذْ دَخَلُوا۟ عَلَىٰ دَاوُۥدَ فَفَزِعَ مِنْهُمْ ۖ قَالُوا۟ لَا تَخَفْ ۖ خَصْمَانِ بَغَىٰ بَعْضُنَا عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فَٱحْكُم بَيْنَنَا بِٱلْحَقِّ وَلَا تُشْطِطْ وَٱهْدِنَآ إِلَىٰ سَوَآءِ ٱلصِّرَٰطِ

"When they entered upon Dāwūd and he was alarmed by them, they said: do not be afraid — two disputants, one of us has wronged the other. So judge between us with truth, do not be unfair, and guide us to the even path."

Ṣād (38:22)

إِنَّ هَـٰذَآ أَخِى لَهُۥ تِسْعٌ وَتِسْعُونَ نَعْجَةً وَلِىَ نَعْجَةٌ وَٰحِدَةٌ فَقَالَ أَكْفِلْنِيهَا وَعَزَّنِى فِى ٱلْخِطَابِ

"This is my brother. He has ninety-nine ewes and I have one ewe — and he said: hand her over to me. And he overpowered me in the argument."

Ṣād (38:23)

Now count who speaks. The first man makes a full complaint with numbers, and he even mentions that he was talked over. The second man is the one who is about to be found guilty, and he never says a single word here or anywhere else. The Quran could have given his side in one short phrase, but it leaves it blank.

That blank is the test, and it also does something to you as a reader. When you hear ninety-nine ewes against one, your sympathy goes to the man with one ewe before you have thought about it at all. The numbers make the judgment for you. So by the time Dāwūd gives his ruling, you have already ruled along with him. The Quran leaves that gap in the story so that you end up in the judge's seat yourself.

The third silence: the backstory the Quran refuses to tell

The Quran tells us nothing else around the scene, starting with who the two visitors were. Some early commentators say they were angels sent to hold up a mirror to him. Others understood them as ordinary men, but the Quran itself does not say. We are also not told when or where this happened, or what became of the two men.

Later storytellers could not leave that gap alone. They took a tale from older religious writings about a woman and a husband who was sent off to die. They attached it to this passage, and it has been passed around ever since. We should set that tale aside. One reason is that it clashes with the honor God gives His prophets. But the main reason is simpler: the Quran had the room to tell it, and chose not to. This book tells us the exact number of the ewes and still does not tell us the man's name. So whatever it left out, it left out on purpose.

That is the real habit this module is trying to teach. When the Quran is silent, the silence is a message in itself. It is not a hole waiting to be filled with the nearest story. God draws a curtain over His prophet at exactly the moment we most want to look, and our job is to leave the curtain where it is. Some readers cannot stand an unfinished sentence, so they finish it themselves. The Quran is quietly training you not to read that way. Stop where the text stops, because what you were given is already complete. You have the verdict and the moment he realized, and you have the bowing.

Why his life is scattered

Now we come to the second big thing the Quran declines to do. His story is spread over sixteen verses in nine chapters. They are Al-Baqarah, An-Nisāʾ, Al-Māʾida and Al-Anʿām. Then come Al-Isrāʾ and Al-Anbiyāʾ, and last An-Naml, Sabaʾ and Ṣād. There is no single place where it is told in order. Nobody put Dāwūd together for you, and it is worth asking why.

The reason is that each chapter takes only the part of his story that its own argument needs. Once you see that, the scattering starts to make sense. So let us go through the chapters.

Ṣād takes the returner. The passage opens with God telling the Prophet ﷺ to be patient with what his opponents say, and remember Our servant Dāwūd (38:17). Dāwūd is brought in to comfort a Prophet who was being mocked, as an example of a man who had everything and still kept turning back to God. This chapter is full of his word. The root of awwāb means return. It appears in seventeen verses in the whole Quran, and eight of them are in this one chapter. It is used for Dāwūd at 38:17 and for the creatures around him at 38:19. Then it is used for his son Sulaymān at 38:30 and for Ayyūb at 38:44. The word for a person's final destination, maʾāb, comes up four more times before the chapter ends. So Ṣād is the chapter about returning, and Dāwūd is the first of the people in it who return.

Sabaʾ takes the grateful craftsman. The scene at the forge sits inside a chapter about gratitude, and the passage says so openly:

يَعْمَلُونَ لَهُۥ مَا يَشَآءُ مِن مَّحَـٰرِيبَ وَتَمَـٰثِيلَ وَجِفَانٍ كَٱلْجَوَابِ وَقُدُورٍ رَّاسِيَـٰتٍ ۚ ٱعْمَلُوٓا۟ ءَالَ دَاوُۥدَ شُكْرًا ۚ وَقَلِيلٌ مِّنْ عِبَادِىَ ٱلشَّكُورُ

"They made for him whatever he wished — sanctuaries, images, basins like great reservoirs, and cauldrons fixed in place. Work, O family of Dāwūd, in gratitude — and few of My servants are grateful."

Sabaʾ (34:13)

God says: work in gratitude, and few of My servants are grateful. Then look at what the chapter does next. It turns straight to the people of Sabaʾ. They were given two gardens and told to be thankful, but they turned away, and then the flood came. So the chapter sets one household that worked with thanks beside a whole nation that did not. That is why the story of the iron is in this chapter.

And the rest, briefly. Al-Anbiyāʾ takes the judge and the armor made "to protect you from your own violence". So there Dāwūd is a man whose gifts keep other people alive. An-Naml gives him two verses as a way into his son's story. An-Nisāʾ and Al-Isrāʾ mention only the Zabūr, as one item in a list showing that the revelation to Muhammad ﷺ was nothing new. Al-Anʿām gives just his name, among the people God guided. Al-Māʾida mentions his tongue, and a curse that was once spoken through it. Al-Baqarah has his most famous moment. It gives Jālūt half a verse inside somebody else's story. Then it steps back to state a law about the whole world. If God did not push back some people by means of others, the earth would fall into ruin (2:251).

What the scattering is for

When you put the nine chapters together, you learn something about how this book works. The Quran is not a biography and never set out to be one. It uses a life instead of preserving it. It gives each chapter the part its argument needs. That part may be patience or gratitude, or protection, succession or warning. So the scattering is part of the message.

So if you want to see the whole man, someone has to gather the pieces. That is exactly what you have been doing through these modules. Your reward for the gathering is this. When you lay all nine chapters side by side, one word keeps appearing underneath them. That word is returning. It is his title in Ṣād and what the mountains do in Sabaʾ. It is the place he is promised after the test, and the word his son inherits. The nine chapters make nine different arguments, yet the same idea runs through all of them. No single chapter shows it to you. You can only see it once you look at all of them together.

There are two habits to take with you from this module. First, let the gaps stay gaps. When the Quran goes silent, the silence is doing work that a filled-in story would spoil. Second, when you meet a familiar figure in an unfamiliar chapter, ask what this chapter needed them for. The answer is almost always a small version of the chapter's whole argument.

Only one module is left. We have watched a man be given everything and be tested in the middle of it all. Then we saw him named for the one thing he did with it. The last question is the only one remaining. It is about you, not him.
