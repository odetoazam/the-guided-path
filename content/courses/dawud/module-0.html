Start Here — The Story

Before we study anything, you should be able to tell this story out loud from start to finish, the way you would tell it to a child or a friend, so here is his whole life in order. The Quran itself does not tell it in order. It gives us separate scenes spread across nine different chapters, and it dates almost none of them. The order below is the one the scenes themselves suggest, and wherever we are guessing, we will tell you. By the end of the course you will understand why the Quran chose to scatter what we are about to gather in one place.

A shepherd in someone else's army

The story begins before anyone has heard of Dāwūd. His people, the Children of Israel, have been driven out of their homes, and they come to their prophet asking for a king so that they can fight in God's way. Before agreeing, he asks them one careful question: And if the order to fight actually came, might you not fight? They are almost insulted and ask why they would ever refuse, after being driven from their homes and their children. Then the order does come, and all but a few of them turn away (2:246). So the story opens with people making a confident promise that falls apart as soon as it is tested, and we will see the same thing happen again later.

God gives them a king anyway, a man named Ṭālūt, and they object straight away. How can he rule over us, when we have more right to it than he does, and he hasn't even been given wealth? Their prophet answers that God chose him and gave him knowledge and physical strength. Then he says the line the whole story rests on: God gives His kingship to whom He wills (2:247). The commentators add that Ṭālūt came from a family that had never held kingship or prophethood, and that this was what really bothered them, although the verse itself mentions only the wealth. Keep that line about God giving kingship in mind, because it comes back twice.

Ṭālūt leads the army out and tests it at a river, telling them that anyone who drinks their fill is out. Most of them fail. The small group that is left crosses the river, sees the enemy army led by a champion named Jālūt, and says out loud that we have no strength for this today. The few who trust God answer with one of the Quran's most famous sentences: how many times has a small band beaten a large one, by God's leave. Then they pray, asking God for patience, for steady feet, and for His help.

Somewhere in that small army is a young shepherd whom the Quran has not mentioned yet. Now, in four words, it does:

فَهَزَمُوهُم بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ وَقَتَلَ دَاوُۥدُ جَالُوتَ وَءَاتَىٰهُ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْمُلْكَ وَٱلْحِكْمَةَ وَعَلَّمَهُۥ مِمَّا يَشَآءُ ۗ وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ ٱللَّهِ ٱلنَّاسَ بَعْضَهُم بِبَعْضٍ لَّفَسَدَتِ ٱلْأَرْضُ وَلَـٰكِنَّ ٱللَّهَ ذُو فَضْلٍ عَلَى ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

"So they defeated them by God's leave, and Dāwūd killed Jālūt — and God gave him the kingship and wisdom, and taught him from what He willed. And were it not for God pushing back some people by means of others, the earth would have fallen into ruin. But God is full of bounty to all the worlds."

Al-Baqarah (2:251)

That is all the Quran says about the battle. There is no sling or stone, and it does not even describe the giant. The famous fight is over in half a line, and the verse moves straight on to what happened next, when God gave him the kingship and wisdom and taught him. A shepherd nobody knew walked into that battle, and he came out of it with everything that would shape the rest of his life.

The king who was given everything

After that, the gifts come one after another. The Quran lists them in different chapters, so we will gather them here. He becomes a king and a prophet. He is given his own scripture, the Zabūr, which is the only book in the Quran named for one man alone. His voice in worship is so true that the mountains echo his praise back to him at dawn and at dusk, and the birds gather above him and join in. He is also given a strange, physical miracle, because iron goes soft in his hands. God teaches him to shape it into coats of mail, which is armor made of linked rings. Then, the way a master speaks to a craftsman, He tells him to make them full-length and to get the size of the rings right. So he is a prophet and a king who works with his hands, making armor that keeps other people alive.

He is also a judge, and people bring their disputes to him. In one case, some sheep wandered into a field at night and ruined the crop. His young son Sulaymān was there, and God gave the boy a deeper understanding of the case than his father had. The Quran praises both of them, and the father is not made smaller by it. Module 2 spends its time inside that scene.

The test

Then comes the hardest moment of his life, and it happens in his private prayer room, not on a battlefield. The Quran does not say when it took place, but it shows him at the height of his power, so we place it here.

Two men climb over the wall, and Dāwūd is frightened. They tell him not to be afraid, because they are two men with a dispute and want him to judge between them fairly. Then one of them speaks: This is my brother. He has ninety-nine ewes. I have one. And he says, give me yours too, and he beat me in the argument.

Dāwūd, the most experienced judge alive, answers at once that the brother has wronged him. Then he realizes that the second man never spoke, and that he gave his ruling after hearing only one side. The whole visit was a test from God, and he had failed it in the most ordinary way a powerful man can fail. He decided too quickly, because he was used to being right. He asks God's forgiveness, falls to the ground bowing, and turns back to Him. God forgives him, and in the very next verse gives him the biggest title in the whole story, khalīfa, which means steward of the earth. A warning comes with the title: judge people by the truth, and do not follow your own leaning, because it will pull you off the road.

Notice the order of what happens. His mistake does not cost him the crown. First he is forgiven, then he is given a higher rank, and then he is warned. This whole course is built around that pattern.

The ending

The Quran ends his story quietly, with no final battle and no scene of his death. Instead, it ends with a son.

وَوَهَبْنَا لِدَاوُۥدَ سُلَيْمَـٰنَ ۚ نِعْمَ ٱلْعَبْدُ ۖ إِنَّهُۥٓ أَوَّابٌ

"And We gifted to Dāwūd — Sulaymān. What an excellent servant! Truly he was ever-returning."

Ṣād (38:30)

Sulaymān inherits both the kingdom and something better than the kingdom from his father. The Quran praises the son with one particular word. Keep that word in mind for now without studying it too closely. You will meet it again in Module 3, and there you will see that the Quran had been using it about the father all along.

How this course works

That is the story, and now here is the plan, because this course does something deliberate with it. Instead of walking through the timeline once, we will walk through the same life five times and go deeper each time. It is like walking around a mountain on a path that keeps climbing, so you see the same mountain from a little higher on every lap.

Module 1: The Complete Man. The whole portrait at a glance, the six gifts he was given, and the question the rest of the course tries to answer.
Module 2: The Forge and the Courtroom. We slow down and stand inside two scenes: the iron, and the case his son understood better than he did.
Module 3: The One Word. The one word God keeps using about this man, and why that word, and never "king", sums up his life.
Module 4: What the Quran Won't Tell You. The strange parts, such as the book that is named but never quoted, and the man in the story who never speaks. It also asks why the Quran spread this life across nine chapters instead of telling it the way we just did.
Module 5: The Return in Your Own Hands. Everything we have learned, turned toward your own life, with three questions and one last look at who that word was really written for.

One promise before you go on: nothing in this course requires Arabic, and every term will be explained in plain words the moment it appears. If a sentence ever sends you to a dictionary, that is our failure, not yours.
