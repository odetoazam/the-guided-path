Module 5 — The Return in Your Own Hands

For five modules you have been watching a man, and this last one is short and about you. There is one fact about the word we have been following that we have kept back until now, and it changes what all of this was for. Before we get to it, there are three questions, and it is worth answering them slowly, because they carry the whole practical point of the course.

What is your iron?

Think back to the workbench. God did not tell Dāwūd to be a great king. His instruction was surprisingly small: make full coats of armor, and measure the links well, and at the end came the words and I see all that you do. So the most powerful man in the Quran was given a material to work with, an instruction to do the job properly, and the knowledge that God was watching.

You have a material too, and it is the one actually in your hands, which may not be the one you wished for. It might be a trade, a house to run, a shift you work, or the meals you cook for somebody. It might be a skill you have quietly become good at over fifteen years and never once thought of as spiritual. Most of us assume the religious part of life happens somewhere else, in the time left over after work, and that the work itself is just what pays the bills. Dāwūd's forge is the Quran's answer to that idea. Even the links in his armor counted, and on the day he was given that instruction, measuring them well was his worship.

So what is your iron, the material you were given to shape with your own hands? And where in it have you stopped measuring the links, because you decided the work was too ordinary to matter?

Where do you rule before hearing the second side?

The second question is harder. Two men climbed a wall, one of them spoke, and the most capable judge alive gave his verdict before the other man had opened his mouth. Think about what kind of failure that was. He was not corrupt or cruel, only too quick. He had been right so many times that being right had become a habit, and the habit moved faster than his listening.

You do not need a throne to fail in this way. All you need is some authority over somebody and enough experience to feel sure of yourself. Think of the parent who knows exactly what happened before the child has finished the sentence, or the manager who has already decided which of two people is the problem. Think of the friend who reads a message and builds a whole case against the sender before the reply arrives. In each of these, you are hearing ninety-nine ewes against one, and your sympathy is making the judgment for you.

There is one part of this worth thinking about carefully. Dāwūd did not catch his mistake by thinking harder. He caught it because he was the kind of man who could still be interrupted. The verdict had already left his mouth, and what saved him was that something in him could still turn around halfway through.

So where do you rule too fast? Name one person, this week, whose case you have already decided without hearing them out. What would it cost you to go back and ask?

Where is the return inside what you've been given?

The third question takes the main idea of the whole course and points it at you. Remember what God did after Dāwūd's mistake. He did not take the kingdom back. Instead He gave Dāwūd the biggest title in the passage, steward of the earth. In the same breath He named the one thing that puts that title at risk: do not follow your own leaning, for it will pull you off the road. The danger came from his own leaning, and the throne itself was never the problem.

So the question was never whether you should give up what you have. You are not being asked to hand back your position, your skill, your voice, your income or your authority over other people, and Dāwūd kept all of his by God's direct command. The question is whether you have a habit of returning built into you, something that can interrupt your own certainty before it hardens. Then, at the moment you are most sure, you can still end up on the floor in prayer.

So you have been given things, but where is the returning inside them? What in your week actually interrupts you, and if the honest answer is nothing, what is the smallest thing that could?

The word was never his alone

Now we come to the fact we have been keeping back.

All through this course, awwāb has seemed like a title, an extraordinary word for an extraordinary man, a prophet and king who softened iron and whose voice made the mountains sing. If you read it that way, it is easy to decide, without quite saying it, that the word has nothing to do with you.

But the Quran uses the word in other places too, and it is worth seeing who it uses it for:

رَّبُّكُمْ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا فِى نُفُوسِكُمْ ۚ إِن تَكُونُوا۟ صَـٰلِحِينَ فَإِنَّهُۥ كَانَ لِلْأَوَّٰبِينَ غَفُورًا

"Your Lord knows best what is within yourselves. If you are righteous — then indeed He is, to the awwābīn, the ones who keep returning, Forgiving."

Al-Isrāʾ (17:25)

Look at where that verse sits. It comes at the end of the passage about parents. That passage tells you not to let even a word of irritation slip out at them, to lower the wing of humility to them, and to pray for the two people who raised you. The commentators say this verse is about someone who snapped at their mother or father and regretted it the moment the words left their mouth. So the setting is an ordinary kitchen, far from any throne room, and a sharp sentence that somebody wishes they could take back.

In that setting, God uses Dāwūd's word in the plural, al-awwābīn, the ones who keep returning. It is the same word, and ordinary people are not given some smaller version of it.

Now read the end of the verse again, because the whole course comes together in its closing words: lil-awwābīna Ghafūrā, which means "to the ones who keep returning, He is Ever-Forgiving". This is the same pairing we saw in Module 3, the two halves of Dāwūd's high point, packed into one short clause: the one who returns, and the One who forgives. Dāwūd fell down bowing, and God said so We forgave him, and when you snap at your mother and then turn back, you meet the same name of God. Every time the Quran shows you someone returning, it also shows you God receiving them. So awwāb was never really a description of special people. It describes what anyone looks like when they take al-Ghafūr, the Ever-Forgiving, seriously.

And the promise is written out for everyone who fits that description:

هَـٰذَا مَا تُوعَدُونَ لِكُلِّ أَوَّابٍ حَفِيظٍ

مَّنْ خَشِىَ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنَ بِٱلْغَيْبِ وَجَآءَ بِقَلْبٍ مُّنِيبٍ

"This is what you were promised — for every awwāb, every keeper: whoever feared the Most Merciful unseen, and came with a heart that turns back."

Qāf (50:32–33)

It says for every awwāb, so the promise is made to everyone who returns and not only to one man. The person it describes is drawn in the very words used for Dāwūd's lowest and highest moment. It is someone who returns, with a heart that turns back, a phrase from the same family of words as the verse where he fell bowing and returned. Paradise is described here as the place where people who keep coming back to God end up.

What the course was for

So this is why the Quran gave us this particular man, complete, with everything at once. If the word had only ever been used for a prophet and king, you could put it aside as something that happens to other people. But it is used for him and it is used for you, and it is the same word. The Quran built the biggest possible example of it, so that when you find the small version in your own life, you would recognize what you are holding.

Dāwūd shows the word at its fullest. He had strength, a kingdom, a book, a voice, a craft and the scales of justice, and he was still a man who could be interrupted in the middle of it all. Your version will be smaller, but it will not be worth less. It might be a sentence you take back, a verdict you reopen, or a day's work done properly when nobody was checking the links. It might be fifteen quiet seconds, at the moment you are most certain, when something in you turns around toward a door that this whole story was told to show you is never closed.

The course deliberately ends here, before the practice itself. Across these six modules we have tried to change what you see, by teaching you to find the returning underneath the gifts, first in his life and then in yours. Building the habit itself is slower work, and we have a separate place for it. Take the three questions with you. They are not homework and there is nothing to hand in, but they are a way to begin.

The mountains had the easy part, because all they had to do was echo. You have to turn back on purpose, and the promise, in plain words, is made to every person who does, by the One whose name is waiting on the other side of the return.
