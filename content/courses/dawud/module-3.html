Module 3 — The One Word

Come to the mountains first, because that is where the Quran wants you standing. Twice a day — at first light and at the edge of dark — a man sings, and the hills sing back. This is not a poetic image someone made up later. The Quran says it plainly: the mountains were made to return his praise with him, and the birds, gathered in the air, joined in. Picture the sound. A whole landscape tuned to one voice. This is Dāwūd. And hidden inside that sound is the word his whole life turns on.

۞ وَلَقَدْ ءَاتَيْنَا دَاوُۥدَ مِنَّا فَضْلًا ۖ يَـٰجِبَالُ أَوِّبِى مَعَهُۥ وَٱلطَّيْرَ ۖ وَأَلَنَّا لَهُ ٱلْحَدِيدَ

"And We gave Dāwūd from Us a favor: O mountains, return [the praise] with him — and the birds. And We softened iron for him."

Sabaʾ (34:10)

A man given everything

You know the list by now — you walked it in Module 1. He killed a giant and was handed a kingdom (2:251). He was made a prophet and given his own book, the Zabūr. He was given the voice the earth answered. He was given iron that softened in his hands like warm clay, and the skill to shape it into armor that saved lives. He was given the wisdom to judge, and the gift of the decisive word — the ability to cut straight to the sentence that settles a matter. Strength, a throne, a book, a voice, a craft, and the scales of justice. If you were building the complete human being, what would you add?

So here is the question from Module 1, ready to be answered. Given a man like that — the fullest portrait of human ability in the whole Quran — what is the one word God chooses to say about him, more than once, as if it were the thing worth remembering? It is not king. It is not warrior, or prophet, or even just. Listen to how the passage in Ṣād opens:

ٱصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَٱذْكُرْ عَبْدَنَا دَاوُۥدَ ذَا ٱلْأَيْدِ ۖ إِنَّهُۥٓ أَوَّابٌ

"Be patient over what they say, and remember Our servant Dāwūd, the possessor of strength. Indeed, he was awwāb — ever-returning."

Ṣād (38:17)

The verse names his strength first — dhā l-ayd, literally "the one with the hands," the man of power. And then, in the same breath, it tells you what actually mattered: innahu awwāb. He was awwāb. The one who keeps coming back.

The word that will not leave him alone

Awwāb is built from a small Arabic root — three letters, a-w-b — that means one simple thing: to return. To turn back toward where you belong. And once you know that, you start hearing the same root everywhere in Dāwūd's story, because the Quran has stitched it through the whole passage like a thread through cloth.

It is his description: awwāb, the returner (38:17) — and the word doesn't stop with him; the chapter keeps sounding it, over the creatures gathered around him (38:19), and later over his son. It is the command given to the mountains: awwibī maʿahu — "return it with him" (34:10). Same root, now spoken to the hills. And it is where his road ends: God promises him ḥusna maʾāb (38:25) — a beautiful place of return. Same root a third time. His character, the mountains' echo, and his final destination are all cut from one word.

Do you see what the mountains actually are now? They take his praise and send it back. He takes everything he was given and sends it back to the One who gave it. The mountains are simply the visible, audible version of what he is. The whole landscape is doing an impression of the man standing in the middle of it.

The summit is not a battle. It is a man bending.

Now the scene his whole life was building toward — and notice what kind of scene the Quran chose for the peak. Not the giant. Not a conquest. A quiet afternoon in a private room, interrupted.

Two men climb the wall into his prayer chamber. He is startled. They say: don't be afraid — we are two men in a dispute; judge between us fairly. One speaks: my brother has ninety-nine ewes, I have one, and he wants mine too. And Dāwūd — the man famous for the decisive word — rules on the spot:

قَالَ لَقَدْ ظَلَمَكَ بِسُؤَالِ نَعْجَتِكَ إِلَىٰ نِعَاجِهِۦ ۖ وَإِنَّ كَثِيرًا مِّنَ ٱلْخُلَطَآءِ لَيَبْغِى بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ وَقَلِيلٌ مَّا هُمْ ۗ وَظَنَّ دَاوُۥدُ أَنَّمَا فَتَنَّـٰهُ فَٱسْتَغْفَرَ رَبَّهُۥ وَخَرَّ رَاكِعًا وَأَنَابَ ۩

"He said: he has certainly wronged you by asking for your ewe on top of his own — and indeed many partners wrong one another, except those who believe and do righteous deeds, and few are they. And Dāwūd realized that We had only tested him — so he asked forgiveness of his Lord, and fell down bowing, and returned [to Him]."

Ṣād (38:24)

Read what actually happens. He hears one side and gives the verdict — he has wronged you — before the second man says a word. And then it lands on him: this was a test. The two who climbed the wall were the exam, and the exam was never about the ewes. It was about whether the most capable judge alive would rule before he listened. He is caught in the most ordinary failure of power there is: deciding too fast, because you are used to being right.

The soundest voices in the tradition — and the plain words of the verse — say the test was the hasty judgment itself. Nothing more. The Quran gives us the slip and stops there. It deliberately leaves out any dramatic backstory, and the leaving-out is part of the lesson. Do not fill a silence God chose to keep.

And watch his response, because it is the whole course in four words: kharra rākiʿan wa-anāb — he fell down bowing, and returned. There is the root one more time, now as an action. The summit of the most powerful life in the Quran is the moment he bends. Not the giant he felled. The knee he dropped.

The other half of the story

Now, the part that is easy to miss — and it is really the point of everything. A return only means something if there is someone on the other end receiving it. Watch what happens the instant he turns back:

فَغَفَرْنَا لَهُۥ ذَٰلِكَ ۖ وَإِنَّ لَهُۥ عِندَنَا لَزُلْفَىٰ وَحُسْنَ مَـَٔابٍ

"So We forgave him that. And indeed, for him with Us is nearness, and a beautiful place of return."

Ṣād (38:25)

No delay. No probation. He turns, and the very next word in the Quran is fa-ghafarnā — "so We forgave." One letter, fa, meaning "so, immediately." The return is answered before the verse can even take a breath.

This is what the story has been showing you all along, underneath the story about him. Dāwūd's returning is only possible because Someone is always there to return to. God is al-Ghafūr — the Ever-Forgiving — and this scene doesn't just say so. It acts it out, in real time, on the most tested man in the book. Every return in this story gets received. The mountains return the praise and it is accepted. The man returns from his mistake and is forgiven mid-fall. The door he keeps walking back to is never once shown closed. The deepest subject of Dāwūd's story is not Dāwūd. It is the One who keeps receiving him.

Why the return keeps the power clean

Then comes the verse that makes it practical. Right after the forgiveness — not a demotion, but a promotion with a warning welded on:

يَـٰدَاوُۥدُ إِنَّا جَعَلْنَـٰكَ خَلِيفَةً فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَٱحْكُم بَيْنَ ٱلنَّاسِ بِٱلْحَقِّ وَلَا تَتَّبِعِ ٱلْهَوَىٰ فَيُضِلَّكَ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ يَضِلُّونَ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌۢ بِمَا نَسُوا۟ يَوْمَ ٱلْحِسَابِ

"O Dāwūd, We have made you a khalīfa on the earth, so judge between people with truth, and do not follow desire — for it will mislead you from the path of God. Indeed, those who stray from the path of God will have a severe punishment for having forgotten the Day of Reckoning."

Ṣād (38:26)

God's answer to the slip is to hand him the largest title in the passage — khalīfa, steward of the earth — and in the same sentence name the one thing that threatens it: hawā. The word means desire, but not really appetite. The commentators read it as the pull of your own preference — the quiet lean that makes you hear a case the way you already want it to come out. That is exactly what nearly tripped him. Not evil. Just the momentum of a man used to his own verdicts.

Now put the two verses together and the logic of the whole life stands clear. Power plus your own leaning equals drifting off the road — that is the warning. And the only thing that empties the leaning back out is the return. That is why God did not name this man king. A king who cannot return will, sooner or later, follow his own tilt off the path, because nothing inside him can interrupt it. The awwāb can hold the throne precisely because he keeps setting it down. His returning is not the opposite of his strength. It is what makes his strength safe to give him.

What the word asks of you

This is why the Quran hands you Dāwūd complete — every gift at once — instead of a man with one virtue. It wanted someone you could not shrug off as simply humble, or simply strong. It wanted the whole thing, so the single word it keeps over him would be unmistakable.

You have been given things too. Maybe not a kingdom — but ability, a position over someone, a voice people listen to, means, skill, the habit of usually being right. The question the word awwāb puts to you is not whether you will give those up. Dāwūd didn't; he was told to keep the throne. The question is whether there is a return built into you — a reflex that can interrupt your own certainty before it hardens. Whether, at the moment you are most sure of your verdict, something in you can still fall down bowing.

The mountains had it easy. They only had to echo. The harder thing — the thing that turned a shepherd into a steward of the earth — was to be strong enough to rule and soft enough, still, to turn back. That is the one word. And on the other side of it, always, the answering one: he kept returning, and God kept receiving. Everything else Dāwūd was given was on loan. The returning is what he did with it.
