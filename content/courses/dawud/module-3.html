Module 3 — The One Word

Let us start with the mountains, because that is where the Quran wants you to stand. Twice a day, at first light and again as it gets dark, a man sings his praise of God and the hills sing it back to him. Nobody invented this later as a poetic image, because the Quran says it plainly. The mountains were made to return his praise with him, and the birds gathered in the air and joined in, until the whole landscape was praising along with one voice. This man is Dāwūd, and the word his whole life turns on is hidden inside that sound.

۞ وَلَقَدْ ءَاتَيْنَا دَاوُۥدَ مِنَّا فَضْلًا ۖ يَـٰجِبَالُ أَوِّبِى مَعَهُۥ وَٱلطَّيْرَ ۖ وَأَلَنَّا لَهُ ٱلْحَدِيدَ

"And We gave Dāwūd from Us a favor: O mountains, return [the praise] with him — and the birds. And We softened iron for him."

Sabaʾ (34:10)

A man given everything

You already know the list, because we went through it in Module 1. He killed a giant and was given a kingdom (2:251). He was made a prophet and given his own book, the Zabūr, and a voice that the earth answered. Iron softened in his hands like warm clay, and he had the skill to shape it into armor that saved lives. He was also given the wisdom to judge, and the gift of the decisive word, which means the ability to go straight to the one sentence that settles a matter. So he had strength, a throne, a book, a voice, a craft and the scales of justice, and if you were building the complete human being, it is hard to know what you would add.

Now we can answer the question from Module 1. He is the fullest picture of human ability anywhere in the Quran, so what is the one word God chooses to say about him more than once, as if it were the thing most worth remembering? The answer is none of king, warrior, prophet, or even just. Here is how the passage in Ṣād opens:

ٱصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَٱذْكُرْ عَبْدَنَا دَاوُۥدَ ذَا ٱلْأَيْدِ ۖ إِنَّهُۥٓ أَوَّابٌ

"Be patient over what they say, and remember Our servant Dāwūd, the possessor of strength. Indeed, he was awwāb — ever-returning."

Ṣād (38:17)

The verse mentions his strength first, calling him dhā l-ayd, which literally means "the one with the hands", the man of power. Then, in the same breath, it tells you what really mattered about him: innahu awwāb. He was awwāb, the one who keeps coming back.

The word that will not leave him alone

Awwāb comes from a small Arabic root of three letters, a-w-b, and that root means one simple thing: to return, or to turn back toward where you belong. Once you know this, you start hearing the same root all through Dāwūd's story, because the Quran has woven it into the whole passage.

First, it describes him, since he is awwāb, the one who returns (38:17). The chapter then keeps using the word for the creatures gathered around him (38:19), and later for his son as well. Second, it is the command God gives to the mountains, awwibī maʿahu, "return it with him" (34:10), so the same root is now spoken to the hills. Third, it names where his road ends, because God promises him ḥusna maʾāb (38:25), a beautiful place of return. So his character, the mountains' echo and his final home are all described with one and the same word.

This helps you see what the mountains are doing. They take his praise and send it back, just as he takes everything he was given and sends it back to the One who gave it. In the mountains you can see and hear what kind of man he is, because the landscape around him is doing the same thing he does.

The summit is a man bending

Now we come to the scene his whole life was building toward, and it is worth noticing what kind of scene the Quran chose for the high point. The Quran passes over the fight with the giant and every conquest, and chooses a quiet afternoon in a private room, when someone interrupts him.

Two men climb the wall into his prayer room, and Dāwūd is frightened. They tell him don't be afraid, because they are two men with a dispute and want him to judge between them fairly. One of them explains that his brother has ninety-nine ewes while he has only one, and his brother wants that one too. Dāwūd, the man famous for the decisive word, gives his ruling on the spot:

قَالَ لَقَدْ ظَلَمَكَ بِسُؤَالِ نَعْجَتِكَ إِلَىٰ نِعَاجِهِۦ ۖ وَإِنَّ كَثِيرًا مِّنَ ٱلْخُلَطَآءِ لَيَبْغِى بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ وَقَلِيلٌ مَّا هُمْ ۗ وَظَنَّ دَاوُۥدُ أَنَّمَا فَتَنَّـٰهُ فَٱسْتَغْفَرَ رَبَّهُۥ وَخَرَّ رَاكِعًا وَأَنَابَ ۩

"He said: he has certainly wronged you by asking for your ewe on top of his own — and indeed many partners wrong one another, except those who believe and do righteous deeds, and few are they. And Dāwūd realized that We had only tested him — so he asked forgiveness of his Lord, and fell down bowing, and returned [to Him]."

Ṣād (38:24)

Look closely at what happens. He hears one side and gives his verdict, he has wronged you, before the second man has said a word. Then he realizes that this was a test. The two men who climbed the wall had come to test him, and the test had nothing to do with the ewes. It was about whether the most capable judge alive would give a ruling before he had listened. He was caught in the most ordinary mistake that powerful people make, which is deciding too fast because you are used to being right.

The most reliable scholars in the tradition, together with the plain words of the verse, say that the test was this hasty judgment and nothing more. The Quran tells us about the mistake and stops there, and it deliberately leaves out any dramatic backstory. That silence is part of the lesson, because we should not fill in a gap that God chose to leave empty.

His response is described in a few short words, kharra rākiʿan wa-anāb, which mean that he fell down bowing and returned. So the idea of returning appears once more, this time as something he does. The high point of the most powerful life in the Quran is the moment he bows down, and that moment counts for more than the giant he killed.

The other half of the story

Now comes the part that is easy to miss, even though it is really the point of everything. Returning only means something if there is someone on the other side to receive you, so look at what happens the moment he turns back:

فَغَفَرْنَا لَهُۥ ذَٰلِكَ ۖ وَإِنَّ لَهُۥ عِندَنَا لَزُلْفَىٰ وَحُسْنَ مَـَٔابٍ

"So We forgave him that. And indeed, for him with Us is nearness, and a beautiful place of return."

Ṣād (38:25)

He does not have to wait or prove himself first. As soon as he turns, the very next word in the Quran is fa-ghafarnā, "so We forgave", and the single letter fa at the front carries the sense of "so, straight away". God answers his return before the verse has even paused.

Underneath the story about Dāwūd, this is what it has been showing you all along. Dāwūd can only keep returning because Someone is always there to return to. God is al-Ghafūr, the Ever-Forgiving, and in this scene we watch that happen in real time, to the most tested man in the book. Every return in this story is received: the mountains return his praise and it is accepted, and the man returns from his mistake and is forgiven even as he falls to the ground. The door he keeps coming back to is never once shown closed. So the deepest subject of this story is the One who keeps welcoming him back.

Why the return keeps the power clean

The next verse makes all of this practical. Right after forgiving him, God gives him a higher rank, and a warning comes attached to it:

يَـٰدَاوُۥدُ إِنَّا جَعَلْنَـٰكَ خَلِيفَةً فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَٱحْكُم بَيْنَ ٱلنَّاسِ بِٱلْحَقِّ وَلَا تَتَّبِعِ ٱلْهَوَىٰ فَيُضِلَّكَ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ يَضِلُّونَ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌۢ بِمَا نَسُوا۟ يَوْمَ ٱلْحِسَابِ

"O Dāwūd, We have made you a khalīfa on the earth, so judge between people with truth, and do not follow desire — for it will mislead you from the path of God. Indeed, those who stray from the path of God will have a severe punishment for having forgotten the Day of Reckoning."

Ṣād (38:26)

After his mistake, God gives him the biggest title in the passage, khalīfa, steward of the earth. In the same sentence He names the one thing that could put it at risk, which is hawā. The word means desire, but here it does not really mean appetite. The commentators understand it as the pull of your own preference, the quiet leaning that makes you hear a case the way you already want it to turn out. That is exactly what nearly tripped Dāwūd up. It is not something evil, just the habit of a man who is used to his own verdicts being right.

When you put these two verses together, the logic of his whole life becomes clear. The warning is that power combined with your own leaning will pull you off the road, and the only thing that corrects that leaning is turning back to God. That is why God did not call this man king. A king who cannot turn back will sooner or later follow his own leaning off the path, because nothing inside him can stop it. The awwāb can safely hold the throne because he keeps turning back to God instead of trusting his own judgment. That habit of returning is what makes it safe to give him so much strength.

What the word asks of you

This is why the Quran shows you Dāwūd complete, with every gift at once, instead of a man with just one good quality. It wanted someone you could not simply dismiss as humble or as strong. It gave you the whole man, so that the one word it keeps using about him would be impossible to miss.

You have been given things too. Maybe you do not have a kingdom, but you may have ability, authority over someone, a voice people listen to, money, skill, or the habit of usually being right. The word awwāb does not ask whether you will give those things up, and Dāwūd never did, since he was told to keep the throne. It asks whether you have a habit of turning back built into you, something that can interrupt your own certainty before it hardens. It asks whether, at the moment you are most sure of your verdict, some part of you can still fall down and bow.

The mountains had the easy part, because all they had to do was echo. The harder thing, the thing that turned a shepherd into a steward of the earth, was being strong enough to rule while staying soft enough to turn back. That is the one word, and whenever he used it, God was always there to answer. He kept returning, and God kept receiving him. Everything else Dāwūd was given was only lent to him, and his returning was what he did with it.
