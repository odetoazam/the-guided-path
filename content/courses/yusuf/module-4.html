Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You

The best of stories is also the Quran's most famous exercise in withholding. Names you would expect are missing. Years are left uncounted. One verse at the story's centre says something about its hero that scholars have stood around for thirteen centuries. This module looks at the gaps on purpose, because in this book what is withheld is part of the teaching.

The names that are not there

The woman is never named. The name the tradition knows her by appears nowhere in the Quran, and that has been checked against every verse. She is the one in whose house he was (12:23) and the wife of al-ʿAzīz (12:30, 12:51). Those are titles rather than a name, and they do work a name would not, because they keep saying power. She is his owner's wife, the mistress of the house he cannot leave. The titles keep your eye on what the scene was: a person with total leverage propositioning a person with none.

The king is never named. There is no pharaoh's name and no dynasty. There is a quiet pattern in the wording here. In this sūrah the ruler is always al-malik, the king, and the word firʿawn never occurs, while Mūsā's Egypt gets firʿawn throughout. Ibn ʿĀshūr counts that distinction among the Quran's marks of historical exactness, while noting that the matter is much disputed and that the earlier commentators spoke freely of "Yūsuf's pharaoh." The famine gets no date. The prison years get only a phrase, biḍʿ sinīn, some years.

Even the well is almost anonymous. It is ghayābat al-jubb, the unseen depth of the cistern, on some unnamed caravan road.

Add the withholdings together and the pattern is the one every course on this site keeps finding. The Quran removes exactly the details that would let you file the story away as somewhere else, someone else, back then. What is left is a portable diagram, with a dreamer, an envy, a pit, a temptation, a cell and a throne, and every name stripped out so that your own can be written in.

The hard verse

This is the verse at the story's centre of gravity, and the reason this module exists:

وَلَقَدْ هَمَّتْ بِهِۦ ۖ وَهَمَّ بِهَا لَوْلَآ أَن رَّءَا بُرْهَـٰنَ رَبِّهِۦ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ لِنَصْرِفَ عَنْهُ ٱلسُّوٓءَ وَٱلْفَحْشَآءَ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا ٱلْمُخْلَصِينَ

"And she certainly determined upon him; and he would have inclined to her had he not seen the proof of his Lord. Thus it was, that We should avert from him evil and immorality. Indeed, he was of Our chosen servants."

Yūsuf (12:24)

Wa-laqad hammat bihi — wa-hamma bihā lawlā an raʾā burhāna rabbihi. There is her hamm, her resolve upon him. Then there is his hamm, which hangs on a lawlā: "had he not seen the proof of his Lord." So what exactly is being said about Yūsuf? Here is the table, with the scholars sitting at it.

Start where there is no disagreement. Al-Qurṭubī sets a boundary before anything else. There is no dispute, he says, that her hamm was for the sin. Every debate that follows is about Yūsuf's clause alone.

The first position: two hamms, and the second one checked. This is the reading of the early transmitted commentary, and al-Ṭabarī does more than preserve it. He rules for it. The verse reports a hamm from each of them, and Yūsuf's was halted by the proof. He takes the obvious objection in his own voice: how can this be said of a prophet? The people of knowledge differed on that, he says. Some held that God allows His prophets a tested moment, precisely so that they stay in awe of Him and never presume on the breadth of His forgiveness. On this reading the verse records a human being, genuinely human and genuinely young and genuinely alone, in whom something moved and was overruled the moment the proof appeared. Some of the reports under this position go much further than an inward motion, and named scholars reject those outright. Al-Shinqīṭī grades them as isrāʾīliyyāt and says one must not dare to say such things of a prophet of God. Abū Ḥayyān calls them mutually contradicting fabrications. The position does not need them, and neither do we.

The second position: the hamm never happened at all. Al-Qurṭubī presents this line and ties it to the protection of prophets. On it, the lawlā governs the whole clause, so the sense is that he would have inclined had he not seen the proof, and since he did see it, the inclination never occurred. Al-Ṭabarī attacks this reading on grammar. Arabic, he objects, does not place the answer of lawlā before it, and he counts those who hold it as departing from the early authorities. The argument did not end with him. Ibn ʿĀshūr takes up al-Ṭabarī's objection by name and answers it, defending the construction. He reads the forces in the scene accordingly. The pull was real and fully supplied, and the burhān was the barrier standing between Yūsuf and the hamm itself. So it is grammar against grammar, master against master, and the dispute is alive on both sides of the page.

The burhān itself is a second dispute. What did he see? Al-Baghawī reports that Qatāda and most of the commentators said it was the image of his father Yaʿqūb, and al-Baghawī names it as a transmitted report, with other early names offering variants. Ibn ʿĀshūr surveys the positions and sorts them into four kinds: a rational proof that made the act ugly to him, a divine revelation, a divine protection, or something shown to him. The Quran itself gives the burhān no content at all. It tells you that it was of his Lord, and stops there.

One thing needs saying about the English above. The translation under the Arabic, "he would have inclined to her had he not seen the proof", already renders the second position. Arabic can hold the ambiguity in a way an English sentence cannot, so every translation of this verse has already chosen a side. Now you know which side yours chose.

On every reading, the verse ends the same way. Read the second half again: thus it was, that We should avert from him evil and immorality; indeed he was of Our chosen servants. Whatever the hamm was, God's own verdict closes the verse. The evil was turned away, and We turned it, and the man is declared mukhlaṣ, one of the chosen. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the reciters carry both vowels here, mukhlaṣīn, the ones God chose, and mukhliṣīn, the sincere ones, which is the chosen and the choosing as two readings of one rasm. So the verse that raises the hardest question in the sūrah carries its own acquittal, in God's voice, in the same breath. Anyone who quotes its first half without its second has quoted half a sentence.

Her file is closed by the text

There is one more thing the Quran does here that the retellings usually undo. Late in the story, the woman speaks for herself, unprompted, in front of the king:

قَالَ مَا خَطْبُكُنَّ إِذْ رَٰوَدتُّنَّ يُوسُفَ عَن نَّفْسِهِۦ ۚ قُلْنَ حَـٰشَ لِلَّهِ مَا عَلِمْنَا عَلَيْهِ مِن سُوٓءٍ ۚ قَالَتِ ٱمْرَأَتُ ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْـَٔـٰنَ حَصْحَصَ ٱلْحَقُّ أَنَا۠ رَٰوَدتُّهُۥ عَن نَّفْسِهِۦ وَإِنَّهُۥ لَمِنَ ٱلصَّـٰدِقِينَ

"He said: What was your case when you sought to seduce Yūsuf? They said: God forbid! We know not of any evil concerning him. The wife of al-ʿAzīz said: Now the truth has become evident: it was I who sought to seduce him, and indeed he is of the truthful."

Yūsuf (12:51)

Now the truth has become evident. It is a full confession, in open court, and it clears him completely. The Quran gives her that moment of truth-telling as her last word in the story. She enters the record wanting him, and she leaves it testifying for him. The book that never named her also never closed the door on her honesty. The course follows the book, so her file ends here.
