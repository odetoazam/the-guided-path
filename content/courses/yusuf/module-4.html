Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You

The best of stories is also the story where the Quran most famously holds things back. Names you would expect are missing, and years are left uncounted. One verse at the centre of the story says something about its hero that scholars have been discussing for thirteen centuries. This module looks at these gaps on purpose, because in the Quran the things left out are part of the teaching.

The names that are not there

The woman is never named. The name that later tradition knows her by does not appear anywhere in the Quran, and that has been checked against every verse. The Quran calls her the one in whose house he was (12:23) and the wife of al-ʿAzīz (12:30, 12:51). These are titles rather than a name. They do something a name would not do, because they keep reminding you of her power. She is his owner's wife and the mistress of a house he cannot leave. So the titles keep your eye on what was really happening in that scene. A person with complete control over someone was trying to seduce a person who had no power at all.

The king is never named. We are given no pharaoh's name and no dynasty. There is a quiet pattern in the wording here. In this sūrah the ruler is always called al-malik, "the king," and the word firʿawn never appears. In the story of Mūsā's Egypt the ruler is called firʿawn throughout. Ibn ʿĀshūr counts this difference among the signs of the Quran's historical accuracy. He also notes that the matter is much disputed, and that the earlier commentators spoke freely of "Yūsuf's pharaoh." The famine is given no date. The prison years are given only a phrase: biḍʿ sinīn, "some years."

Even the well is almost anonymous. It is called ghayābat al-jubb, the hidden depth of the well, on some unnamed caravan road.

Put all these missing details together and you find the same pattern that every course on this site keeps finding. The Quran leaves out exactly the details that would let you think of the story as far away. Without them you cannot say it happened to someone else, somewhere else, long ago. The story that remains fits any life. It has a dreamer and envy, a pit and a temptation, a prison cell and a throne. Every name is taken out so that you can put your own in.

The hard verse

This verse sits at the heart of the story, and it is the reason this module exists:

وَلَقَدْ هَمَّتْ بِهِۦ ۖ وَهَمَّ بِهَا لَوْلَآ أَن رَّءَا بُرْهَـٰنَ رَبِّهِۦ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ لِنَصْرِفَ عَنْهُ ٱلسُّوٓءَ وَٱلْفَحْشَآءَ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا ٱلْمُخْلَصِينَ

"And she certainly determined upon him; and he would have inclined to her had he not seen the proof of his Lord. Thus it was, that We should avert from him evil and immorality. Indeed, he was of Our chosen servants."

Yūsuf (12:24)

Wa-laqad hammat bihi, wa-hamma bihā lawlā an raʾā burhāna rabbihi. First comes her hamm, her firm intention toward him. Then comes his hamm, which depends on the word lawlā, "had he not." The full phrase reads "had he not seen the proof of his Lord" in English. So what exactly is the verse saying about Yūsuf? Here is what the scholars said, name by name.

Start where there is no disagreement. Al-Qurṭubī draws a clear line before anything else. He says there is no dispute that her hamm was an intention to sin. Every argument that follows is only about the part of the verse that describes Yūsuf.

The first position: two hamms, and the second one checked. This is how the early commentators read the verse in the reports passed down from them. Al-Ṭabarī does more than record their view, because he rules in favour of it. On this view the verse reports a hamm from each of them, and Yūsuf's was stopped by the proof. Al-Ṭabarī raises the obvious objection himself: how can this be said about a prophet? He answers that the people of knowledge differed on this. Some held that God lets His prophets go through a moment of testing. He does this so that they stay in awe of Him and never take the vastness of His forgiveness for granted. On this reading the verse describes a real human being who was young and alone. He felt something stir inside him, and that feeling was overruled the moment the proof appeared. Some of the reports under this position go much further than a feeling inside him. Named scholars reject those reports completely. Al-Shinqīṭī classes them as isrāʾīliyyāt, which are stories borrowed from earlier traditions. He says no one should dare to say such things about a prophet of God. Abū Ḥayyān calls them fabrications that contradict one another. This position does not need those reports, and neither do we.

The second position: the hamm never happened at all. Al-Qurṭubī presents this view and connects it to the belief that prophets are protected from sin. On this view the lawlā controls the whole clause. So the meaning is that he would have inclined to her if he had not seen the proof. He did see it, so the inclination never happened. Al-Ṭabarī argues against this reading on grammatical grounds. He objects that Arabic does not put the answer to a lawlā clause before the lawlā itself. He also says the people who hold this view are departing from the early authorities. But the argument did not end with him. Ibn ʿĀshūr answers al-Ṭabarī's objection and mentions him by name, and he defends the grammar of this reading. Then he explains the scene in that light. The pull toward her was real and fully present. The burhān, the proof, was the barrier that stood between Yūsuf and the hamm itself. So on both sides great scholars are making grammatical arguments against each other, and the disagreement is still very much alive.

The burhān itself is a second dispute. What was the proof that he saw? Al-Baghawī reports that Qatāda and most of the commentators said it was the image of his father Yaʿqūb. Al-Baghawī presents this as a transmitted report, and other early scholars offered different versions. Ibn ʿĀshūr goes through all the positions and sorts them into four kinds. The first is a rational proof that made the act seem ugly to him. The others are a revelation from God, a protection from God, or something that was shown to him. The Quran itself does not tell us what the burhān was. It only says that it was of his Lord, and it says nothing more.

You should also know something about the English translation above. It reads "he would have inclined to her had he not seen the proof", which already follows the second position. Arabic can hold both meanings at once in a way an English sentence cannot, so every translation of this verse has already had to choose a side. Now you know which side yours chose.

On every reading, the verse ends the same way. Read the second half again: thus it was, that We should avert from him evil and immorality; indeed he was of Our chosen servants. Whatever the hamm was, the verse closes with God's own verdict. The evil was turned away from him, and it was God who turned it away. Yūsuf is declared mukhlaṣ, one of the chosen. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the reciters read this word with two different vowels. One reading is mukhlaṣīn, which means the ones God chose. The other is mukhliṣīn, which means the sincere ones. So the same written word can mean both that God chose him and that he was sincere. In this way the verse that raises the hardest question in the sūrah also clears Yūsuf's name. It does so in God's own voice and in the very same breath. Anyone who quotes the first half of this verse without the second half has only quoted half a sentence.

Her file is closed by the text

The Quran does one more thing here that retellings of the story usually undo. Late in the story the woman speaks up for herself in front of the king, and nobody forces her to:

قَالَ مَا خَطْبُكُنَّ إِذْ رَٰوَدتُّنَّ يُوسُفَ عَن نَّفْسِهِۦ ۚ قُلْنَ حَـٰشَ لِلَّهِ مَا عَلِمْنَا عَلَيْهِ مِن سُوٓءٍ ۚ قَالَتِ ٱمْرَأَتُ ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْـَٔـٰنَ حَصْحَصَ ٱلْحَقُّ أَنَا۠ رَٰوَدتُّهُۥ عَن نَّفْسِهِۦ وَإِنَّهُۥ لَمِنَ ٱلصَّـٰدِقِينَ

"He said: What was your case when you sought to seduce Yūsuf? They said: God forbid! We know not of any evil concerning him. The wife of al-ʿAzīz said: Now the truth has become evident: it was I who sought to seduce him, and indeed he is of the truthful."

Yūsuf (12:51)

When she says now the truth has become evident, she is making a full confession in public. It clears him completely. The Quran lets this honest moment be her last word in the story. She first appears wanting him, and she last appears speaking up for him. The Quran never gave her a name, but it also never took away the honesty she showed at the end. This course follows the Quran, so her part of the story ends here.
