Module 5 — Your Own Wasted Years

Every course here ends facing the learner, and this one has an easier turn than most, because you did not need help locating yourself in it. Somewhere in your life there is a well, or a false accusation, or a stretch of years that sits in your memory like the dungeon. It is time the story of your life seems to have thrown away. This module is about those years. There are three teachers left to hear from: the father, the son, and a sentence said twice.

The father: what patience actually permits

Yaʿqūb is this story's other sufferer, and he is the tradition's byword for patience. His phrase from the night of the false blood, ṣabrun jamīl, a beautiful patience, is the standard the Quran sets. So it matters what this most patient of men says when his sons tell him to stop grieving:

قَالَ إِنَّمَآ أَشْكُوا۟ بَثِّى وَحُزْنِىٓ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ وَأَعْلَمُ مِنَ ٱللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

"He said: I only complain of my suffering and my grief to God, and I know from God that which you do not know."

Yūsuf (12:86)

Two facts sit together here. The man of ṣabrun jamīl wept until his eyes whitened, and he describes himself as full of bathth, which is anguish that spills over, and ḥuzn, grief. His own standard survives intact, because of where the complaint is aimed. It goes ilā llāh, to God. So the patience the Quran praises is grief with a correct address rather than the suppression of grief. You are allowed to be destroyed by your losses in the direction of God. What ṣabr will not do is file the complaint against Him with the neighbours, and Yaʿqūb never once does that. His whole remaining hope sits in the next clause, I know from God what you do not know.

The sentence said twice

The night the brothers bring the bloodied shirt, Yaʿqūb says: bal sawwalat lakum anfusukum amrā — fa-ṣabrun jamīl. "Rather, your souls have enticed you to something, so a beautiful patience." Decades later the youngest son is detained in Egypt, and the brothers come home diminished again. The old man says it once more:

قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا ۖ فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ ۖ عَسَى ٱللَّهُ أَن يَأْتِيَنِى بِهِمْ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلْعَلِيمُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

"He said: Rather, your souls have enticed you to something, so patience is most fitting. Perhaps God will bring them to me all together. Indeed, it is He who is the Knowing, the Wise."

Yūsuf (12:83)

The two openings are identical, word for word, and the corpus confirms it: bal sawwalat lakum anfusukum amrā, fa-ṣabrun jamīl. After that, each one turns to God differently. The first night's turn was for endurance: God is the one whose help is sought against what you describe. The second, decades and another loss later, turns toward something larger: perhaps God will bring them to me, all of them, together. The first ṣabr held one loss. The second holds three, which are Yūsuf, the youngest son now detained, and the eldest who refused to come home without him. The commentators count those three by name under this verse, and al-Muyassar explains it plainly as my three sons. The hope in the second is also wider than the hope in the first. It asks for the whole table restored rather than for word that Yūsuf is alive. That is what decades inside a beautiful patience did to this man. The losses doubled, and the asking grew. Whatever your own years in the dark are doing to the size of your asking, this is the direction the story recommends.

The son: what the summit makes possible

When the restoration comes, the man who was wronged first and longest gets the last word over the men who started it:

قَالَ لَا تَثْرِيبَ عَلَيْكُمُ ٱلْيَوْمَ ۖ يَغْفِرُ ٱللَّهُ لَكُمْ ۖ وَهُوَ أَرْحَمُ ٱلرَّٰحِمِينَ

"He said: No blame will there be upon you today. God will forgive you; and He is the most merciful of the merciful."

Yūsuf (12:92)

Lā tathrība ʿalaykumu l-yawm. No blame, today. The day he holds all the power is the day the account is torn up. Something made the tearing light. A man who genuinely believes the well was freight, and that every link of the chain was carriage, has less and less reason to keep the ledger open on the people who forged the links. His forgiveness is his theology arriving at its conclusion in front of witnesses. Tell yourself the story of your worst years the way Module 3 taught you, and certain old debts start to look payable.

Three questions

1. Which link are you standing on? Yūsuf's chain only showed its shape from the throne. From inside the well it looked like a well. If your current stretch looks like pure waste, that is how every link in his chain looked at the time. The narrator's thus was spoken over the burial scenes as well as the harvest ones. You cannot see your chain from the middle of it. That was true of him too.

2. Where is your complaint addressed? The question is not whether you have stopped grieving, because Yaʿqūb never stopped. Read your grief's address line. If your anguish spills toward everyone except God, redirect the mail. The door of 12:86 is open, and the most patient man in the Quran used it freely.

3. What would you do in the dungeon? Yūsuf spent his prison years answering the two men in front of him, because his working assumption was that placement is assignment. Somewhere in your own cell there is a person in front of you and a task-sized reason you might be there. The muḥsin tag was earned in rooms like that, before any throne confirmed it.

The close

Look back down the whole course. A boy is loved, envied, thrown away, sold cheap, tempted, framed, jailed and forgotten. A word follows him through all of it that strangers keep saying without knowing they are quoting each other. A narrator keeps saying thus over the disasters. At the top, a man surveys the entire supply line that had been disguised as his ruin. He gives it a name: my Lord is Laṭīf in what He wills, the kindness too fine to see while it is working.

The other courses on this shelf each end on a divine name their story showed. Al-Ghafūr, whose forgiveness receives every returner. Al-Ghanī, too rich to need the thanks He asks for. Al-Tawwāb, who turns toward the turner first. Al-ʿAzīz, whose might the enemy himself swore by. This one ends on the name for the kindness you cannot yet see from where you stand, which is why it is the name this course sends you home with. Al-Laṭīf does not mean your years will explain themselves on schedule. It means the explanation exists, and is kind, and is already in motion. It also means that the only version of you who gets to say nothing was wasted over the whole chain is the one who keeps being a muḥsin inside the link you are standing on.

God does not waste the reward of the muḥsinīn. He said it over Yūsuf's life. Yūsuf said it back over his own. The sentence is still true, and it has room in it for yours.
