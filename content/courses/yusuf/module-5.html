Module 5 — Your Own Wasted Years

Every course here ends by turning to the learner. This one has an easier turn than most, because you have probably already found yourself in it. Somewhere in your life there is a well or a false accusation. Or there is a stretch of years that sits in your memory like that dungeon, time your life seems to have thrown away. This module is about those years. We will hear from three last teachers: the father, the son and a sentence that is said twice.

The father: what patience actually permits

Yaʿqūb is the other person who suffers in this story, and the tradition holds him up as the great example of patience. On the night of the false blood he used the phrase ṣabrun jamīl, "a beautiful patience." That phrase is the standard the Quran sets. So it matters what this most patient of men says when his sons tell him to stop grieving:

قَالَ إِنَّمَآ أَشْكُوا۟ بَثِّى وَحُزْنِىٓ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ وَأَعْلَمُ مِنَ ٱللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

"He said: I only complain of my suffering and my grief to God, and I know from God that which you do not know."

Yūsuf (12:86)

Two things are true of him at the same time. The man of ṣabrun jamīl cried until his eyes turned white. He describes himself as full of bathth and ḥuzn. Bathth is anguish that spills over, and ḥuzn is grief. Yet he still lives up to his own standard because of where he takes his complaint: ilā llāh, to God. So the patience the Quran praises means taking your grief to the right place rather than holding it in. You are allowed to be crushed by your losses, as long as you bring that pain to God. Ṣabr will not complain about God to other people, and Yaʿqūb never once does that. All the hope he has left is in the next part of the verse: I know from God what you do not know.

The sentence said twice

On the night the brothers bring home the bloodied shirt, Yaʿqūb says: bal sawwalat lakum anfusukum amrā, fa-ṣabrun jamīl. It means "Rather, your souls have enticed you to something, so a beautiful patience". Decades later the youngest son is held back in Egypt, and the brothers come home once again with someone missing. Then the old man says it again:

قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا ۖ فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ ۖ عَسَى ٱللَّهُ أَن يَأْتِيَنِى بِهِمْ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلْعَلِيمُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

"He said: Rather, your souls have enticed you to something, so patience is most fitting. Perhaps God will bring them to me all together. Indeed, it is He who is the Knowing, the Wise."

Yūsuf (12:83)

Check the text and you will find the two openings are identical word for word: bal sawwalat lakum anfusukum amrā, fa-ṣabrun jamīl. After that, each one turns to God in a different way. On the first night he asks for strength to endure: God is the one whose help is sought against what you describe. The second time comes decades and another loss later. Now he hopes for something bigger: perhaps God will bring them to me, all of them, together. The first time his patience carried one loss. The second time it carries three: Yūsuf, the youngest son who is now held in Egypt, and the eldest son who refused to come home without him. The commentators count those three by name under this verse, and al-Muyassar explains it simply as "my three sons." His hope is also wider the second time. He is no longer asking just for news that Yūsuf is alive. He asks for the whole family to be together again. Decades of beautiful patience did this to the man. His losses grew, and so did what he asked God for. Think about what your own years in the dark are doing to how much you ask God for. The story points you in this direction.

The son: what the summit makes possible

When everything is finally restored, the man who was wronged first and longest gets the last word over the brothers who started it all:

قَالَ لَا تَثْرِيبَ عَلَيْكُمُ ٱلْيَوْمَ ۖ يَغْفِرُ ٱللَّهُ لَكُمْ ۖ وَهُوَ أَرْحَمُ ٱلرَّٰحِمِينَ

"He said: No blame will there be upon you today. God will forgive you; and He is the most merciful of the merciful."

Yūsuf (12:92)

Lā tathrība ʿalaykumu l-yawm means "no blame upon you today." He has all the power on that day, and on that very day he lets the whole debt go. Something made that easy for him. A man may truly believe that the well carried him forward and that every step of the chain was bringing him somewhere. Then he has less and less reason to keep holding a grudge against the people who caused those steps. His forgiveness is simply his belief about God, acted out in front of everyone. If you tell yourself the story of your worst years the way Module 3 taught you, some old debts may start to feel easier to let go.

Three questions

1. Which link are you standing on? Yūsuf could only see the shape of his chain once he reached the throne, and from inside the well it just looked like a well. If the stretch you are in right now looks like pure waste, remember that every step of his chain looked like that at the time. The Quran said thus over the scenes that looked like burial as well as the ones that looked like harvest. You cannot see your whole chain while you are in the middle of it, and neither could he.

2. Where is your complaint addressed? You do not have to stop grieving, since Yaʿqūb never stopped. But look at who you take your grief to. If your pain pours out to everyone except God, turn it toward Him instead. The door of 12:86 is open, and the most patient man in the Quran went through it freely.

3. What would you do in the dungeon? Yūsuf spent his prison years helping the two men in front of him. He assumed that wherever God had placed him, there was something for him to do. Somewhere in your own prison there is probably a person in front of you. There is probably also a small and manageable reason you might be there. Yūsuf was called a muḥsin in rooms like that, long before any throne proved it.

The close

Look back over the whole course. A boy is loved and envied, then thrown away and sold cheaply. He is tempted and framed, then jailed and forgotten. Through all of it one word follows him. Strangers say it without knowing they are repeating each other, and the Quran keeps saying thus over each disaster. At the end a man looks back over everything that had looked like his ruin. He sees that it was what carried him, and he gives it a name: my Lord is Laṭīf in what He wills. Laṭīf is a kindness so fine that you cannot see it while it is working.

The other courses on this site each end on a name of God that their story showed. Al-Ghafūr is the One whose forgiveness welcomes everyone who returns. Al-Ghanī is so rich that He does not need the thanks He asks for. Al-Tawwāb turns toward the person who repents before they turn to Him. Al-ʿAzīz is the One whose might even the enemy swore by. This course ends on the name for the kindness you cannot yet see from where you are standing. That is why it is the name we send you home with. Al-Laṭīf does not promise that your years will explain themselves when you want them to. It means the explanation exists and is kind, and that it is already on its way. It also means that one day a version of you may be able to say nothing was wasted about your whole life. That will only be the one who keeps being a muḥsin in the step you are standing on right now.

God does not waste the reward of the muḥsinīn. He said it over Yūsuf's life, and Yūsuf said it back about his own. The promise is still true, and there is room in it for your life too.
