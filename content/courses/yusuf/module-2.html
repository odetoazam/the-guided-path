Module 2 — The Locked Room and the Dungeon

Two scenes, up close. In the first, Yūsuf loses his freedom for refusing to do wrong. In the second, he shows you what a person who trusts the Weaver does with a dungeon. Together they answer a question Module 1 left open: the narrator keeps saying God is arranging things — so what does that look like from inside, on the days when the arrangement looks like ruin?

Scene one: the locked room

وَرَٰوَدَتْهُ ٱلَّتِى هُوَ فِى بَيْتِهَا عَن نَّفْسِهِۦ وَغَلَّقَتِ ٱلْأَبْوَٰبَ وَقَالَتْ هَيْتَ لَكَ ۚ قَالَ مَعَاذَ ٱللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ رَبِّىٓ أَحْسَنَ مَثْوَاىَ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ لَا يُفْلِحُ ٱلظَّـٰلِمُونَ

"And she, in whose house he was, sought to seduce him. She closed the doors and said: Come, you. He said: I seek the refuge of God. Indeed, he is my master, who has made good my residence. Indeed, wrongdoers will not succeed."

Yūsuf (12:23)

Weigh the scene honestly. He is young, enslaved, far from anyone who knows him. She holds all the power in his life. The doors are locked — the verb is intensive, door after door. There is no witness, no consequence visible, and every earthly incentive points one way.

His answer is three sentences, and each does distinct work. Maʿādha llāh — refuge with God, the door that is never locked, named first, before any reasoning. Then a reasoning of loyalty — he is my master who honoured my residence; I will not repay shelter with betrayal. Then the principle over both: wrongdoers do not succeed. Refuge, gratitude, principle — in that order. He does not negotiate, and he does not linger to test his strength; the next verse has him racing her to the door.

The shirt speaks again

At the door they meet her husband, and she accuses first: prison or painful punishment for the one who meant evil against your family. Yūsuf states the truth in four words — hiya rāwadatnī ʿan nafsī, it was she who sought me. And then the story produces the strangest witness in the Quran:

قَالَ هِىَ رَٰوَدَتْنِى عَن نَّفْسِى ۚ وَشَهِدَ شَاهِدٌ مِّنْ أَهْلِهَآ إِن كَانَ قَمِيصُهُۥ قُدَّ مِن قُبُلٍ فَصَدَقَتْ وَهُوَ مِنَ ٱلْكَـٰذِبِينَ وَإِن كَانَ قَمِيصُهُۥ قُدَّ مِن دُبُرٍ فَكَذَبَتْ وَهُوَ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰدِقِينَ

"He said: It was she who sought to seduce me. And a witness from her family testified: If his shirt is torn from the front, then she has told the truth and he is of the liars. But if his shirt is torn from the back, then she has lied and he is of the truthful."

Yūsuf (12:26–27)

The shirt, torn from behind — the fleeing man's shirt, grabbed by a pursuer. The evidence is examined; the husband sees it; the household knows. Notice the pattern the sūrah is building: a shirt with false blood convicted the brothers' story in Yaʿqūb's eyes; a shirt torn from behind acquits Yūsuf here; and a shirt will one day travel from Egypt to heal an old man's sight. Three times in this story, the truth rides on a garment — and twice it speaks for someone whose own voice was not believed.

Cleared, then, by the evidence? Yes — and imprisoned anyway. The talk of the women spreads; containment matters more to the household than justice; it appeared to them, after they had seen the signs, that they should imprison him for a time (12:35). Sit with that verse. The signs were seen. He went to prison after being proven innocent — because the innocent man was less trouble locked up. If injustice has ever closed a door on you while everyone involved knew better, this verse was written about the machinery you met.

Scene two: the dungeon, and the tag

Two young men enter the prison with him, each carrying a dream. But before they tell him the dreams, listen to how they address a fellow prisoner — a foreign slave, convicted of nothing, with no rank left to lose:

وَدَخَلَ مَعَهُ ٱلسِّجْنَ فَتَيَانِ ۖ قَالَ أَحَدُهُمَآ إِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰنِىٓ أَعْصِرُ خَمْرًا ۖ وَقَالَ ٱلْـَٔاخَرُ إِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰنِىٓ أَحْمِلُ فَوْقَ رَأْسِى خُبْزًا تَأْكُلُ ٱلطَّيْرُ مِنْهُ ۖ نَبِّئْنَا بِتَأْوِيلِهِۦٓ ۖ إِنَّا نَرَىٰكَ مِنَ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ

"And there entered the prison with him two young men. One of them said: Indeed, I have seen myself pressing wine. The other said: Indeed, I have seen myself carrying upon my head bread, from which the birds were eating. Inform us of its interpretation; indeed, we see you as one of the doers of good."

Yūsuf (12:36)

Innā narāka mina l-muḥsinīn — "we see you as one of the muḥsinīn," the people of iḥsān, of excellence. Strangers, in a dungeon, with nothing to gain from flattery, look at this man stripped of everything and name what is left. Keep this sentence. It is the spine of the whole course, and this is the first time a human being says it out loud — it will be said again, word for word, by people who have no idea they are repeating anyone.

What he does with their question

قَالَ لَا يَأْتِيكُمَا طَعَامٌ تُرْزَقَانِهِۦٓ إِلَّا نَبَّأْتُكُمَا بِتَأْوِيلِهِۦ قَبْلَ أَن يَأْتِيَكُمَا ۚ ذَٰلِكُمَا مِمَّا عَلَّمَنِى رَبِّىٓ ۚ إِنِّى تَرَكْتُ مِلَّةَ قَوْمٍ لَّا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱللَّهِ وَهُم بِٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ هُمْ كَـٰفِرُونَ

"He said: There will not come to you food you are provided with, but I will inform you of its interpretation before it comes to you. That is from what my Lord has taught me. Indeed, I have left the creed of a people who do not believe in God, and they, concerning the Hereafter, are disbelievers."

Yūsuf (12:37)

Watch the structure. They asked for a dream reading. He promises the answer — establishes he can do it, and says where the knowledge is from — and then, before delivering it, he gives them what they had not asked for: who his Lord is, why one God rather than scattered lords, what he inherited from Ibrāhīm and Isḥāq and Yaʿqūb. Three full verses of invitation. Then, and only then, the dreams.

He treated a dungeon as a post. He had been thrown away by his family, sold by his finders, framed by his household, forgotten by the state — and his working assumption, in the dark, was still: I am here; these two are in front of me; that means something is assigned. That assumption — not the palace, not the throne — is what the strangers had already smelled on him when they called him a muḥsin.

The forgetting, and the counted-out years

وَقَالَ لِلَّذِى ظَنَّ أَنَّهُۥ نَاجٍ مِّنْهُمَا ٱذْكُرْنِى عِندَ رَبِّكَ فَأَنسَىٰهُ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنُ ذِكْرَ رَبِّهِۦ فَلَبِثَ فِى ٱلسِّجْنِ بِضْعَ سِنِينَ

"And he said to the one whom he knew would be saved: Mention me before your master. But Satan made him forget the mention of his master, so he remained in prison several years."

Yūsuf (12:42)

He asks the survivor to mention him to the king — and the verse says Satan caused a forgetting, and the prison years followed. Who forgot is a real disagreement, and both sides deserve their names. Ibn ʿAbbās — and, al-Baghawī says, the majority with him, al-Ṭabarī among them — read it as Yūsuf momentarily forgetting his Lord: a passing lapse, seeking his rescue through a creature instead of the One who owns the doors, and al-Ṭabarī says it in his own voice: the lapse lengthened his stay — with reports beneath the point at varying strength. Ibn Taymiyya and Abū Ḥayyān rule the other way: the forgetter is the cupbearer — the pronoun points to him, Yūsuf was never neglectful of his Lord, and three verses later the Quran says of the cupbearer, he remembered after a period — you only remember what you forgot. We leave it where the scholars left it. What no reading changes: the door that should have opened stayed shut, and the sentence lands like a cell door — several years.

The Quran does not count them. A phrase — biḍʿ sinīn, "some years"; the commentators' counts range from three up to nine or ten, and al-Ṭabarī rules only that the word stays under ten — and silence. Years of a blameless man's one life, uncounted, in the dark, on a clerical error. If the story's machinery is real, these are the years it has to answer for. The next module is the answer.
