Module 2 — The Locked Room and the Dungeon

This module looks closely at two scenes. In the first, Yūsuf loses his freedom because he refuses to do wrong. In the second, he shows what a person who trusts God does when he is thrown into a dungeon. Together they answer a question that Module 1 left open. The Quran keeps telling us that God is arranging events. So how does that look from the inside, on the days when everything looks like ruin?

Scene one: the locked room

وَرَٰوَدَتْهُ ٱلَّتِى هُوَ فِى بَيْتِهَا عَن نَّفْسِهِۦ وَغَلَّقَتِ ٱلْأَبْوَٰبَ وَقَالَتْ هَيْتَ لَكَ ۚ قَالَ مَعَاذَ ٱللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ رَبِّىٓ أَحْسَنَ مَثْوَاىَ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ لَا يُفْلِحُ ٱلظَّـٰلِمُونَ

"And she, in whose house he was, sought to seduce him. She closed the doors and said: Come, you. He said: I seek the refuge of God. Indeed, he is my master, who has made good my residence. Indeed, wrongdoers will not succeed."

Yūsuf (12:23)

Think about his situation for a moment. He is young and a slave, far from anyone who knows him. She holds all the power over his life. The verb used for locking the doors is an intensive form, and it suggests she locked one door after another. Nobody is watching and no obvious punishment waits for him. Everything this world offers is pushing him in one direction.

His answer has three parts, and each part does its own job. First he says maʿādha llāh, "I seek refuge with God." Before he gives any reason at all, he turns to the one door that is never locked. Then he gives the reason of loyalty: he is my master who honoured my residence. He will not repay the man who sheltered him by betraying him. Finally he states the principle behind both: wrongdoers do not succeed. So he turns to God first, then to gratitude and then to principle. He does not bargain with her, and he does not stay in the room to see how strong he is. In the very next verse he is racing her to the door.

The shirt speaks again

At the door they run into her husband, and she speaks first to accuse Yūsuf. She asks for prison or a painful punishment for the man who wanted to harm his family. Yūsuf tells the truth in four words: hiya rāwadatnī ʿan nafsī, "it was she who sought me." Then an unexpected witness speaks up:

قَالَ هِىَ رَٰوَدَتْنِى عَن نَّفْسِى ۚ وَشَهِدَ شَاهِدٌ مِّنْ أَهْلِهَآ إِن كَانَ قَمِيصُهُۥ قُدَّ مِن قُبُلٍ فَصَدَقَتْ وَهُوَ مِنَ ٱلْكَـٰذِبِينَ وَإِن كَانَ قَمِيصُهُۥ قُدَّ مِن دُبُرٍ فَكَذَبَتْ وَهُوَ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰدِقِينَ

"He said: It was she who sought to seduce me. And a witness from her family testified: If his shirt is torn from the front, then she has told the truth and he is of the liars. But if his shirt is torn from the back, then she has lied and he is of the truthful."

Yūsuf (12:26–27)

The shirt turns out to be torn from behind. That is what happens when a man is running away and someone chasing him grabs his shirt. The husband looks at the evidence, and the whole household knows the truth. Notice how often a shirt matters in this sūrah. A shirt stained with false blood showed Yaʿqūb that the brothers were lying. This torn shirt clears Yūsuf, and one day a shirt will be carried from Egypt to heal an old man's eyes. Three times in this story the truth depends on a piece of clothing. Twice it speaks up for someone whose own words were not believed.

The evidence clears him, yet he is sent to prison anyway. The women's gossip is spreading, and the household cares more about keeping things quiet than about justice. It appeared to them, after they had seen the signs, that they should imprison him for a time (12:35). They had seen the signs. So he went to prison after he had been proven innocent, simply because an innocent man was less trouble behind bars. If you have ever had a door shut on you unfairly while everyone involved knew better, this verse describes exactly what happened to you.

Scene two: the dungeon

Two young men are sent to prison with him, and each of them has had a dream. Before they tell him their dreams, they say something about him. Remember that he is a foreign slave with no status left to lose, and he has been convicted of nothing. Yet this is how they speak to him:

وَدَخَلَ مَعَهُ ٱلسِّجْنَ فَتَيَانِ ۖ قَالَ أَحَدُهُمَآ إِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰنِىٓ أَعْصِرُ خَمْرًا ۖ وَقَالَ ٱلْـَٔاخَرُ إِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰنِىٓ أَحْمِلُ فَوْقَ رَأْسِى خُبْزًا تَأْكُلُ ٱلطَّيْرُ مِنْهُ ۖ نَبِّئْنَا بِتَأْوِيلِهِۦٓ ۖ إِنَّا نَرَىٰكَ مِنَ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ

"And there entered the prison with him two young men. One of them said: Indeed, I have seen myself pressing wine. The other said: Indeed, I have seen myself carrying upon my head bread, from which the birds were eating. Inform us of its interpretation; indeed, we see you as one of the doers of good."

Yūsuf (12:36)

Innā narāka mina l-muḥsinīn means "we see you as one of the muḥsinīn." These are the people of iḥsān, which means excellence. These two strangers in a dungeon have nothing to gain by flattering him. Yet they look at a man who has lost everything and name the one thing he still has. This description of Yūsuf runs through the whole course, and this is the first time a human being says it out loud. Later in the story other people will say it again word for word, with no idea that they are repeating someone.

What he does with their question

قَالَ لَا يَأْتِيكُمَا طَعَامٌ تُرْزَقَانِهِۦٓ إِلَّا نَبَّأْتُكُمَا بِتَأْوِيلِهِۦ قَبْلَ أَن يَأْتِيَكُمَا ۚ ذَٰلِكُمَا مِمَّا عَلَّمَنِى رَبِّىٓ ۚ إِنِّى تَرَكْتُ مِلَّةَ قَوْمٍ لَّا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱللَّهِ وَهُم بِٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ هُمْ كَـٰفِرُونَ

"He said: There will not come to you food you are provided with, but I will inform you of its interpretation before it comes to you. That is from what my Lord has taught me. Indeed, I have left the creed of a people who do not believe in God, and they, concerning the Hereafter, are disbelievers."

Yūsuf (12:37)

Look at the order of his reply. They only asked him what their dreams meant. He promises to tell them, which shows he is able to. He also tells them where that knowledge comes from. But before he gives the answer, he gives them something they did not ask for. He tells them who his Lord is and why one God is better than many scattered lords. He tells them the faith he inherited from Ibrāhīm, Isḥāq and Yaʿqūb. He spends three whole verses inviting them to God. Only after all of that does he explain their dreams.

He treated the dungeon as a place he had been posted to. His family had thrown him away and the people who found him had sold him. The household he served had framed him, and the government had forgotten him. Even so, he sat in the dark and still assumed he must have been given something to do. He was there, and these two men were in front of him. When the two strangers called him a muḥsin, this attitude is what they had already seen in him. They were not looking at any palace or throne.

The forgetting, and the counted-out years

وَقَالَ لِلَّذِى ظَنَّ أَنَّهُۥ نَاجٍ مِّنْهُمَا ٱذْكُرْنِى عِندَ رَبِّكَ فَأَنسَىٰهُ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنُ ذِكْرَ رَبِّهِۦ فَلَبِثَ فِى ٱلسِّجْنِ بِضْعَ سِنِينَ

"And he said to the one whom he knew would be saved: Mention me before your master. But Satan made him forget the mention of his master, so he remained in prison several years."

Yūsuf (12:42)

Yūsuf asks the man who will be freed to mention him to the king. The verse says that Satan made someone forget, and the years in prison followed. The scholars genuinely disagree about who forgot, and both sides should be named. Ibn ʿAbbās understood it to mean that Yūsuf forgot his Lord for a moment. Al-Baghawī says most scholars agreed with him, including al-Ṭabarī. On that reading it was a brief lapse. Yūsuf looked for rescue from a human being instead of from the One who controls every door. Al-Ṭabarī says in his own words that this lapse made his time in prison longer, though the reports on this point vary in strength. Ibn Taymiyya and Abū Ḥayyān ruled the other way. They said the one who forgot was the cupbearer. They point out that the pronoun refers to him, and that Yūsuf was never careless about his Lord. They also note that three verses later the Quran says of the cupbearer he remembered after a period. A person can only remember what he had forgotten. We will leave the question where the scholars left it, because neither reading changes what happened next. The door that should have opened stayed shut, and the verse ends with the words several years.

The Quran does not say how many years. It only uses the phrase biḍʿ sinīn, "some years," and says nothing more. The commentators' estimates range from three years to nine or ten. Al-Ṭabarī rules only that the word means fewer than ten. So these were years out of an innocent man's one life. They were spent in the dark and never counted, all because someone forgot to pass on a message. If God really is arranging everything in this story, then the story has to explain these lost years. The next module gives that explanation.
