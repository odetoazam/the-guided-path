Module 2 — The Locked Room and the Dungeon

Two scenes, up close. In the first, Yūsuf loses his freedom for refusing to do wrong. In the second, he shows you what a person who trusts the Weaver does with a dungeon. Together they answer a question Module 1 left open. The narrator keeps saying that God is arranging things, so what does that arrangement look like from inside, on the days when it looks like ruin?

Scene one: the locked room

وَرَٰوَدَتْهُ ٱلَّتِى هُوَ فِى بَيْتِهَا عَن نَّفْسِهِۦ وَغَلَّقَتِ ٱلْأَبْوَٰبَ وَقَالَتْ هَيْتَ لَكَ ۚ قَالَ مَعَاذَ ٱللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ رَبِّىٓ أَحْسَنَ مَثْوَاىَ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ لَا يُفْلِحُ ٱلظَّـٰلِمُونَ

"And she, in whose house he was, sought to seduce him. She closed the doors and said: Come, you. He said: I seek the refuge of God. Indeed, he is my master, who has made good my residence. Indeed, wrongdoers will not succeed."

Yūsuf (12:23)

Weigh the scene as it stands. He is young, enslaved, and far from anyone who knows him. She holds all the power in his life. The verb used for the locking of the doors is an intensive one, which means door after door. There is no witness, no visible consequence, and every earthly incentive points one way.

His answer is three sentences, and each one does its own work. Maʿādha llāh means refuge with God, the one door that is never locked, and he names it first, before any reasoning. Then comes the reasoning of loyalty: he is my master who honoured my residence, so he will not repay shelter with betrayal. Then he states the principle that covers both: wrongdoers do not succeed. Refuge, gratitude, principle, in that order. He does not negotiate, and he does not stay to test his own strength. In the next verse he is racing her to the door.

The shirt speaks again

At the door they meet her husband, and she accuses first. She asks for prison or painful punishment for the man who meant evil against his family. Yūsuf states the truth in four words, hiya rāwadatnī ʿan nafsī, it was she who sought me. Then the story produces an unusual witness:

قَالَ هِىَ رَٰوَدَتْنِى عَن نَّفْسِى ۚ وَشَهِدَ شَاهِدٌ مِّنْ أَهْلِهَآ إِن كَانَ قَمِيصُهُۥ قُدَّ مِن قُبُلٍ فَصَدَقَتْ وَهُوَ مِنَ ٱلْكَـٰذِبِينَ وَإِن كَانَ قَمِيصُهُۥ قُدَّ مِن دُبُرٍ فَكَذَبَتْ وَهُوَ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰدِقِينَ

"He said: It was she who sought to seduce me. And a witness from her family testified: If his shirt is torn from the front, then she has told the truth and he is of the liars. But if his shirt is torn from the back, then she has lied and he is of the truthful."

Yūsuf (12:26–27)

The shirt is torn from behind, which is the shirt of a man running away, grabbed by someone chasing him. The evidence is examined, the husband sees it, and the household knows. The sūrah is building a pattern here. A shirt with false blood convinced Yaʿqūb of the brothers' story. A shirt torn from behind clears Yūsuf here. A shirt will one day travel from Egypt to heal an old man's sight. Three times in this story the truth rides on a garment, and twice it speaks for someone whose own voice was not believed.

So the evidence clears him, and he is imprisoned anyway. The talk of the women spreads, and containment matters more to the household than justice. It appeared to them, after they had seen the signs, that they should imprison him for a time (12:35). The signs had been seen. He went to prison after he was proven innocent, because the innocent man was less trouble locked up. If injustice has ever closed a door on you while everyone involved knew better, this verse describes the machinery you met.

Scene two: the dungeon, and the tag

Two young men enter the prison with him, each carrying a dream. Before they tell him the dreams, they say something about him. He is a foreign slave, convicted of nothing, with no rank left to lose, and this is how they address him:

وَدَخَلَ مَعَهُ ٱلسِّجْنَ فَتَيَانِ ۖ قَالَ أَحَدُهُمَآ إِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰنِىٓ أَعْصِرُ خَمْرًا ۖ وَقَالَ ٱلْـَٔاخَرُ إِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰنِىٓ أَحْمِلُ فَوْقَ رَأْسِى خُبْزًا تَأْكُلُ ٱلطَّيْرُ مِنْهُ ۖ نَبِّئْنَا بِتَأْوِيلِهِۦٓ ۖ إِنَّا نَرَىٰكَ مِنَ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ

"And there entered the prison with him two young men. One of them said: Indeed, I have seen myself pressing wine. The other said: Indeed, I have seen myself carrying upon my head bread, from which the birds were eating. Inform us of its interpretation; indeed, we see you as one of the doers of good."

Yūsuf (12:36)

Innā narāka mina l-muḥsinīn means "we see you as one of the muḥsinīn," the people of iḥsān, of excellence. Two strangers in a dungeon, with nothing to gain by flattering him, look at a man stripped of everything and name what is left. This sentence is the spine of the whole course, and this is the first time a human being says it out loud. It is said again later, word for word, by people who have no idea they are repeating anyone.

What he does with their question

قَالَ لَا يَأْتِيكُمَا طَعَامٌ تُرْزَقَانِهِۦٓ إِلَّا نَبَّأْتُكُمَا بِتَأْوِيلِهِۦ قَبْلَ أَن يَأْتِيَكُمَا ۚ ذَٰلِكُمَا مِمَّا عَلَّمَنِى رَبِّىٓ ۚ إِنِّى تَرَكْتُ مِلَّةَ قَوْمٍ لَّا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱللَّهِ وَهُم بِٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ هُمْ كَـٰفِرُونَ

"He said: There will not come to you food you are provided with, but I will inform you of its interpretation before it comes to you. That is from what my Lord has taught me. Indeed, I have left the creed of a people who do not believe in God, and they, concerning the Hereafter, are disbelievers."

Yūsuf (12:37)

The structure of his reply matters. They asked for a dream reading. He promises them the answer, which establishes that he can do it, and he says where the knowledge comes from. Then, before he delivers it, he gives them what they had not asked for: who his Lord is, why one God rather than scattered lords, and what he inherited from Ibrāhīm and Isḥāq and Yaʿqūb. That is three full verses of invitation. Only after all of it does he read the dreams.

He treated a dungeon as a post. His family had thrown him away, his finders had sold him, his household had framed him, and the state had forgotten him. In the dark, his working assumption was still that he was there, these two men were in front of him, and so something had been assigned to him. That assumption, rather than the palace or the throne, is what the two strangers had already seen in him when they called him a muḥsin.

The forgetting, and the counted-out years

وَقَالَ لِلَّذِى ظَنَّ أَنَّهُۥ نَاجٍ مِّنْهُمَا ٱذْكُرْنِى عِندَ رَبِّكَ فَأَنسَىٰهُ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنُ ذِكْرَ رَبِّهِۦ فَلَبِثَ فِى ٱلسِّجْنِ بِضْعَ سِنِينَ

"And he said to the one whom he knew would be saved: Mention me before your master. But Satan made him forget the mention of his master, so he remained in prison several years."

Yūsuf (12:42)

He asks the survivor to mention him to the king. The verse says that Satan caused a forgetting, and that the prison years followed. Who did the forgetting is a real disagreement, and both sides deserve their names. Ibn ʿAbbās read it as Yūsuf forgetting his Lord for a moment, and al-Baghawī says the majority held that with him, al-Ṭabarī among them. On that reading it was a passing lapse, seeking rescue through a creature instead of the One who owns the doors. Al-Ṭabarī says in his own voice that the lapse lengthened his stay, and the reports under the point vary in strength. Ibn Taymiyya and Abū Ḥayyān rule the other way, that the one who forgot was the cupbearer. The pronoun points to him, Yūsuf was never neglectful of his Lord, and three verses later the Quran says of the cupbearer, he remembered after a period, and you only remember what you forgot. We leave it where the scholars left it. No reading changes the outcome. The door that should have opened stayed shut, and the sentence lands like a cell door: several years.

The Quran does not count them. It gives a phrase, biḍʿ sinīn, "some years", and then says nothing more. The commentators' counts range from three up to nine or ten, and al-Ṭabarī rules only that the word stays under ten. So these are years of a blameless man's one life, uncounted, in the dark, lost to a clerical error. If the story's machinery is real, these are the years it has to answer for.
