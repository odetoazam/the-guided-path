Module 3 — Nothing Was Wasted

This is the module the course is named after. It follows one word through the whole story, and it reads one verse that changes how you see everything God was doing in it. It ends with the name Yūsuf gives his Lord when he finally sees the shape of his own life. If you only keep one module of this course with you, keep this one.

The word that follows him

The word is muḥsin, a person of iḥsān, someone who does things with excellence. It describes someone whose goodness holds no matter what is happening to them. In this sūrah it appears exactly five times, and the places where it appears teach the lesson.

12:22 — God's narration, as Yūsuf comes of age in slavery: and when he reached maturity, We gave him judgment and knowledge — and thus do We reward the muḥsinīn. So the first person to call him a muḥsin is God Himself, and He says it about a trafficked young man living in a stranger's house.

12:36 — the prisoners: innā narāka mina l-muḥsinīn — we see you as one of the muḥsinīn. These are two strangers in a dungeon, and they say it before they even ask him for help.

12:56 — God's narration again, as Yūsuf takes charge of Egypt:

وَكَذَٰلِكَ مَكَّنَّا لِيُوسُفَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ يَتَبَوَّأُ مِنْهَا حَيْثُ يَشَآءُ ۚ نُصِيبُ بِرَحْمَتِنَا مَن نَّشَآءُ ۖ وَلَا نُضِيعُ أَجْرَ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ

"And thus We established Yūsuf in the land, to settle therein wherever he willed. We touch with Our mercy whom We will, and We do not waste the reward of the doers of good."

Yūsuf (12:56)

12:78 — the brothers, pleading before the ruler of Egypt they do not recognise: innā narāka mina l-muḥsinīn. This is the prisoners' sentence from 12:36, the same words in the same order, from different people in a different decade and a different building. The prisoners said it to a slave in a dungeon, and now the brothers say it to a powerful minister sitting on a raised seat, and neither group knows the other exists. So the same description has followed Yūsuf from the bottom of society to the top, spoken by strangers who each thought they had just noticed something new about him.

12:90 — Yūsuf himself, unveiled to his brothers:

قَالُوٓا۟ أَءِنَّكَ لَأَنتَ يُوسُفُ ۖ قَالَ أَنَا۠ يُوسُفُ وَهَـٰذَآ أَخِى ۖ قَدْ مَنَّ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْنَآ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ مَن يَتَّقِ وَيَصْبِرْ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يُضِيعُ أَجْرَ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ

"They said: Are you indeed Yūsuf? He said: I am Yūsuf, and this is my brother. God has certainly favoured us. Indeed, whoever fears God and is patient — then indeed, God does not waste the reward of the doers of good."

Yūsuf (12:90)

Now you can see how the five fit together. The first and third times, God is the one speaking, and the third time He adds a promise: We do not waste the reward of the muḥsinīn. The fifth time, Yūsuf says it himself, and he repeats that same promise back: God does not waste the reward of the muḥsinīn. When Yūsuf says it, it is no longer a teaching he has heard but the story of his own life, and he names the two things it took: whoever has taqwā and holds patient. The root for "waste" occurs exactly twice in this sūrah, once when God says it as Yūsuf rises to power and once when Yūsuf says it over a life that has finally come back together. So God makes the promise, and the man who lived through it confirms that it came true.

The verse where God plans for Yūsuf

Everyone in this story plots, and the Arabic word for plotting is kayd. In the very first scene Yaʿqūb warned about the brothers' kayd. The master of the house tells the women your kayd is great, and Yūsuf speaks of their kayd twice, once in his prayer and once in his message to the king. Then comes the cleverest moment in the story, when Yūsuf arranges for his cup to be found in his brother's bag so that he can keep his family within reach, and here the Quran uses the same word about God:

فَبَدَأَ بِأَوْعِيَتِهِمْ قَبْلَ وِعَآءِ أَخِيهِ ثُمَّ ٱسْتَخْرَجَهَا مِن وِعَآءِ أَخِيهِ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ كِدْنَا لِيُوسُفَ ۖ مَا كَانَ لِيَأْخُذَ أَخَاهُ فِى دِينِ ٱلْمَلِكِ إِلَّآ أَن يَشَآءَ ٱللَّهُ ۚ نَرْفَعُ دَرَجَـٰتٍ مَّن نَّشَآءُ ۗ وَفَوْقَ كُلِّ ذِى عِلْمٍ عَلِيمٌ

"So he began with their bags before the bag of his brother; then he extracted it from the bag of his brother. Thus did We plan for Yūsuf. He could not have taken his brother within the religion of the king except that God willed. We raise in degrees whom We will, and above every possessor of knowledge is one more knowing."

Yūsuf (12:76)

Kadhālika kidnā li-Yūsuf means "thus did We plan for Yūsuf." The verb is kayd, the same word used for all the plotters, and God uses it for Himself, with one small preposition making all the difference: every other plot in the story was aimed against someone, but God planned for Yūsuf. That means every scheme in the sūrah was happening inside a bigger plan that the schemers could not see. The brothers' plot brought Yūsuf to Egypt, and the women's plot put him in the prison that led to the throne. Now God's own plan brings his brother into his care. The plotters made things move, but they never got to decide where things would end up.

The chain, laid out flat

Try walking the story backwards from the throne. He fed whole nations during the famine because he was in charge of Egypt's storehouses. He was put in charge of the storehouses because the king had a dream that nobody else could explain. The king heard about him because a freed prisoner finally remembered the man who explained dreams in prison. He was in that prison because he had been jailed unjustly, and he was jailed because of the scandal in his master's house. He was in that house because he was a slave there, and he was a slave because his brothers sold him cheaply to a caravan heading for Egypt. And the caravan only found him because the brothers had thrown him into a well on the caravan's route.

Every step that led to the throne of Egypt was a disaster, and if you took any one away, or even made one a little easier, the whole chain would break. Without the well, he never reaches Egypt, and without slavery, he never reaches the palace. Without the false accusation there is no prison, and without the prison he never meets the king. So the worst days of his life turn out to be the very things that carried him forward. They were the road itself rather than obstacles on the road.

The Quran said this at the time, using the small word thus that we noticed in Module 1. It says "thus We established him" over the sale (12:21), and "thus We established him" again over his rise to power (12:56). It uses the same verb and the same thus both times, once over what looked like burial and once over what looked like harvest. The Quran can use the same words for both because, from God's point of view, they were one and the same work.

The name he gives it

Then the day comes. His parents sit on the throne, the sun and moon of his boyhood dream, and his eleven brothers bow down like the stars, so the dream comes true and the story comes full circle. Every part of what Yūsuf says at that moment about his own life matters:

وَرَفَعَ أَبَوَيْهِ عَلَى ٱلْعَرْشِ وَخَرُّوا۟ لَهُۥ سُجَّدًا ۖ وَقَالَ يَـٰٓأَبَتِ هَـٰذَا تَأْوِيلُ رُءْيَـٰىَ مِن قَبْلُ قَدْ جَعَلَهَا رَبِّى حَقًّا ۖ وَقَدْ أَحْسَنَ بِىٓ إِذْ أَخْرَجَنِى مِنَ ٱلسِّجْنِ وَجَآءَ بِكُم مِّنَ ٱلْبَدْوِ مِنۢ بَعْدِ أَن نَّزَغَ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنُ بَيْنِى وَبَيْنَ إِخْوَتِىٓ ۚ إِنَّ رَبِّى لَطِيفٌ لِّمَا يَشَآءُ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلْعَلِيمُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

"And he raised his parents upon the throne, and they fell down before him in prostration. And he said: O my father, this is the interpretation of my vision of before. My Lord has made it reality. And He was certainly good to me when He took me out of prison and brought you from the desert life, after Satan had induced discord between me and my brothers. Indeed, my Lord is subtle in what He wills. Indeed, it is He who is the Knowing, the Wise."

Yūsuf (12:100)

Three things in this speech tie up threads this course has been following.

The first is qad aḥsana bī, "He has been good to me," and the verb here comes from the same root as iḥsān. So the word that followed Yūsuf through five scenes comes back one last time. All along, while strangers kept calling him a muḥsin, God had been showing iḥsān to him. The goodness people saw in Yūsuf was a sign of God's goodness working on him from above.

The second is after Satan induced discord between me and my brothers. This is where you would expect him to list what his brothers did to him, but instead he puts the whole thing down to Satan stirring up trouble between them. He mentions the prison but never mentions the well. Standing in front of the brothers who threw him into it, at a moment when he has the power and the audience to say it, he leaves their crime unnamed. In the very first scene his father had taught him where hatred between brothers really comes from, and now, at the height of his success, Yūsuf places the blame exactly there.

The third is the sentence this whole course is here to give you: inna rabbī laṭīfun li-mā yashāʾ, "my Lord is laṭīf in what He wills." The word means subtle and gentle, someone who works finely and out of sight, with a kindness that reaches into the smallest gaps. The Quran calls God al-Laṭīf seven times. Six of those teach it as a belief, including the one other time a human being says it, when Luqmān uses it to teach his son. This verse is the seventh, and it is the only place in the whole Quran where someone says it while looking back at the evidence of his own life. Yūsuf looks back over the well, the chains and the prison cells, sees that they were what carried him here, and gives what he sees its name.

This answers the question in the course's title. The years that seemed wasted did not disappear, because they were never wasted in the first place. They were carrying something, and what they carried was you, being moved to where you needed to be. Two modules are left. One looks at what the Quran deliberately leaves out of this story, and the other looks at the years of your own life that still seem thrown away.
