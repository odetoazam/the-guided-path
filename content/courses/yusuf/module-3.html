Module 3 — Nothing Was Wasted

This is the module the course is named for. It follows one word through the story and reads one verse that changes how the whole machinery looks. It ends on the name Yūsuf gives his Lord when he finally sees the shape of his own life. If you carry one module of this course with you, carry this one.

The word that follows him

The word is muḥsin, a person of iḥsān, of doing things excellently. It is the Quran's word for someone whose goodness stays good whatever the conditions. In this sūrah it appears exactly five times, and where those five sit is the lesson.

12:22 — God's narration, as Yūsuf comes of age in slavery: and when he reached maturity, We gave him judgment and knowledge — and thus do We reward the muḥsinīn. The first tag is the narrator's, and it is stamped on a trafficked young man in a stranger's house.

12:36 — the prisoners: innā narāka mina l-muḥsinīn — we see you as one of the muḥsinīn. Strangers in a dungeon, speaking before they ask their favour.

12:56 — God's narration again, as Yūsuf takes charge of Egypt:

وَكَذَٰلِكَ مَكَّنَّا لِيُوسُفَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ يَتَبَوَّأُ مِنْهَا حَيْثُ يَشَآءُ ۚ نُصِيبُ بِرَحْمَتِنَا مَن نَّشَآءُ ۖ وَلَا نُضِيعُ أَجْرَ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ

"And thus We established Yūsuf in the land, to settle therein wherever he willed. We touch with Our mercy whom We will, and We do not waste the reward of the doers of good."

Yūsuf (12:56)

12:78 — the brothers, pleading before the ruler of Egypt they do not recognise: innā narāka mina l-muḥsinīn. That is the sentence from 12:36 again. Different people, a different decade, a different building, and the same words in the same order. The prisoners said it to a slave in a dungeon. The brothers say it to a vizier on a dais. Neither group knows the other exists. The tag has followed him from the bottom of society to the top, in the mouths of strangers who each thought they were describing something they had just noticed.

12:90 — Yūsuf himself, unveiled to his brothers:

قَالُوٓا۟ أَءِنَّكَ لَأَنتَ يُوسُفُ ۖ قَالَ أَنَا۠ يُوسُفُ وَهَـٰذَآ أَخِى ۖ قَدْ مَنَّ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْنَآ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ مَن يَتَّقِ وَيَصْبِرْ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يُضِيعُ أَجْرَ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ

"They said: Are you indeed Yūsuf? He said: I am Yūsuf, and this is my brother. God has certainly favoured us. Indeed, whoever fears God and is patient — then indeed, God does not waste the reward of the doers of good."

Yūsuf (12:90)

Now the shape of the five is visible. The first and the third are God's narration, and the third carries a clause: We do not waste the reward of the muḥsinīn. The fifth comes from Yūsuf's own mouth, and he says that clause back: God does not waste the reward of the muḥsinīn. In his mouth it is autobiography rather than doctrine, and he names the two ingredients, whoever has taqwā and holds patient. The root for "waste" occurs exactly twice in this sūrah. Once when God says it over Yūsuf's rise, and once when Yūsuf says it over his own reassembled life. God states the policy, and the man who lived it countersigns it.

The verse that turns the machine inside out

Everyone in this story plots. The brothers plot, and Yaʿqūb warned of their kayd in the first scene. The women plot, and the master of the house tells them your kayd is great, while Yūsuf speaks of their kayd twice, once in his prayer and once in his message to the king. The word runs through the sūrah like a wire. Then, at the story's cleverest moment, when Yūsuf arranges for the cup to be found in his brother's bag and so brings his family within reach, the narrator takes hold of that wire:

فَبَدَأَ بِأَوْعِيَتِهِمْ قَبْلَ وِعَآءِ أَخِيهِ ثُمَّ ٱسْتَخْرَجَهَا مِن وِعَآءِ أَخِيهِ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ كِدْنَا لِيُوسُفَ ۖ مَا كَانَ لِيَأْخُذَ أَخَاهُ فِى دِينِ ٱلْمَلِكِ إِلَّآ أَن يَشَآءَ ٱللَّهُ ۚ نَرْفَعُ دَرَجَـٰتٍ مَّن نَّشَآءُ ۗ وَفَوْقَ كُلِّ ذِى عِلْمٍ عَلِيمٌ

"So he began with their bags before the bag of his brother; then he extracted it from the bag of his brother. Thus did We plan for Yūsuf. He could not have taken his brother within the religion of the king except that God willed. We raise in degrees whom We will, and above every possessor of knowledge is one more knowing."

Yūsuf (12:76)

Kadhālika kidnā li-Yūsuf. The verb here is the plotters' own verb, kayd, and God attaches it to Himself, with one preposition doing the work: for Yūsuf. Everyone else in the story plots against someone. Heaven plots for someone. So every scheme in the sūrah was running inside a larger scheme it could not see. The brothers' kayd delivered Yūsuf to Egypt. The women's kayd delivered him to the prison that led to the throne. Now a plan of God's own delivers his brother into his care. The plotters supplied the motion, and the destination was never theirs to set.

The chain, laid out flat

Walk the story backwards from the throne and see what each link is made of. He feeds nations in a famine because he ran Egypt's storehouses. He ran the storehouses because the king dreamed and nobody else could read the dream. The dream reached him because a freed prisoner finally remembered the man who read dreams in prison. He was in that prison because he was jailed unjustly. He was jailed because of the scandal in his master's house. He was in that house because he was a slave there. He was a slave there because his brothers sold him cheap to a caravan bound for Egypt. The caravan found him because they had thrown him into a well on the caravan road.

Every link in the chain that ends on the throne of Egypt is a disaster. Take any one of them away, or soften any one of them, and the chain breaks. No well, no Egypt. No slavery, no palace. No false accusation, no prison. No prison, no audience with the king. The worst days of his life turn out to be the transport, every one of them. They were the road rather than obstacles on the road.

The narrator said as much at the time, in the small word this course flagged in Module 1, which is thus. Thus We established him (12:21, over the sale), and thus We established him (12:56, over the rise). The same verb and the same thus, one spoken over what looked like burial and one over what looked like harvest. The Quran uses them interchangeably because, from where the Weaver sits, they were the same operation.

The name he gives it

Then the day comes. His parents sit on the throne he once dreamed of as sun and moon, eleven brothers bow like the stars, and the dream closes over the story. Every clause of what Yūsuf says over his own life does some work:

وَرَفَعَ أَبَوَيْهِ عَلَى ٱلْعَرْشِ وَخَرُّوا۟ لَهُۥ سُجَّدًا ۖ وَقَالَ يَـٰٓأَبَتِ هَـٰذَا تَأْوِيلُ رُءْيَـٰىَ مِن قَبْلُ قَدْ جَعَلَهَا رَبِّى حَقًّا ۖ وَقَدْ أَحْسَنَ بِىٓ إِذْ أَخْرَجَنِى مِنَ ٱلسِّجْنِ وَجَآءَ بِكُم مِّنَ ٱلْبَدْوِ مِنۢ بَعْدِ أَن نَّزَغَ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنُ بَيْنِى وَبَيْنَ إِخْوَتِىٓ ۚ إِنَّ رَبِّى لَطِيفٌ لِّمَا يَشَآءُ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلْعَلِيمُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

"And he raised his parents upon the throne, and they fell down before him in prostration. And he said: O my father, this is the interpretation of my vision of before. My Lord has made it reality. And He was certainly good to me when He took me out of prison and brought you from the desert life, after Satan had induced discord between me and my brothers. Indeed, my Lord is subtle in what He wills. Indeed, it is He who is the Knowing, the Wise."

Yūsuf (12:100)

There are three things in this speech, and each one closes a thread this course has been pulling.

Qad aḥsana bī means "He has been good to me," and the verb is the verb of iḥsān. So the word that followed Yūsuf through five stations resolves in the last scene. While strangers kept calling him the muḥsin, he was on the receiving end of an iḥsān that had been running downward the whole time. The tag on the man was a trace of the hand above him.

After Satan induced discord between me and my brothers. This is where you would expect an accounting of his brothers' crime, and instead he files the whole thing under the enemy's jab between them. He mentions the prison and never the well. The crime dug by his brothers' own hands goes unnamed in front of them, at the moment when he has both the power and the audience to name it. His father had taught him in the story's first scene where the enmity really comes from, and the son, at the summit, files it exactly there.

Then comes the sentence this course exists to hand you. Inna rabbī laṭīfun li-mā yashāʾ. "My Lord is laṭīf in what He wills." The word means subtle and gentle, working finely and unseen, the kindness that moves through the smallest gaps. The Quran calls God al-Laṭīf seven times. Six of those teach it as doctrine, and that includes the one other human voice among them, Luqmān, who says it to instruct his son. This is the seventh. It is the only place in the whole book where someone says it over the evidence of his own life. A man looks back down a chain of wells and chains and cells that turned out to be a supply line, and he gives what he sees its name.

That is the answer to the question on this course's front door. Where did the wasted years go? Nowhere. They were never waste. They were freight, and the freight was you, being moved. Two modules remain. One is for what the Quran deliberately leaves out of this story, and one is for the years of your own that still look thrown away.
