Start Here — The Story

Every course on this site opens by telling the story straight through — and this time the Quran has done the work for us. Yūsuf's story is the longest single narrative in the book, told once, in order, in one chapter, from his boyhood dream to the day it comes true. The Quran itself gives the story a title before it begins:

نَحْنُ نَقُصُّ عَلَيْكَ أَحْسَنَ ٱلْقَصَصِ بِمَآ أَوْحَيْنَآ إِلَيْكَ هَـٰذَا ٱلْقُرْءَانَ وَإِن كُنتَ مِن قَبْلِهِۦ لَمِنَ ٱلْغَـٰفِلِينَ

"We relate to you the best of stories in what We have revealed to you of this Quran, although you were, before it, among the unaware."

Yūsuf (12:3)

Aḥsan al-qaṣaṣ — the best of stories. Hold the claim; by the end of the course you can judge it.

A dream, and a warning

A boy tells his father a dream: eleven stars, and the sun and the moon — I saw them prostrating to me. His father Yaʿqūb — a prophet, son and grandson of prophets — understands instantly, and his first response is a warning: do not tell this to your brothers. He names the danger precisely: they would plot against you, for Satan is to man a clear enemy (12:5). The first scene of the best of stories is a father teaching a son that some gifts must be carried quietly.

The well

The brothers' envy does not need the dream — the love is enough. Yūsuf and his brother are dearer to our father than we are. They talk themselves from resentment to murder to a compromise: throw him into the bottom of a well; some caravan will pick him up. They manufacture trust — send him with us tomorrow, we will play and look after him — and they come home at nightfall weeping, with his shirt dipped in false blood. Their father is not deceived. He answers with a sentence he does not know he will need again:

وَجَآءُو عَلَىٰ قَمِيصِهِۦ بِدَمٍ كَذِبٍ ۚ قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا ۖ فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ ۖ وَٱللَّهُ ٱلْمُسْتَعَانُ عَلَىٰ مَا تَصِفُونَ

"And they brought upon his shirt false blood. He said: Rather, your souls have enticed you to something. So patience is most fitting. And God is the one sought for help against that which you describe."

Yūsuf (12:18)

Ṣabrun jamīl — a beautiful patience. Mark the phrase. It returns, word for word, decades later.

Sold, and bought

A caravan lowers its bucket and pulls up a boy. He is sold for a reduced price, a few coins — they valued him lightly (12:20); on al-Ṭabarī's ruling and the majority reading, the sellers who priced their own blood that cheaply were the brothers themselves. The caravan carries him to Egypt, where the man who buys him tells his wife: give him an honourable place; perhaps he will be useful to us. And here, over a trafficked child in a stranger's house, the Quran states its thesis for the first time: thus We established Yūsuf in the land... and God is predominant over His affair, but most people do not know (12:21).

The house, the prison

He grows into a man of striking beauty, and the wife of his master sets her will on him. She locks the doors. He refuses — I seek refuge in God; he is my master, who has made good my residence — and runs for the door; the shirt torn from behind becomes the evidence that clears him. The women of the city talk; she shows him off to them; they cut their hands in astonishment. And then, cleared by the evidence and desired by the powerful, he goes to prison anyway — because imprisoning him was easier than admitting the truth. His own prayer had asked for it: my Lord, prison is dearer to me than what they invite me to (12:33).

The dungeon years

In prison, two young men bring him their dreams — but first they tell him something about himself: we see you as one of the muḥsinīn, the people of excellence (12:36). Remember that sentence; you will hear it again from very different mouths. He answers their dreams — one will pour wine for his king, one will be crucified — but only after teaching them who he is and whose religion he carries. He asks the survivor to mention him to the king. The man forgets. So he remained in prison several years (12:42) — the Quran counts them no further than that.

The king's dream

Then the king of Egypt dreams: seven fat cows devoured by seven lean ones, seven green ears of grain and seven dry. His court calls it a jumble. The freed prisoner finally remembers. Yūsuf — still in the dungeon — interprets it without conditions: seven years of plenty, seven of famine, and a plan to survive them. The king summons him; Yūsuf refuses to come out until his name is cleared, and it is — the women of the city testify they know no evil of him, and the wife of al-ʿAzīz herself confesses: now the truth has become evident (12:51). The king makes him overseer of the storehouses of Egypt. The boy from the well now feeds the region through a famine.

The brothers return

The famine drives his brothers to Egypt for grain. He recognized them, and they did not recognize him (12:58). Across two journeys he steers them — the money returned in their saddlebags, the demand to bring the youngest brother, the cup found in the youngest brother's bag, the eldest refusing to leave without him — while their father, back home, loses his sight from grief and says his old sentence again, word for word: your souls have enticed you to something; so patience is most fitting (12:83). And when his sons scold him for still grieving, he tells them where his grief actually goes: I only complain of my suffering and my sorrow to God (12:86).

The unveiling

On the third audience, broken by need, the brothers beg. And Yūsuf asks one question — do you know what you did to Yūsuf and his brother, when you were ignorant? — and they know him. His answer to the men who threw him in a well is a single sentence: no blame upon you today; God will forgive you (12:92). He sends his shirt to heal his father's eyes, and the whole family comes to Egypt. His parents and his eleven brothers bow — and the boyhood dream clicks shut over the whole story. His speech at that moment is the summit of the course, and we will spend most of Module 3 inside it: my Lord is subtle in what He wills (12:100).

The course map

  • Module 0 — Start Here (you are here). The story, in the Quran's own order.
  • Module 1 — Loved, and Thrown Away. The descent, slowly: the dream held in trust, the envy, the well, the sale. Feel how much is taken before anything is given back.
  • Module 2 — The Locked Room and the Dungeon. Two scenes up close — the torn shirt, and what Yūsuf did with a prison.
  • Module 3 — Nothing Was Wasted. The flagship. One sentence follows Yūsuf through every station of his life, and one verse late in the story turns the whole machinery inside out.
  • Module 4 — What the Quran Won't Tell You. The names it withholds, the years it doesn't count — and the hardest verse in the story, faced with the scholars at the table.
  • Module 5 — Your Own Wasted Years. The course turns to face you, and the question in its title gets its answer.

One sentence to carry in: in this story, every single disaster is also a vehicle — and you are not told that at the end. You are told it in the middle, by a narrator who keeps saying, over the worst scenes: and thus We established him.
