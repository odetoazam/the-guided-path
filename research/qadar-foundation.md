## 0. The problem, stated honestly

The book's thesis is that guidance reshapes the determinate world — the inner filter
through which reality is perceived. That is a claim about a *process God runs on a person*.
The instant you say "God runs it," the reader asks: **then is the person free? If God
guides whom He wills and misguides whom He wills (a phrase the Quran uses repeatedly), and
if no one believes except by God's leave (10:100), what is left of human choice — and how
is anyone justly accountable?**

This is not a side-question the book can footnote. A book titled around "the Machine of
Hidāyah" *summons* exactly the reader who asks it first. The corpus census confirms the
scale: the root شيأ (to will) occurs **519 times / 449 verses**; **54 verses** yoke
guidance-or-misguidance directly to divine *mashīʾa*. Divine willing is not a marginal
theme the book can steer around. It is load-bearing structure, and the book must say where
it stands.

**The good news the census delivers: the Quran does not leave the tension unmanaged. It
holds it — repeatedly, deliberately, in its own syntax — and the classical tradition built
a precise vocabulary for that holding. The book does not need to invent a resolution or
pick a kalam school. It needs to (a) show the tension is the Quran's own, held on purpose,
(b) give the reader the classical tools that dissolve the *apparent* contradiction, and
(c) locate its own thesis at the exact level where all the Sunni schools agree.**

## 1. The two sides are both explicit — and often in adjacent verses

The census and tafsir confirm the Quran states *both* poles plainly, and the honest move
is to quote both at full strength before resolving anything.

### The human-choice pole (real, addressed, accountable)
- **18:29** — *wa-quli l-ḥaqqu min rabbikum **fa-man shāʾa fal-yuʾmin wa-man shāʾa
  fal-yakfur*** — "the truth is from your Lord; so whoever wills, let him believe, and
  whoever wills, let him disbelieve." The imperative is addressed to a *chooser*.
  (al-Muyassar: *fa-man arāda… fal-yafʿal… wa-mā ẓalama illā nafsah* — "whoever wills… let
  him do so… he wrongs only himself" — accountability located in the human.)
- **76:3** — *innā hadaynāhu l-sabīla **imma shākiran wa-imma kafūrā*** — "We guided him
  the way, whether grateful or ungrateful." al-Jalālayn: *bayyannā lahu ṭarīqa l-hudā
  bi-baʿthi l-rusul* — the guidance here is the *showing* of the way; the fork
  (grateful/ungrateful) is genuinely open. al-Muyassar: We acquainted him with the path
  of guidance *and* misguidance "so he may be either a grateful believer or an ungrateful
  denier."
- **81:28** — *li-man shāʾa minkum an yastaqīm* — "for whoever of you wills to go
  straight." al-Ṭabarī notes God made the Reminder a reminder *for the one who wills*, not
  imposed on all.
- **13:11** — *inna llāha lā yughayyiru mā bi-qawmin **ḥattā yughayyirū mā bi-anfusihim***
  — "God does not change a people's condition until they change what is in themselves."
  The human self-change is the *antecedent*; the divine response follows. (Tafsir caveat,
  §4: the mufassirūn read the primary sense in the *negative* direction — loss of blessing
  following self-corruption — mirrored at 8:53. The book may use both directions but must
  not present the popular self-improvement reading as the only classical one.)

### The divine-will pole (sovereign, enabling, decisive)
- **10:100** — *wa-mā kāna li-nafsin an tuʾmina **illā bi-idhni llāh*** — "no soul
  believes except by God's leave." al-Jalālayn: *bi-idhnihi = bi-irādatihi* (by His will).
  al-Ṭabarī: "the guidance of the soul is in the hand of its Creator." Belief itself
  requires divine *idhn*.
- **10:99** — *wa-law shāʾa rabbuka la-āmana man fī l-arḍi kulluhum jamīʿan* — "had your
  Lord willed, everyone on earth would have believed." God *could* compel universal
  belief; He does not. al-Muyassar: *lahu ḥikmatun fī dhālika* — He has wisdom in the
  restraint. **The non-compulsion is itself a divine choice** — which is the theodicy
  seed (see §3).
- **81:29** — *wa-mā tashāʾūna **illā an yashāʾa llāhu** rabbu l-ʿālamīn* — "you do not
  will except that God wills." al-Muyassar: *wa-mā tashāʾūna l-istiqāmata wa-lā taqdirūna
  ʿalā dhālika illā bi-mashīʾati llāh* — you neither will nor are able to, except by God's
  will.
- **28:56** — *innaka **lā tahdī man aḥbabta** wa-lākinna llāha yahdī man yashāʾ* — "you do
  not guide whom you love; God guides whom He wills." Revealed (al-Jalālayn) about the
  Prophet's ﷺ longing for Abū Ṭālib's faith.

**The decisive datum:** at **81:28–29** and again at **76:29–30** the Quran places the two
poles *in the same two-verse breath*, in an identical structure — clause 1 affirms the
human "whoever wills," clause 2 nests it "you do not will except that God wills." The
tension is not an artifact of comparing distant verses; **the Quran builds it into a single
sentence, twice, with the same syntax.** This is the book's method exactly (thesis carried
in the text's own structure), applied to qadar: the Quran's own syntax refuses both
autonomy and puppetry.

## 2. The resolution the book will use — NOT a kalam verdict, but two classical tools

The book does not need to declare for Ashʿarī, Māturīdī, or anyone. It needs the two
classical instruments that dissolve the *apparent* contradiction, both of which every
Sunni school accepts.

### Tool 1 — the hidāyah-taxonomy dissolves the "guidance contradiction" (the centerpiece)

The Quran predicates *hidāyah* of the Prophet ﷺ in one verse and **denies it of him in
another**:
- **42:52** — *wa-innaka **la-tahdī** ilā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm* — "you surely guide to a
  straight path."
- **28:56** — *innaka **lā tahdī** man aḥbabta* — "you do not guide whom you love."

One Prophet, one mission, guides and does-not-guide. The only resolution is that *hidāyah*
names **two different operations**, and the classical tradition names them:
- **hidāyat al-bayān / al-dalāla** — the showing of the way. Universal, offered to all,
  and the Prophet's actual work (42:52; 76:3 *hadaynāhu l-sabīl*, glossed *bayyannā… bi-
  baʿthi l-rusul*). This level leaves the fork open — *imma shākiran wa-imma kafūrā*.
- **hidāyat al-tawfīq** — the enabling, the light cast into the heart, the *sharḥ al-ṣadr*
  of 6:125. Particular, God's alone (28:56, glossed by al-Muyassar precisely: *lā tahdī
  **hidāyata tawfīqin** man aḥbabta*), and the referent of every "God guides whom He wills"
  verse.

**This is the book's single most important qadar move, and it is a gift, because it is
*also* the book's own thesis.** The determinate-world reshaping the whole book describes
*is hidāyat al-tawfīq* — the level-3 operation. The book is a phenomenology of tawfīq. So
the qadar resolution and the book's subject are the same thing seen from two angles:
"guidance reshapes the inner filter" (the book's language) = "tawfīq" (the tradition's).
When the reader asks "if God guides whom He wills, why strive?", the answer is: striving,
hearing, turning, remembering all live at the *bayān*-and-response level that is genuinely
yours (18:29, 76:3, 13:11, 29:69 *jāhadū fīnā la-nahdiyannahum*) — and tawfīq is God's gift
*in response*, never earned, never automatic, never withheld from the sincere.

### Tool 2 — the khalq/kasb two-level reading dissolves the "responsibility contradiction"

**4:78–79** is the pair that looks self-contradictory and is the key:
- 4:78 — *kullun min ʿindi llāh* — "all [good and evil] is from God."
- 4:79 — *mā aṣābaka min ḥasanatin **fa-mina llāh** wa-mā aṣābaka min sayyiʾatin **fa-min
  nafsik*** — "what reaches you of good is from God; what reaches you of evil is from
  yourself."

All four mufassirūn resolve it by *level*: 4:78's "all from God" is the level of **creation
and decree** (khalq, taqdīr — everything that occurs is by God's decree); 4:79's "evil from
yourself" is the level of **earning and cause** (al-Ṭabarī: *bi-dhanbin istawjabtahā,
ktasabathu nafsuka* — "by a sin you made deserved, which your self earned"; al-Jalālayn:
*ḥaythu rtakabta mā yastawjibuhā mina l-dhunūb*). **Creation is God's; moral authorship of
evil is the human's.** This is precisely the classical *kasb* doctrine in Quranic form, and
the census sharpens it: at **2:286** the Quran uses two different verb-forms — *lahā mā
**kasabat*** (good, simple Form I) and *ʿalayhā mā **ktasabat*** (evil, Form VIII,
reflexive/effortful). al-Jalālayn marks the split (good = *thawābuhu*, evil = *wizruhu*).
The more effortful verb for the acquisition of evil is a standard balāghah observation and
a genuine gift to the book: **evil takes more "earning" than good** — the determinate-world
thesis (the heart is built to receive light; darkness must be actively acquired) stated in
morphology. *(Label as census + balāghah, not as a kalam proof — Zaynab's standing rule.)*

The Ashʿarī term of art here is *kasb* ("acquisition"): God creates the act; the human
*acquires* it and is accountable for the acquisition. The book need not use the term or
defend the doctrine — but the 4:78–79 / 2:286 structure is the Quranic floor under it, and
the book can present *that* (the two levels) without entering the kalam.

## 3. The theodicy the book already owns — the whisper-verses and 10:99

The book's companion waswasa research already established (34:21, 22:52–54) that the
adversary's limited *sulṭān* and the divine *naskh* both exist *so that belief becomes
manifest* — a theodicy of the whisper. The qadar material extends it: **10:99** states God
*could* have compelled universal belief and chose not to (*wa-law shāʾa… la-āmana man fī
l-arḍi kulluhum*), because compelled belief is not the thing being sought. al-Muyassar:
*lahu ḥikmatun fī dhālika*. The whole apparatus of guidance-and-response, of the open fork
(76:3), of the un-compelled heart — exists *because* God wills a freely-turning creature,
not a coerced one. This is the deepest answer to "why the tension at all": the tension *is*
the dignity. A determinate world that could only ever resolve one way would not be a self
being guided; it would be a mechanism being driven. The book's counter-force chapter and
this page share one root: **no coercion** — not from the adversary (14:22, no *quwwa*), and
— astonishingly — not from God either, who withholds the compulsion He is capable of
(10:99).

## 4. Where the schools actually diverge (ikhtilāf honored — Hasan/Tariq)

The book presents the schools fairly and does not pretend the matter is settled. The corpus
even shows the fork *inside a single verse*:

- **37:96 — the pivot.** *wa-llāhu **khalaqakum wa-mā taʿmalūn*** — "God created you and
  what you do/make." al-Jalālayn flags the grammatical ambiguity explicitly: *mā* may be
  **maṣdariyya** ("and your doing" → God creates your *acts* — the Ashʿarī khalq-al-afʿāl
  proof) or **mawṣūla** ("and what you make" → the idols you carve — the Muʿtazilī reading,
  God created you and your raw material, not your acts). al-Muyassar leans maṣdariyya
  (*khalaqakum wa khalaqa ʿamalakum*). **The divergence is a real grammatical fork the
  tafsir itself marks** — the book should present it *as* a fork, not resolve it: here is
  the exact word (*mā*) where the schools split, and here is what each does with it.

The three positions, briefly and fairly:
- **Jabriyya (determinist, rejected by Sunni consensus)** — the human is compelled; acts
  are God's alone with no human role. Leans on the divine-will pole (81:29, 10:100) read
  to the exclusion of the choice pole. The tradition rejects this: it voids 18:29's
  address and 4:79's location of evil in the self.
- **Qadariyya / Muʿtazila** — the human *creates* his own acts (khalq al-afʿāl by the
  servant), to protect divine justice and accountability. Leans on the choice pole (18:29,
  13:11, 4:79) and reads 37:96 as *mawṣūla*. The tradition faults this for cutting the act
  loose from divine will (against 81:29, 10:100).
- **Ashʿarī (kasb) and Māturīdī** — the Sunni mainstream, holding both poles: God *creates*
  the act; the human *acquires* it (*kasb*) by a real, God-given faculty of will and is
  justly accountable for the acquisition. Māturīdī grants the human's *juzʾī* (partial)
  will a slightly more robust reality than the Ashʿarī does; the difference between them is
  fine and internal. Both read 81:28–29 as the exact map: clause 1 (human will, real) +
  clause 2 (nested in divine will, not autonomous).

**The book's stance:** it stands with the Sunni mainstream (both poles held) *without
litigating the Ashʿarī/Māturīdī fineness*, and it grounds that stance in the two-clause
verses (81:28–29, 76:29–30) and the two-level readings (hidāyah-taxonomy; khalq/kasb) rather
than in kalam argument. It names the Muʿtazilī and Jabrī positions honestly as the two
edges the mainstream holds between, cites 37:96 as the live grammatical fork, and moves on.
It does **not** claim to resolve qadar. It claims only to place *guidance* correctly within
it — at the tawfīq level, downstream of a real human turning.

## 5. The one-page version (what actually goes in the book, in substance)

1. **Pose it honestly** — the reader's real question, both poles quoted at full strength
   (18:29 vs 10:100; and 81:28–29 as the same-breath holding of both).
2. **The Prophet who guides and does not guide** (42:52 vs 28:56) — and the taxonomy that
   dissolves it (bayān, universal, yours to receive; tawfīq, particular, God's to give).
3. **Name where the book lives** — the whole book is a phenomenology of *tawfīq*; the
   determinate world is what tawfīq reshapes; striving/turning/remembering are the
   bayān-level response that is genuinely yours.
4. **The two-level answer to "why strive?"** — 13:11 + 29:69: you change what is in
   yourself; God's guidance-as-tawfīq answers the change. Never earned, never automatic,
   never withheld from the sincere (a line the book can stand on).
5. **The dignity in the tension** (10:99) — God withholds the compulsion He is capable of,
   because a freely-turning heart is the thing being made. The tension is not a flaw to
   apologize for; it is the room in which a self exists.
6. **Honesty coda** — name the schools in a sentence, cite 37:96's grammatical fork, and
   decline to adjudicate. The book's authority comes from *not* overreaching here.

**Voice note:** this must read as pastoral clarity for the common person, not a kalam
seminar (house simple-language rule). The Fable draft of this page should carry none of §4's
apparatus in the body — it belongs in an endnote. The body carries only tools 1–2 and the
dignity-in-the-tension, in plain language, with the verses doing the work.

## 6. How this interlocks with the rest of the book (bonus the census surfaced)

The A.7 additions (dossier §A.7), tafsir-verified the same session, turn out to share the
qadar page's exact structure — **a divine act keyed to a human cause**:
- **6:25** (the veiling paradigm) — al-Muyassar: *bi-sababi ttibāʿihim ahwāʾahum jaʿalnā
  ʿalā qulūbihim aghṭiya* — "**because** they followed their desires, We placed covers over
  their hearts." God veils (khalq/divine act) *in response to* hawā (kasb/human cause).
- **3:151** (the ruʿb-cast) — God casts terror *bimā ashrakū* — because of their shirk.
- **61:5** (already in §A.6) — *zāghū* → *azāgha llāhu* — they swerved, then God swerved
  their hearts.

All three are the khalq/kasb two-level reading *in operation*: the human cause is prior and
real; the divine act is the ratifying response. This means the qadar page is not a
defensive digression bolted on — **it is the same grammar the machine-chapters already run**,
and every hardening-verse in the book (rust, seal, veil, swerve) can point back to it. The
positive direction is identical: 13:11 self-change → divine response; 29:69 striving →
*la-nahdiyannahum*. One structure, both signs. The qadar page and the two machines are the
same claim about how God and the human interlock, stated at the level of the will.
